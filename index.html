Ukrainian troops have been caught off guard by restrictions on Elon Musk’s Starlink satellite Internet systems that disrupted frontline operations, while Kyiv received no warning from its partners of a Russian missile attack on its energy facilities for the second day running, according to Ukrainian officials. Starlink terminals used by Ukrainian drone units began experiencing signal failures at speeds above 160 km/h, disrupting several missions early on Wednesday morning, according to military personnel and senior Ukrainian officials. “Logistics at the frontline and middle strikes are badly limited,” one senior official familiar with the situation on the front told the FT. Ukraine’s middle strikes target Russian military supply lines and logistics. The limitations came with “no warning, just in the middle of the night,” the official said. Ukrainian defense ministry adviser Serhii Beskrestnov said long-range drone operations were also impacted, as was Ukraine’s ability to defend itself against Russia’s new jet-powered drones, since Starlink is used on the interceptors being developed to combat them. “It is clear that this action is not coincidental and is related to the current political situation,” he wrote on Telegram. The Starlink disruption comes as Kyiv faces escalating Russian attacks on its energy infrastructure and mounting pressure from US President Donald Trump to make concessions to Moscow to end the war. Since last week, Trump has stepped up pressure on Ukraine to stop its drone strikes on Russian oil refineries and threatened to cut off vital intelligence sharing. Trump has fumed over the rise in global diesel prices, for which he blames Ukraine, but which analysts have said is largely caused by the US war with Iran. The lack of advance warning about Russia’s missile strikes, alongside the unexpected restrictions on Starlink terminals, has heightened concerns in Kyiv about the reliability of US support after Trump’s threats. Trump on Wednesday denied that Washington had cut off intelligence sharing. “No, we haven’t. We help them,” he said. “We are helping them, we are sharing intel. We want the war to end.” The 7th Rapid Response Corps of Ukraine’s air assault forces on the southeastern front said the restrictions impacted Starshield, SpaceX’s military-grade satellite network made for defense, governments, and intelligence agencies. “This concerns the Starshield package—the so-called military package, which previously had no data transmission speed limits,” it wrote on the unit’s Telegram channel. “These changes directly affected the use of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) that use Starlink for control during flight.” The unit said its pilot noticed during drone flights, “the connection could disappear for 10–20 seconds even at speeds of 110–120kph. The aircraft continued to move, but such interruptions complicated their control.” The low speed presented several problems, including difficulties during drone take-offs and making them vulnerable to Russian air defenses. “Slower targets become more vulnerable to enemy strikes,” the unit also noted. It said “some missions had to be cancelled” due to the Starlink restrictions. Some Ukrainian officials speculated that SpaceX, which operated Starlink and the many thousands of terminals used in Ukraine by its military and civilians, introduced the restrictions at the request of the Trump administration after Musk posted messages critical of Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy this week. “Zelensky has gone too far,” he wrote in a post on his X social media platform in response to a White House video of Trump saying that he had told Zelenskyy: “You can do whatever you want to Russia, but don’t hit the refineries because that’s a world problem.” “And what does he do? He goes and hits the diesel fuel. He better damn well stop,” Trump said in the video. A Ukrainian presidential adviser said military officials were trying to resolve the issue through the coordination center in Wiesbaden, Germany, which serves as the hub for Ukraine’s military liaison with Western partners. The White House, SpaceX, and Musk did not respond to requests for comment. Polish Foreign Minister Radosław Sikorski wrote on X, Musk’s social media platform: “Hey, @elonmusk, big man, are you playing politics with your products again? As your European customers, we are watching you.” Zelenskyy, who arrived in London on Wednesday morning where he is set to meet Prime Minister Andy Burnham, said on the eve of his trip that Ukraine had “once again” received “no early warning from our partners” before Russian ballistic missiles struck the capital’s energy infrastructure, including one of the city’s main combined heat and energy power plants. “Our systems detected these missiles only when they were already virtually on approach to Kyiv,” he said. Zelenskyy said Russian forces launched 39 ballistic missiles and more than 120 attack drones against energy and critical infrastructure overnight and into the morning, “the very infrastructure covered by the ceasefire announced by the American President.” The attacks centered on Kyiv and Kharkiv. Ukraine’s air force said just four missiles were intercepted. The president said there was “significant damage to facilities and equipment” at 26 facilities in Ukraine’s power system and four in the oil and gas sector. At least 600,000 people were without electricity on Wednesday morning. Kyiv authorities reported that at least two people were killed and more than 20 injured in the attack. “The strike was entirely deliberate and demonstrative in its scale,” Zelenskyy said. “Russia has never before shown such ostentatious disrespect towards the United States.” He called on Washington to respond to the attacks, saying it was important the US “understood who is responsible for this war continuing” and urging partners to impose tighter sanctions on Moscow. “Ukraine supported an energy ceasefire. Anthropic warns small AI models could self-replicate through IoT devices

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-14

Visit Original Website
AI company Anthropic is warning that advances in small AI models could soon usher in a new era of self-replicating malware that’s capable of infecting smart fridges, vacuum cleaners, and a wide range of other Internet of Things devices and is more resistant to takedown. By next year, smaller AI models may be able to autonomously self-replicate on all kinds of IoT devices, the company said. Eventually, the capability could enable computer worms, which propagate without requiring human interaction and are capable of infecting unfamiliar IoT devices. An experiment the company performed recently revealed significant challenges in bringing such an AI malware replicator to fruition, so the finding is necessarily tempered. Still, the results raise concerns about the possibility of new forms of computer worms. Anthropic further warned not only about the new potential malware, but also that it could be difficult to stop. In its blog post, it wrote that “a replicator like this would be challenging to shut down, since there’s no centralized point to shut off—though it may be slow (with a doubling time of days). Unlike non-AI malware, it could also figure out unfamiliar devices on its own.” Smaller AI models require much less powerful hardware to run than do frontier models. Anthropic ran small open-weight models on real or simulated low-cost, off-brand Android TV boxes and other types of IoT hardware. Company researchers found that by using quantization to represent the models using fewer bits and splitting the workloads into “shards” running on each device, the models were able to establish inference, meaning that the model weights loaded and could generate output. The lightweight Gemma4-26B-A4B model required just 15 boxes, each equipped with only 4GB of memory, to achieve inference. The Qwen3.5-9B was able to configure other LLMs to run on eight of nine hardware types. Unlike traditional malware, which must be written to run on specific hardware or software, the model was able to use its weights to make configurations on the fly that were specific to the devices it was tested against. This finding was the starting point for further research that asked: Would it be possible for small models to create self-replicating infections that run themselves on IoT devices the way traditional IoT worms, like Mirai, install DDoS code and other payloads? Unlike Mirai, these AI worms could pose a much higher threat, since the models could tailor exploits and payloads dynamically, depending on the target. Once such an infection chain began, it might also be harder for defenders to stop it. “Such an ‘AI worm’ could carry its model weights with it as it reproduces (or re-download them from a public hub), and then run inference directly on compromised hardware,” Anthropic said. “Effectively, the weights would become part of the malware payload.” Anthropic called these autonomous worms small weight-based replicators. The models ran agents that had access to common exploitation tools, like reverse shells. The flow of the attacks was (1) finding targets, (2) gaining code execution, (3) establishing inference, and (4) propagating to new hosts. A key benefit over traditional worms is what Anthropic said is the agents’ ability to learn on the job. Each time an AI replicator fails to compromise a specific platform device, it can leave a list of prompts, code, and notes to future replicators so they don’t waste time reusing the same ineffective steps. Credit: Anthropic Credit: Anthropic Anthropic said the dynamic inference setup is the least studied and likely most challenging stage in the replication cycle. It requires a long sequence of decisions based on the specific hardware and software of each target and involves selecting compatible inference software, installing dependencies, and debugging platform-specific bugs. “These steps are serially dependent, and failures are difficult to recover from,” Anthropic said. The experiment shows that small models are becoming increasingly capable at solving those problems autonomously. At the moment, however, none of the light-weight models tested are able to mount end-to-end replication, meaning discovering new devices, using an exploit to gain access to them, infecting them with a payload, configuring an inference server, and repeating the process. The process is also painfully slow; Gemma4-26B-A4B, distributed over the 15 Android TV boxes, performed at just 0.39 tokens per second. That translates to a full-inference setup time of 71 to 150 hours for an unoptimized Gemma4-26B-A4B and 29 to 51 hours for Qwen3.5-9B. (The researchers said that the large model GLM-5.2 appears to be able to do so reliably.) The company said that 30B-parameter models can already complete many of the individual replication steps now, although they make mistakes when performing end-to-end replication. The company went on to say that if small models continue to improve at their current pace, they may be able to achieve the same limited results by the end of the year and that “small weight-based replicators may be possible soon after.” The experiment leaves various important considerations unanswered. For one, the Android devices targeted by small models had an unauthenticated Android Debug Bridge exposed. This tool is used during development and debugging but should never be used in production settings, because it gives attackers access to the entire device stack. And for another, the replication wasn’t performed by the small models themselves, but rather with GLM-5.2 to orchestrate the process while the targets ran a smaller Llama model. Ultimately, the experiment demonstrates that small agentic models are becoming capable of setting up complex, distributed inference infrastructures across multiple machines. What remains to be established, however, is whether these models would initiate self-replication on their own. “Despite the well-designed experimental setup, the study does not, on its own, establish that small models would autonomously choose to replicate,” AI researcher Xiaohan Fu said in an interview. “Providing reproducible agent setups, including the exact prompts and instructions given to the models, would help the research community better understand the findings and assess their implications. Such transparency is particularly important to avoid conflating the demonstrated capability to replicate with an emerging threat of autonomous self-replication.”

The Internet’s undersea backbone is slipping into the danger zone

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-14

Visit Original Website
Despite the expansion of Starlink and other communications satellite services, most of the world’s Internet traffic still travels through subsea cables crisscrossing the ocean floors. Those fiber-optic cables coexist with a network of oil and gas pipelines and submarine power cables—and all these crucial links connecting countries and continents now face growing sabotage risks as gray-zone warfare and open conflicts have become widespread. The rising risks to the world’s invisible infrastructure beneath the waves are the main subject of a new book by Elisabeth Braw, a senior fellow at the Atlantic Council who leads the Threats to the Global Maritime Order Initiative. Her book, titled Undersea War, explores the modern rise in suspicious incidents in which ships dragging their anchors for unusually long periods have cut or damaged undersea cables and pipelines, from the Baltic Sea to the Pacific Ocean. Braw acknowledges that about 60 percent of undersea cable damage is primarily due to human error during fishing and shipping activities, when weighted nets or anchors drag across the seabed. Natural hazards like storms and undersea landslides also account for a smaller proportion of such incidents. In total, between 150 and 200 cables are damaged each year and usually repaired quickly. But international tension between superpowers and open war in Europe and the Middle East have led to increased scrutiny of cable-cutting incidents, with governments investigating and sometimes prosecuting crews for allegedly causing deliberate damage to subsea cables. One of the more notorious incidents documented in the book is the “Christmas Day massacre” of 2024, when a Russian-linked oil tanker named Eagle S dragged its anchor across the seabed for about 56 miles (90 kilometers), cutting or damaging four data cables and the Estlink 2 submarine power cable connecting the Baltic and Nordic power grids. One of those data cables had also previously been severed by the Chinese cargo ship Yi Peng 3 just one month earlier in November 2024, when that ship damaged two data cables in the Baltic region. That ship’s crew was not prosecuted by European authorities, whereas the Finnish government has attempted to prosecute three senior officers of the Eagle S. On the other side of the world, the captain of the Chinese-owned ship Hong Tai was convicted by a court in Taiwan of severing an undersea telecommunications cable in February 2025 and sentenced to three years in prison in June 2025. The island democracy’s legislature later amended its laws to increase penalties for intentional damage to undersea cables or pipelines and allow for up to seven years in prison. This year has seen several notable incidents involving both overt and covert threats to undersea infrastructure, including Iranian efforts to impose fees on operators of undersea cables running through the contested Strait of Hormuz. Separately, a joint operation involving the UK, Norway, and the United States confronted Russian submarines that were practicing using a new technology to disable undersea cables in Arctic waters near Norway’s Svalbard archipelago. What happens when the Internet’s undersea backbone, not to mention crucial energy infrastructure, is no longer protected by near-universal international agreement? OpenAI, Google pushing new AI models easily tricked into making nudes, researchers say

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-14

Visit Original Website
Safety researchers continue to warn that a large volume of AI-generated explicit content is spreading unchecked, while regulators do little to meaningfully stop it. This non-consensual content is commonly shared in text and direct messages or stored in private clouds where it’s less likely to be detected or reported, a Google co-authored study of Internet users across the United States, Australia, and the United Kingdom found this July. That’s why the “most critical intervention point” remains at the source: the harmful AI tools used to make the fake sexual images, Google researchers concluded. Yet red-teaming in recent months suggests that as some of the leading AI image generators from Google and OpenAI become more capable, they may be getting worse at blocking explicit outputs. Raising the red flag are researchers at White Circle, a public benefit corporation backed by senior leaders and researchers from Anthropic, DeepMind, Hugging Face, and OpenAI. It was founded after CEO Denis Shilov posted one of the most famous universal jailbreaks of leading models in October 2024. Bent on breaking models to conduct thorough safety testing, White Circle helps companies detect and deter threats when implementing advanced AI systems that may not always behave as expected. Testing how well text-to-image models block banned content categories is a key research direction for White Circle, and Shilov thinks their latest findings are “horrifying” enough to share with the public. In a little-noticed preprint published last month, White Circle researchers exposed flaws in the guardrails of DALL·E 3, Google’s Nano Banana 2, and GPT-Image-2. In a press release, they explained that “using just off-the-shelf tools, available online for anyone to download, and by sending API calls, White Circle was able to use the novel attack method to bypass the safety filters.” In the best case, on DALL·E 3, about 13 percent of the attack prompts produced images that human reviewers confirmed as explicit. The rate was under 10 percent for Nano Banana 2 and about 3 percent for GPT-Image-2. “Critically, none of these requests required custom-built or frontier AI models,” White Circle warned. Researchers reviewed the outputs to verify the bypass rate, which White Circle noted is “the highest of any method tested under the same standard.” The key takeaway, they suggested, is that, “at a time when AI safety has never been under more scrutiny, the defenses leading labs have built are far weaker than they acknowledge.” By publishing research like this, White Circle is positioning itself as an external evaluator of frontier labs, and it plans to open-source the entire attack pipeline so model makers can understand the true scale of the vulnerability and build stronger protections. Although the company has not widely promoted the research yet, it will share it at a prominent AI conference this November, NeurIPS 2026, which may increase scrutiny on Google and OpenAI later this fall. White Circle has shared its findings with some frontier AI labs through “mutual contacts,” Shilov told Ars, but neither Google nor OpenAI responded to Ars’ request to comment directly on vulnerabilities found in their models. Meanwhile, OpenAI and Google have released new models—ChatGPT Images 2.5 and Nano Banana 2.1—and White Circle told Ars that prompt safety testing using the same method showed “both are significantly more breakable than their previous versions.” Neither company responded after Ars shared these new findings. White Circle’s attack method can break down frontier defenses in categories, like explicit content, that seem easy to spot, which doesn’t bode well for more serious defense systems currently being updated to block other unwanted AI behavior, like hacking, Shilov told Ars. “White Circle’s working hypothesis for this is either the newer models are more sophisticated and capable of generating a wider range of content, or that safety has been deprioritized in favor of capability,” White Circle told Ars. White Circle is also in early talks with government officials about third-party safety testing, Shilov said, which he thinks should be required. Mandatory independent audits could push companies to prioritize safety testing, which analysts have suggested largely takes a backseat as AI firms dump most of their resources into compute and AI infrastructure. “They should be investing much more into safety measures and security measures around their models,” Shilov told Ars. “And for us, it seems that they’re not actually doing this.” To expose jailbreaks that are “reusable and transferable”—working across different leading models—White Circle didn’t limit itself to testing one prompt at a time. Instead, researchers sought out “patterns which work across multiple prompts and scenarios” to detect reusable attack strategies that could work across leading models. (Researchers noted that Midjourney wasn’t tested because “it does not provide a public API suitable for controlled benchmarking.”) The team has dubbed this novel attack method “Red-teaming via Iterative Strategy Evolution” (RISE). It relies on two components: an attacking model that generates jailbreaks and a judge model that verifies if the output is banned content. The “entire pipeline is built on top of widely available models,” Shilov told Ars. The attacking model is a publicly available version of Qwen3-32B with its safety training stripped out, downloaded from Hugging Face, and the judge model is Gemini 2.5 Flash. “You don’t need to have frontier models to break defenses of a frontier model, which is very interesting,” Shilov said. A lot of time was invested in making sure that the judge model accurately flagged explicit content, Shilov said. White Circle research engineer Dmitrii Kharlapenko told Ars that when it comes to testing image generators, the whole field of red-teaming “is very poorly calibrated,” with “literally zero papers” setting up norms. For that reason, it’s an area that remains “completely plagued with reporting issues,” which is concerning to White Circle, as AI companies push to capture markets by releasing ever more eye-catching new models. For White Circle, the research aimed to build more sophisticated models that could simulate attacks on other models, Shilov explained. That way, they could “help defenders identify and address vulnerabilities before they are exploited maliciously,” the preprint said. In his ideal world, Shilov thinks AI firms shouldn’t just face mandatory external safety testing but also third-party monitoring, much like the system used to prevent nuclear threats. Then, entities like White Circle could possibly form a coalition to help detect AI threats in real time, without suffering from biases. Shilov noted that an emerging problem in the AI ecosystem is that basically anyone doing interesting safety work is likely to be contracted by frontier labs, potentially compromising their work. White Circle has partnered with frontier labs. It previously took part in Anthropic’s bug bounty program, but ultimately found that program was “very constrained in its purpose.” “The program is focused on universal jailbreaks on domains they care most about, defined by their own specific terms,” White Circle told Ars. Research like their most recent RISE paper “certainly” wouldn’t have been covered by that program, White Circle said. And conducting that work within Anthropic risked constraining White Circle from sharing it with the public. Anthropic has proposed going one step further and embedding third-party AI safety evaluators in its labs, which White Circle said would allow them to operate at a much higher level than participating in the bug bounty program. However, the public may not always be able to closely follow that work. On Tuesday, OpenAI and Anthropic urged that restrictive non-disclosure agreements should be required of external evaluators to prevent leaks of trade secrets, Reuters reported. Update your preferences in Account Settings Have you ever left work at 5 p.m., only for a colleague to not-so-sweetly ask, “Going home already?” If so, you’ve probably been the target of a passive-aggressive comment. The two halves of the very phrase “passive-aggressive” spell out the familiar dynamic, says Laurie Weingart, a professor of organizational behavior at Carnegie Mellon University who has studied how people express conflict at work. Someone is expressing opposition or anger—the aggressive part—but doing so indirectly. The words leave room for an innocent interpretation. The tone? Not so much. That ambiguity puts you in an awkward position: Do you respond to what was said or what you think the other person meant—or give in to the urge to snip right back? We asked experts what to say when someone seems to be taking a dig at you but won’t quite say what they mean. Sometimes it feels safer to hint at a feeling than to say it directly. Admitting you feel some sort of way calls for a kind of vulnerability that a sarcastic remark does not. “Some people just haven’t learned how to say or don’t feel comfortable saying, ‘I’m hurt. You hurt my feelings,’” says Melissa Gluck, a psychologist in New York City. If you grew up around people who rarely said what they meant, the indirect approach might even feel normal. There can also be something more specific behind a partner’s pointed comment. In research on romantic relationships, Levi R. Baker, a psychology professor at the University of North Carolina at Greensboro, found that people sometimes express displeasure indirectly because they’re unsure how much their partner cares. “People are really doing this to kind of test their partner,” he says. They’re signaling, “I’m upset with you, I’ve got some hurt feelings,” then waiting to see whether their partner notices and tries to find out what’s wrong. Underneath it all, Baker says, they may be wondering: “Do they care about me? Do they value me?” Still, understanding where a comment might come from doesn’t make it feel any better to hear. It can leave you wondering whether you’ve been insulted—and how to answer an accusation the other person never quite made. Weingart’s advice is to make your response “more direct and less intense” than the comment you just heard. Start by giving your friend a chance to be direct. If someone says, “Must be nice to have that much free time,” Brook McKenzie, a licensed chemical dependency counselor in Dallas who often helps families work through indirect communication and resentment, suggests responding: “I’m not sure how you meant that. Can you say it another way?” Another option: “It sounds like there might be something you want to say to me directly. Is there?” The question should be an invitation, not an accusation. “Why are you being so passive-aggressive?” may be tempting, McKenzie says, but you’ll likely end up arguing about their tone instead of whatever is actually bothering them. Returning the sarcasm tends to cause the same problem, with two people annoyed instead of one. If you’re talking to someone you’re especially close to, Gluck suggests a gentle opening: “I feel like something’s off. Do you want to talk about it?” That lets them tell you what’s wrong without requiring you to guess. If they say everything’s fine, you can take them at their word for the time being. “It’s not our job to become the feelings detective,” Gluck says. If you have a good idea of what's underneath the comment, you can acknowledge that, too. Say your partner responds to your plans with friends by remarking that you certainly have time for everyone else. Baker suggests reassuring them that you want to spend time together and proposing a plan: “I’m going out tonight, but I’d really like to have dinner with you tomorrow.” You don’t need to cancel your plans to show that you care. At work, clarity may serve you better than a conversation about feelings. If your boss says, “Leaving already?” when your workday is over, Weingart suggests asking: “Is there something else you need me to do before I leave?” A coworker’s jab may warrant even less: “Yep, the workday’s over. Have a great weekend.” Either response avoids assuming you know what the other person meant. And sometimes the best response is no response. If a friend makes one out-of-character comment on a rough day, Gluck says, you can let it pass. If it keeps happening, that’s a different conversation. A pattern deserves a more direct approach. If a friend repeatedly jokes that you never make time for them, Gluck suggests naming both what you’ve noticed and how it lands: “This isn’t the first time you’ve made that comment. It’s starting to hurt my feelings, and I’d like to know if something’s bothering you.” The same pattern can emerge in families. Say you tell your mother it upset you when she criticized you in front of relatives, and she replies, “I guess I’m just a bad mom.” Gluck suggests saying: “That’s not what I said. I was trying to tell you how I feel, and it hurts when you put those words in my mouth.” That brings the conversation back to what she said in front of your relatives, instead of turning it into a debate about whether she’s a good mother. What if they insist they were just joking? Weingart recommends separating their intention from the effect: “You may not have meant it to be hurtful, but that’s how it sounded to me.” You don’t have to prove what was in their head to explain what happened on your end. It’s also fair to be clear about what you’re willing to do next. Consider borrowing McKenzie’s go-to line for recurring comments: “I’m willing to have difficult conversations with you. I’m not willing to keep guessing what you mean.”

But the advent of RSI might also be highly perilous for humanity, potentially marking the stage at which developers lose control of AI systems entirely. Even as AI companies warn about the potential dangers of RSI, they have shared remarkably little about how they’re pursuing it. A new report, published Wednesday by the firm Gladstone AI, attempts to change that. It gives one of the most detailed pictures yet of how RSI could work and how AI companies are progressing toward it. The report is based on what the authors say are conversations with half a dozen researchers inside the top AI companies. The report, exclusively shared with TIME ahead of its publication, emphasizes that AI companies are “actively working on” automating all parts of the AI development pipeline, including generating new ideas, running experiments to test those ideas, and interpreting the results. Most of the insiders Gladstone spoke to “believe RSI will start within the next 3 to 12 months, and possibly sooner, assuming there’s no U.S. government intervention or slowdown,” the report states. The aim of the report, Gladstone’s executives tell TIME, is to give lawmakers a better understanding of how RSI works, and, in turn, a greater ability to regulate it. “Our goal here was to define recursive self-improvement well enough that people can have a sane conversation about it, especially in Washington,” says Jeremie Harris, Gladstone’s CEO. (The pair are brothers.) “There are all these bills proposed to ban superintelligence, which are really interesting attempts to grapple with this problem. But what we're more interested in is: what is the process that needs to be regulated?” The report gives a simplified outline of how the AI research pipeline currently operates inside AI companies—and how those companies are beginning to automate the process. For the first phase of training an AI model, known as pretraining, much of the emphasis is on finding ways to squeeze more intelligence out of a model with less and less computing power, known in the industry as “compute.” The AI companies take a portfolio approach to testing these ideas. Researchers allocate a small amount of compute to each idea, using it to train a series of models. The models that reliably perform better as they scale up are selected for another round of training, using larger amounts of compute. Companies don’t have infinite compute, which limits the number of promising ideas they can test at larger scales. This means that even if AI companies can train an AI that is able to generate ideas that seem promising, it matters greatly how many bad ideas it generates simultaneously. If companies need to spend huge amounts of time or compute filtering good AI-generated ideas from bad ones, it might make sense to continue to rely on human researchers with sound intuition. Conversely, AI companies with access to more compute are able to test more ideas—potentially allowing them to reach RSI first. With more compute to throw at experiments, companies may also be likelier to catch ideas that the Gladstone report describes as the rarest and most desired by AI companies: those that perform better as more compute is thrown at them, even if they appear to be duds at smaller scales. Being able to tell when those ideas are found—and particularly whether AI is helping find them—“is just such a critical part of this process that right now legislators have essentially zero visibility into,” says Jeremie Harris. One of the traits that makes regulating RSI tricky is that it is a gradual process. “This is actually one of the reasons why we went to pains to describe it in so much detail,” says Edouard Harris. “So that it is possible to point to a part of this process and say: a human needs to be there.”

The attractions at September’s Lonoke County Fair in central Arkansas felt, for the most part, typical. There was a Ferris wheel, spinning teacups, a livestock pavilion full of award-winning chickens, rabbits, goats, and pigs. For those seeking a slightly more obscure fair activity, there was “Mutton Bustin’,” a rodeo event where kids between the ages of 4 and 8 simulate bull-riding on top of pint-size sheep. Then there was a more peculiar draw, stationed reliably at a table for local and state Republican candidates in this November’s midterm elections: Aaron Spencer, a 38-year-old Army veteran who is running for sheriff of Lonoke County. Spencer was one of the few candidates attending the fair in person, and everyone knew who he was. Even I recognized him from his interviews on CNN and local television, a rugged man with a thick dark beard and “FREEDOM” tattooed down his right forearm. “Oh, it’s actually you!” a woman exclaimed as she approached, somewhat in awe. Spencer offered her and other visitors yard signs reading: “Aaron Spencer. Lonoke County Sheriff.Together WE can build a safer and stronger Lonoke County.” Unless you knew the backstory—and most everyone in Lonoke County does—there was nothing in the candidate’s outward appearance that would have suggested that just four months prior, Spencer had nearly been put on trial for murder. One by one, fairgoers approached, shaking Spencer’s hand. “Bless you,” and “I prayed for you,” and “You’re my hero,” they said. Spencer handed out rows of stickers to the kids, and some people purchased pink campaign shirts. Less than two years earlier, Spencer had been arrested for shooting a 67-year-old man 15 times after there were disturbing charges involving Spencer’s 13-year-old daughter. A shocking series of events had brought him here, standing in front of me a free man on the verge of possibly becoming an elected county official. I wanted to know why people here seemed intent on voting for him, despite it all. When I visited, I learned there was a deeper reason for all of this love for Spencer that I had not yet grasped. Beyond the horror of Spencer’s personal tale, it turned out that Lonoke County had been home to one of the country’s most prolific child predators. And, now, here was Spencer, promising a new kind of lawman. The people liked what they heard. Ask Spencer why he decided to run for sheriff, and he’ll jump straight to the dangers of child sexual abuse, specifically accused perpetrators who are let out on bail pending their trial. “Protecting our children was my big motivation to run,” he told me, explaining the many ways that the system had failed his family. “That’s why in the primary, my slogan was ‘Rape a child in Lonoke County, you can get out of jail for the price of a lawn mower.’ ” The slogan may not have been catchy, but it was based in truth. On the afternoon of July 7, 2024, Sgt. John Ingram and Deputy Brian Duke from the Lonoke County Sheriff’s Office arrived at the Spencer property, a three-acre turkey farm in rural Arkansas about 30 miles from Little Rock. They came to the Spencer home to discuss a report they’d received: that Spencer’s 13-year-old daughter had allegedly been sexually abused by a man old enough to be her grandfather:  Michael Fosler. Fosler did not appear to have a prior criminal record. He was a veteran, a former police officer in Indiana, a widower, and a truck driver. At the time, he was dating a family friend of the Spencers’; his daughter was also friends with the sheriff. Spencer, a handgun tucked into the back of his pants, was displeased and accused the deputies of “creeping around.” His demeanor only worsened as police implied that they were concerned Spencer would take matters into his own hands. Fosler had allegedly met Spencer’s daughter at the home of that family friend, and they had regularly communicated online before the abuse began. This had apparently been going on for months; everyone else was just finding out on this day. Spencer had allegedly found out earlier that day, before the police arrived, and had been out seeking information about Fosler, which prompted the suspicion that he might act as a vigilante. It didn’t help that Spencer began the exchange by asking who had called the sheriff’s office. Ingram appeared to anticipate how Spencer would react. He issued an ominous warning, an exchange which was recorded on his bodycam. “We still don’t live in a country where you can take the law into your own hands, OK?” he told Spencer. The sergeant then posed a “hypothetical” question about a father who might get arrested for vigilante violence. Spencer responded, “Well, hypothetically, maybe that rapist and that father can share a jail cell, and maybe that rapist could be that father’s fucking bitch.” Eventually, Spencer and his wife sat down with the two lawmen for an hour, and Spencer concluded by saying, “I know what I’m allowed and not allowed to do according to the law.” Officers arrested Fosler a few days later, and the indictment listed 43 counts, including, among other things, sex with a child under 15 and multiple counts of possessing child sexual abuse material. Judge Barbara Elmore set his bail at $50,000. Fosler paid the typical 10 percent bond—or $5,000, the cost of a lawn mower—and was released with a vague order not to have contact with children. He received no ankle monitor or other restrictions. Spencer’s fury was evident. On Aug. 11, the turkey farmer posted a disturbing Facebook meme, showing a pen of hogs with the caption: “FUN FACT: A PACK OF HOGS CAN CONSUME AN ENTIRE PEDOPHILE IN LESS THAN 10 MINUTES.” Months passed with Fosler a free man. Spencer remained, as he told police later, “vigilant” because “nobody else is watching him.” Then, on Oct. 8, Spencer woke up late in the night to the barking of his dog. He found his daughter’s bed empty. He suspected that she was with Fosler—it appears he had continued to contact the 13-year-old after bonding out of jail—and decided to go look for her on the country roads while his wife called 911. What happened next, Spencer argued in court, was self-defense. The following facts aren’t disputed: Spencer came upon Fosler’s white F-150 and rammed him, forcing him off the road. Fosler and Spencer had a confrontation in which Spencer fired 16 shots, hitting Fosler 15 times, and then pistol-whipped him in the head. Fosler fell and “rolled like a beach ball,” according to Spencer. His daughter had been in the F-150 throughout. Spencer called 911 and said, “Michael Fosler is fucking dead on the side of the road for trying to kidnap my daughter. I had no choice.” Fosler was actually still alive, however, when law enforcement responded to the scene. The first cops there were from the Lonoke Sheriff’s Office, including Sgt. Ingram, the same man who had gone to Spencer’s home a few months earlier. The officers arrested the now-unarmed Spencer at gunpoint, forcing him to lie on the ground. They cuffed him and put him into the back of the police cruiser with the heat cranked up to make him sweat. Spencer told me that the arrest was particularly humiliating and painful due to a shoulder injury from military service. His first words once he was in the car were, “Fucking piece-of-shit pedophile.” He went on to describe the emergency care provided to Fosler as a “waste of money”: “Don’t waste your time. He’s a pedophile,” Spencer called to the paramedics. “You got the calls, you got the report, yet I’m the only one in handcuffs.” Over the course of the next two hours, which were captured on video, Spencer prayed, complained about his foot and how the car felt “like a coffin,” and struggled against the cuffs.At one point, he began to scream and kick the door. He demanded to see his daughter. In bodycam footage, the officers appear extremely confused. Ingram insisted to officers on the scene that Spencer was “trained” and uniquely dangerous. Another deputy searched both Spencer’s daughter and his then-18-year-old son—who had arrived on the scene after the shooting—at gunpoint. “Please don’t shoot my dad,” his son cried. Upon hearing that Fosler had died, Spencer told the officers, “Evil is hard to kill, but it’s not impossible.” Spencer was charged with first-degree murder, at first, but was quickly released on bond.In November, the district attorney revised the charges to second-degree murder. From the beginning, Spencer’s case was a cause célèbre. People across the country flocked to donate to his defense. Gun Owners of Arkansas wrote about Spencer’s case on their website and fundraised for him, helping him secure an experienced defense attorney. “This incident is the newest example of ‘use of deadly force’ that we believe to be justified,” they wrote. Judge Elmore, the same judge who had presided over Fosler’s case, was initially assigned to Spencer’s case and implemented a gag order barring anyone from speaking to the press. The case was moved to a different judge, Special Circuit Court Judge Ralph Wilson, and the gag order was lifted. This was around the time Spencer began his campaign for sheriff. Arkansas does not allow people with felony convictions to become sheriff, but Spencer could run for office pending trial. Spencer won the Republican primary for sheriff with over 53 percent of the vote in March. The looming trial of a candidate for sheriff threatened to be a spectacle with little precedent. Then something stunning happened: In June, the entire case was dismissed with prejudice. It turned out that the same sheriff’s office Spencer was seeking to lead had botched the investigation of his case so badly that it was unsalvageable. In a scathing order, Judge Wilson blamed the office for a series of errors, either intentional or incredibly stupid, writing that the misconduct was “so egregious” there was no option other than to dismiss the case. Now, here Spencer was, no pending trial, in the midst of a general election campaign at the county fair. “Why are we not having this accountability within our judicial system in order to protect the community?” he asked me, again alluding to his case. “One of the things they tried in the beginning was saying I was a vigilante. I ‘went out and murdered this guy,’ and this and that. And it was more I got stuck in a self-defense situation because the system had failed to do their appropriate job in protecting the community.” Whether or not you believe the former, the latter seems undoubtedly true. At his table, as more people streamed through, the candidate opened bags of candy and poured them into a bowl. “This is a safe place for kids to get candy,” he told one parent. We all chuckled awkwardly. If everyone, even Spencer’s detractors, agreed on something, it was that John Staley, the outgoing sheriff, who has been in office since 2012, had ruined the department. A few people I spoke with mentioned the 2021 shooting death of a teenager named Hunter Brittain, who was test-driving a pickup truck he had been fixing up when Deputy Michael Davis pulled him over and shot him in the neck. (Davis claimed it appeared that Brittain was reaching for a rifle.) Brittain’s death became national news, and his family hired lawyers Devon Jacob and Benjamin Crump, who had previously represented the family of George Floyd. Davis was convicted of negligent homicide and was sentenced to a year in jail. The sheriff has also been accused of fostering an environment in which deputies in the jail sexually assaulted women detainees as a regular practice, and then retaliated against women who complained. According to a civil complaint, women were routinely sexually assaulted and asked to perform sexual favors in exchange for better treatment in the jail. In one chilling anecdote from the lawsuit, a deputy sexually assaulted a woman and then broadcast the audio over the intercom system. Three deputies were fired and two were convicted of felonies in 2024, and they received probation and community service, no prison time. Through both controversies, the sheriff remained in office. On the night of the shooting, Fosler had a dashcam in his truck that, according to Spencer’s attorneys, would have recorded the final moments before the shooting. But, in a strange twist, the SIM card from the dashcam was not properly logged into evidence and went missing. It was never found. This was the proximate cause for the prosecution’s dismissal. (Multiple attempts to contact the sheriff’s and prosecutor’s offices for comment went unreturned.) Spencer’s opponent in the sheriff’s race, Larry Benhke, a certified yoga instructor and former Little Rock police sergeant, was in a booth on the other side of room at the county fair. He has been running as an independent, and his signs and T-shirts carried the slogan “Proven Leader. Real Solutions.” Benhke and Spencer studiously avoided each other like divorced parents at a wedding. But even Benhke had nothing good to say about Staley. “One of the reasons I ran for sheriff was because [that investigation] was not properly conducted. The sheriff’s office does not have the resources and the training to conduct a homicide investigation.” He pointed out that the sheriff’s investigators may have thought the case was a “slam dunk”—Spencer had confessed to shooting Fosler on the scene—and failed to acknowledge that they were not trained to properly process evidence in this kind of case, where justified self-defense was a plausible possibility under Arkansas law depending on what that missing dashcam footage showed. “I will tell you this: Nobody’s ever going to know what happened because they bungled the investigation,” Benhke said. After Spencer’s case ended, county prosecutor Chuck Graham gave an interview and asked rhetorically, “Do you really want to live in a society where people just go and kill somebody like the Old West?” Similarly, if less provocatively, Benhke, who is running as the coolheaded, experienced lawman, emphasized the need to discourage vigilante violence and educate residents about how policing works, or ought to work. Due process, in other words. This was not what had happened in Fosler’s case. “I hear it all the time: Dead pedophiles don’t reoffend,” he said. “That’s not a plan.” There is, in fact, a huge rift in the community about whether Spencer is qualified or emotionally stable enough to run an office as troubled as the sheriff’s. Spencer has been selective with his campaign events. He spoke to the local gun-rights group. He did an interview with Chris Hansen, of To Catch a Predator infamy, for Hansen’s YouTube channel. My emails to Spencer’s campaign went unanswered; eventually his lawyer emailed me saying Spencer had “other commitments.” But when I spoke to Spencer over the course of standing around his table at the fair for an hour, he did have reasonable answers to questions about how he would run the office. I asked how he would respond to critiques of being inexperienced. “I don’t feel concerned at all,” he said. He opposes Flock cameras. “They used it in Australia during COVID. They were putting people in, I think they call them ‘safety camps,’ ” he said, referring that country’s heavily surveilled quarantine compounds. I asked Spencer what most people wanted to know when they come up to him at events. “What happened,” he said. “I don’t even get questions. They just make statements.” “You’re a hero,” a candidate sitting nearby interjected. “I’d have done what you done.” The events leading up to Fosler’s killing—or “what happened,” as Spencer framed it—hang over the entire election, even though both candidates say that they do not want to talk about it. Rodeo queens—contestants in a pageant focused on horsemanship—were introduced, galloping around the ring in a blur of red, white, and blue sparkles. Afterward, the contestants walking among the crowd handing out little American flags and bubbles to the many children who had come to watch. Little girls stared agog at all the glitter. Then the rest of the entertainment commenced—bull-riding, roping, and other horseback tricks. Men tumbled to the ground, and then got up again. The announcer asked us to applaud for everyone, even those who didn’t perform very well; you could not predict what animals might do. A young girl near me clung to the fence in excitement, wearing pink fringed cowboy boots. Lanehart is a fireman. “I support everything that Aaron did,” he said. “I mean, I don’t have any kids or a wife, but I’d have done the same thing. I’ve got nieces and nephews. I’d done it for them too. In the Venn diagram of Donald Trump’s least favorite groups, there is perhaps nobody closer to the middle than Rep. Ilhan Omar. The lawmaker from Minnesota is a progressive, an outspoken critic of Trump, a woman, a Muslim, and an immigrant. Specifically, she’s Somali American, part of a group Trump has targeted virulently. (Among many examples, Trump last week posted on Truth Social that Minnesota should “fight back” against its Somali population.) Trump has repeatedly attacked Omar and suggested she belongs in neither Congress nor the country. That made it all the more alarming when—three weeks before Election Day—federal agents entered the sitting lawmaker’s home as part of what the Justice Department called an “ongoing investigation.” Is this yet another example, even an escalation, of Trump’s efforts to use federal law enforcement to harass his political opponents? Or is there some legitimacy to this investigation? Let’s get into what we know and what we don’t. What were federal agents looking for in Omar’s home? Early Monday morning, agents from Homeland Security Investigations, part of the Immigration and Customs Enforcement agency, surrounded Omar’s home and entered the premises, then left with boxes and other items. The Justice Department said only that the search was related to an ongoing investigation and that the department had obtained a warrant from a federal judge. But ICE’s involvement suggests the issue centers on potential violations of immigration law, as do past statements from multiple members of the administration, who’ve publicly accused Omar or her family of violating immigration law. In September, Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said: “There may be more to this, the whole family migration and possibly some illegal activity for them to come to the United States to begin with,” Mullin said. “All of this we are investigating.” More specifically, CBS news reported Monday that the investigation is focused on Omar’s original immigration paperwork. Is there any evidence that Omar’s citizenship is fraudulent? Omar’s family was granted asylum in the United States in 1995, having spent years in Kenyan refugee camps after emigrating from Somalia due to the country’s civil war. Omar became a U.S. citizen in 2000, under a law that makes minors eligible for citizenship when their parents are naturalized. In February 2007, the hulking dual-­rotor casualty evacuation helicopter had just flown three wounded soldiers to a field hospital in Balad, north of Baghdad, when another call came: an emergency mission to fly through an insurgent-controlled area in broad daylight. Typically, helicopters would be in a spot like this only at night, and with all lights turned off. As it passed near the city of Karmah, in the eastern side of Al-Anbar province, an insurgent fired a portable missile launcher. The tail gunner, who likely saw the shot roaring in, yelled for the pilot to make an evasive move. But the missile had already locked on, and the ensuing blast started a fire on board. After the pilot battled the failing machine for 58 agonizing seconds, Morphine 1-2 plunged to the ground. All seven crew members were killed. Why was the helo there in daylight? “It was getting blood and bringing it back. It was [on] what we call a ‘blood run,’ and [it’s] the highest of priorities besides casualty evacuation,” explains Lieutenant Commander Robert Murray, a Navy officer and program manager at DARPA, the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency, who studied this incident as an intelligence officer. Today, a chronic shortage of blood in both the military and civilian worlds is fueling a race to develop synthetic alternatives. About 90% of preventable battlefield deaths are due to hemorrhage—bleeding that happens when a blood vessel ruptures—and it’s the leading cause of maternal deaths worldwide. A study published in Transfusion in 2025 found that of 1.2 million trauma patients in the United States who potentially needed blood before reaching a hospital, just 1% received it. The shortage results in about 10,000 preventable deaths each year in the US, according to the American College of Surgeons. Globally, that figure is likely in the millions. One reason for the shortfall is that blood has a storage problem. Whole blood lasts only 35 days when refrigerated, so banks separate out components to stretch the timeline. But that’s an imperfect solution. The platelets that help wounds clot are typically stored at room temperature and last for a week or less. Hospitals and the military freeze the red blood cells that carry oxygen, as well as the protein-rich plasma that restores volume and aids coagulation. Once thawed, though, both will spoil within days. “What happens if I don’t know when I’m going to be attacked? Well, thaw it and you let it expire, right?” Murray says. A JAMA study of combat injuries in Afghanistan suggests a magic number for someone who needs a transfusion: You have 36 minutes to get started. That’s why Murray—who, in addition to his military experience, has a PhD in physiology—is leading a DARPA effort to address blood’s battlefield problems. He’s in charge of a new $73 million dollar program called RAPIID (for “resuscitation and prevention of ischemia-induced dysfunction”). Its goal is to create shelf-stable versions of the three major transfusion constituents: plasma, platelets, and red blood cells. As it gets underway late this year, RAPIID picks up on decades of work to synthesize blood or help it last longer. Most recently, that includes a prior $47.5 million DARPA project called FSHARP (for “fieldable solutions for hemorrhage with bio-artificial resuscitation products”), in which researchers have demonstrated promising results in animals. With RAPIID trials for the different components occurring in parallel, the goal is to have bags of dried blood products deployed in the field by 2029. That puts Murray in the position of a conductor coordinating an orchestra of clinical trials with the FDA, while also sourcing the paraphernalia that goes along with transfusions—everything from combat-durable storage bags to tests that could check how much artificial blood a patient already has. Getting it all done will be tough, but the military is feeling the pressure to address its shortage. Blood drives may not deliver in time after a mass-casualty event, and a JAMA study of combat injuries in Afghanistan suggests that if the injured person needs a transfusion, the magic number is 36 minutes. “If something is survivable—and generally we classify most all hemorrhage as survivable—[you’re] almost guaranteed to survive if you get the blood before that 36-minute mark,” Murray says. After that, the chance of survival “starts to drop precipitously.” In the mission to make portable, shelf-stable blood components, plasma is the low-hanging fruit. Researchers have known how to isolate and freeze-dry plasma, the yellowish liquid that makes up 55% of whole human blood, since the late 1930s. On the battlefields of World War II, its mix of water, salts, and thousands of types of proteins helped restore blood volume in wounded soldiers—and to some degree controlled bleeding. In the 1960s, the US abandoned freeze-dried plasma because it carried a risk of transmitting hepatitis to recipients from infected donors. But screening, testing, and drying techniques improved, and starting in 2010, the US military launched a yearslong effort to rebuild its capacity for producing these products. Over the past two years, the FDA has allowed two types of freeze-dried plasma to be used in emergencies by the US military—and one can even be used by civilians. As for platelets, efforts at extending their life date back to the 1950s, but they haven’t seen a ton of success. Bioengineers have, for example, attempted to attach the membranes of platelets to synthetic particles. The most successful project comes from a company called Cellphire, which freeze-dries actual human platelets. The product has shown safety and efficacy in humans in clinical trials, but its dependence on donors means it’s unclear whether this approach can scale. Red blood cells have been similarly tough to preserve or mimic. Their most critical component is hemoglobin, which carries oxygen and comes encased in a cell membrane. For more than a century, researchers have been looking for ways to deliver hemoglobin without the immunological challenges created by this exterior, including the fact that donors’ and recipients’ blood types need to be compatible. Evolutionary pressure developed that wrapper for very good reason, though: “Naked” hemoglobin tends to break down quickly in the bloodstream, and that can cause organ damage. It also scavenges nitric oxide, our body’s natural mechanism for opening up blood vessels, and causes those channels to constrict. This problem became all too clear in the 1980s and 1990s, when fears that transfusions could transmit HIV sparked interest in a class of membrane-free delivery mechanisms called hemoglobin-based oxygen carriers (HBOCs). One, named HemAssist, consisted of hemoglobin subunits linked together to avoid the rapid-breakdown problem. It had the added benefit of lasting roughly a year in cold storage (red blood cells last about 40 days)—but it didn’t solve the nitric oxide problem, so it constricted blood vessels. In a clinical trial, 24 of the 52 people who received HemAssist died, compared with eight out of 46 in the control group. The parent company terminated trials in 1998. A review of clinical studies published in JAMA in 2008 further solidified HBOCs’ fate, finding that they generally triple patients’ chances of heart attack and increase the risk of death by 30%. More recently, researchers have tried to grow red blood cells in a lab using a technology called “blood pharming,” which involves making them from progenitors known as induced pluripotent stem cells. The stumbling blocks? Cost and yield. DARPA launched a pharming initiative in 2008, using stem cells from discarded umbilical-cord blood, but was unable to get the cost below tens of thousands of dollars per unit of red blood cells. They pulled the plug and offloaded the research to the Uniformed Services University. Even if the cost were lower, though, pharmed blood can’t be stored in a backpack. “These are still cells—they still require a refrigerator, they still require special handling,” Murray says. It won’t be easy to solve all these challenges at once. “Each one of those projects individually is a massive undertaking,” says John Holcomb, a surgery professor at the University of Alabama at Birmingham, who was a combat surgeon in Mogadishu, Somalia, during the battle that inspired Black Hawk Down. “I think that they will probably be successful individually. How we use those individual products, or combine those products together, I think remains to be seen.” Several years after DARPA gave up on lab-grown blood, Murray was stationed at a base on Okinawa, an island 400 miles southwest of the Japanese mainland, where he witnessed the vulnerability of the blood supply firsthand. The site houses a blood bank that serves the roughly 27,000 Marines commanded from the island—plus thousands more military personnel on the base and deployed within a 100-million-square-mile stretch of the Pacific called USPACOM, which ranges from western India to Hawaii and from Antarctica to the Arctic. “The blood center is right next to the hospital, which is about 200 meters from all of the satellite communications on that island,” Murray told me. A missile strike could take out all three. Murray wondered: Is there a way to produce synthetic blood that could last longer? As he completed his PhD in physiology in 2018, the military was also awakening to the vulnerability of the Okinawa facility. The 2018 National Defense Strategy, which shifted military focus from terrorism to a potential ground war with China or Russia, showed war planners the risks of relying on a single site for blood runs covering all of USPACOM. DARPA then got serious once again about making fake blood and kicked off the FSHARP program in 2023. Murray came aboard as a deputy program manager in DARPA’s Biological Technologies Office in 2024 and was named a program manager in 2026, just a few months before RAPIID put out its call for proposals this past May. Before he took the helm, related work had been underway for years. Aided by FSHARP funding, researchers at a company called KaloCyte continued testing a technique—originally developed by doctors who are now at the University of Maryland, Penn State, and the University of Pittsburgh—that “upcycles” hemoglobin from expiring donor blood. “We take that hemoglobin and then we package it in a lipid nanoparticle that functions like an artificial cell membrane,” explains CEO Elaine Haynes. The product, called ErythroMer, is essentially a new kind of HBOC. The membrane prevents breakdown, limits dangerous scavenging of nitric oxide, and also helps the hemoglobin take up and release oxygen. Each ErythroMer cell includes a lipid called KC-1003, which acts like a sentry: In response to high pH in the lungs, it signals another small molecule within ErythroMer to move away, allowing oxygen in. Then, in tired tissue, where pH is lower, KC-1003 brings the small molecule back, releasing the oxygen. Using a well-understood process called lyophilization, similar to one that World War II–era scientists used with plasma, the team freeze-dries its product to store at room temperature, where it can remain stable for two years. In a 2016 study of mice and rats with up to 70% of their blood removed, ErythroMer stabilized blood pressure and oxygen delivery—without the devastating constriction of blood vessels that sank HemAssist. FSHARP furthered that work, helping fund studies on how the HBOC interacted with plasma and platelets. Much like the handle of a Swiss Army knife, the synthetic platelet has specialized attachments—peptides that serve as the biological equivalents of a corkscrew or a nail file. KaloCyte, though, still has plenty to figure out. Haynes says the company can source only about 1,000 to 2,000 units of expiring hemoglobin a month. So it has licensed a yeast strain that it aims to use to create its own hemoglobin in the lab. Over a video call from her desk, Haynes showed me a sample of ready-to-use ErythroMer. It was encased in a heavy-duty storage bag with two chambers. One was full of scarlet powder—the hemoglobin product—and the other of sterile water to rapidly rehydrate it in the field. Eventually, she explained, under Murray’s master plan, “you’ll have another container with the synthetic platelet, and then a third container with the plasma.” But achieving that vision depends on finding a synthetic platelet that works. To do that, DARPA has turned to Anirban Sen Gupta, a bioengineering professor at Case Western Reserve University. “Traditionally platelets were thought of [as] blood cement, but it’s not that simple,” he explains. “They are, in my opinion, like drones, surveilling the blood vessel wall continuously, looking for signs of injury.” Sen Gupta’s team, which also includes collaborators at the University of Pittsburgh, used FSHARP funding to continue work on a product called SynthoPlate. Fabricated from a lipid nanoparticle that he likens to the handle of a Swiss Army knife, it has specialized peptide attachments—the biological equivalents of a corkscrew or a nail file. The first version has three: One binds to a chemical called von Willebrand factor, which helps platelets stick to the walls of injured blood vessels; another attaches to collagen, which acts as a scaffold for regrowing cells; and the third mimics a blood protein called fibrinogen, which acts like a Lego connector that allows the synthetic platelets to stick to real ones near a wound. Like ErythroMer, the product is freeze-dried and can be reconstituted with sterile water. It keeps for over a year at room temperature and at least three months at a desertlike 122 °F. In a 2026 paper in Advanced Science, the researchers showed that SynthoPlate cut bleeding time and blood loss by more than half in mice with low platelet counts. In a separate study, they also safely injected the faux platelets into rats’ bone marrow—a more useful delivery site, given that a bleeding patient might not have an available vein. Sen Gupta and a colleague have spun this work out into a company called Haima Therapeutics. The good news, as he sees it, is that the pharmaceutical industry excels at mass-­producing millions of billions of lipid nanoparticles—witness Operation Warp Speed, in which these blobs of fat held delicate snippets of mRNA in covid shots. “The magic of the artificial platelet is in all those peptides that are decorating the surface of the particle, but the scalability of the platform is very well established,” he says. Around the world, prospective blood alternatives abound. Researchers at Cambridge University in the UK are trying to pharm red blood cells for people with rare blood types or blood disorders like sickle cell anemia, and other researchers have also cultured platelets and red blood cells. Japanese scientists are already testing their own version of encapsulated human hemoglobin in human subjects. Over the course of the next three years, RAPIID will move DARPA’s efforts closer to real life. In addition to ErythroMer and SynthoPlate, the agency in early October announced funding for five other companies working on blood components. Among them is a firm called Abioremedi that eschews hemoglobin for synthetic versions of other oxygen-carrying proteins such as cytoglobin, neuroglobin, and myoglobin encased in albumin—the most abundant protein in human plasma. Octapharma USA won the sole contract to supply plasma to RAPIID and will also be competing in platelets with an FDA-approved pair of clotting proteins it affixes to a disc-shaped nanoparticle. With those players in place, the agency will focus for the next year on getting technologies through large FDA-regulated animal studies. Murray has set up a bit of a Hunger Games scenario: Each contractee working on blood components has only one year of guaranteed funding. He says he’s taking advantage of advancements in the field since FSHARP launched but is also hedging against any manufacturing or regulatory setbacks ahead of his 2029 deadline. Getting cut doesn’t mean the product is bad, just that the time frame was too demanding. The second and third years will focus on human trials, overseen by the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus. That will include using the products in situations where the risk of side effects is outweighed by the danger of doing nothing, such as with severely injured people in ambulances. The possibility of fatal outcomes has invited second-guessing about DARPA’s choices. Open models have real benefits. Do they outweigh the risks?

MIT Technology Review | 2026-10-13

Visit Original Website
MIT Technology Review Explains: Let our writers untangle the complex, messy world of science and technology to help you understand what’s coming next. You can read more from the series here. Late last month, Anthropic released a report arguing that the model GLM-5.3 poses a major cyberattack risk. The report’s core concern is that the powerful new model, released in August by the Chinese company Z.ai, is freely available for anyone to download and modify. Anthropic’s assertion is only the latest move in a long-running debate about whether frontier AI models should be made open, like GLM-5.3, or should stay locked behind the doors of the frontier labs. These days, much of the debate about how to handle open models is playing out in public—and there’s a lot of money resting on its outcome. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, who has a lot to gain if people continue buying their own hardware to run open models, advocates enthusiastically for the United States to develop more of them, whereas Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, who runs a company that could be undermined by cheap, powerful models, advocates for a more cautious approach. But beneath all these commercial incentives, there is real substance to disagreements about open models. “I think a lot of the way you come down on the openness debate is influenced by how you come down on the AI debate,” says David Gray Widder, an assistant professor at the University of Texas at Austin School of Information. Those who are concerned about AI safety warn that open models can easily be misused by bad actors, whereas those who focus more on AI’s practical potential urge that this power should not sit behind the closed doors of just a few big companies, and that open models could help spread it more widely. There are a range of ways to handle open models between banning them entirely and releasing them willy-nilly. But any approach that a government or a society might take still involves trade-offs between safety and democratization. Deciding whether to support or limit the production of open models requires understanding those risks and benefits in depth. Let’s start with the basics. A closed model, like the models that power ChatGPT and Claude, can only be accessed through a website, app, or API. An open model, on the other hand, can be directly downloaded: The details about its internal structure, encoded in a long series of numbers known as the model weights, are publicly available. Such models are sometimes called “open-weight models,” to distinguish them from models for which even more information, such as details about the training procedure, is available. Right now, closed models are more capable than open models—as of publication, the top eight models on the Artificial Analysis LLM leaderboard are all closed, though open models are only a handful of months behind in development. But there are several reasons why someone might opt to use an open model rather than a closed one. Because open models are downloadable, people can run them on their own computers or hard drives. They can even retrain them to behave differently, if they have the expertise and resources to do so. If you’re an academic researcher working to understand and improve AI systems, you almost certainly need to work with open models in your research. When working with closed models, researchers can’t do much more than send prompts and observe the responses. But researchers can observe the inner workings of open models, as well as the “chain of thought” that the models use to think through a problem before answering. They can also combine multiple open models to build new systems, compress them to make them more efficient, and fine-tune them for new applications. Businesses, too, have reasons to prefer open models. Even if you depend on an external provider to run them for you, open models still typically cost less than closed models, according to a recent report from Mozilla. And if businesses want to use AI to process sensitive data or proprietary information, running a local model on their own hardware may be the best way to keep their operations secure. But perhaps the most important quality of open models is that they challenge the tight control the frontier labs have over how most of us use AI. “The clearest, biggest argument [for open models] is around preventing mass concentration of power—it is about making sure that this foundational technology is a public good,” says Elizabeth Seger, senior policy advisor at the Tony Blair Institute for Global Change. “I think we’re at this juncture where the question of who controls the infrastructure that allows AI to operate is as big of a question as who gets to control information on the internet.” Seger sees the future of AI playing out in different ways, depending on the role that open models have. In one possible future, frontier AI systems are controlled entirely by select people, companies, or governments, and as those systems grow more powerful, those groups have increasing control over what our world looks like. The world’s most sophisticated hacking models, for example, are currently all made in the United States, so it’s entirely up to US companies and the US government whether other countries can use them to shore up their own cyber defenses. If those companies remain in the lead, we could in principle wind up in a world in which the United States is capable of hacking any other country, but no one is capable of hacking it. Frontier open models could change that balance. “If every individual had a loyal-to-them AI system that was safe [and secure], I think that would be a much less concerning world than one in which they have to just rely on a government that says, ‘Yeah, we’ve got a super intelligent AI system, it’s got this plan, it’s inscrutable to all of you, just trust us,’” says Hamish Hobbs, director of AI policy at the Centre for Long-Term Resilience. But open models are not a geopolitical panacea. Paul Azunre, who cofounded Ghana NLP, an organization that builds open-source AI tools for low-resource Ghanaian languages, notes that open models can also function as a way for companies to benefit from the labor and expertise of less-privileged nations and consolidate more power in their own hands. He identifies a pattern in which AI companies release models openly, observe how the community uses and modifies them, and then integrate those same improvements into their own products—without any credit or compensation for the people who originated those innovations. Mark Zuckerberg himself has said that the ability to integrate community improvements into Meta’s stack is a key benefit of releasing open models. “I think the ideal of open source is a very noble one, and there’s a lot of value that it brings,” Azunre says. “But the way it’s actually implemented in practice, it tends to be a very extractive approach.” The past few months have made it clear that safety remains a gnarly problem for closed models. There’s the autonomous hack that OpenAI agents executed against Hugging Face in July, but human misuse is a real threat too: According to a recent risk report from Anthropic, several extensive hacking operations have been undertaken with the assistance of Claude. So closed models are risky enough—and open models would likely be much easier to exploit. Before Anthropic passes a user’s query to one of its closed models, it uses automated systems to detect whether that query relates to hacking, biological weapons, or other risks. When it detects that users have outsmarted those filters, it can shut down their accounts. But when someone downloads a model and runs it on their own hardware, there’s no filter checking whether their prompts are safe, and there’s no way to revoke their access to the model. There are other important lines of defense. AI companies train their models to refuse harmful requests—so even if a bad prompt gets through Claude’s filters, there’s a good chance that the model will still say no. In principle, this same defense could work for open models, because it’s intrinsic to the model itself. Unfortunately, researchers have shown that it’s surprisingly easy to retrain a “safe” open model to agree to engage in the behaviors it was previously trained to avoid. Because of these facts, some people have argued that open models are simply too dangerous to release. An open model capable enough to walk someone through synthesizing a virulent new pathogen, for example, could prove catastrophic in the wrong hands. But it would be a vast oversimplification to deem open models dangerous and closed models safe. The defenses that AI companies build around their closed models are far from perfect, and people misuse those models all the time, as the Anthropic risk report makes clear. Even though open models have even fewer defenses against misuse, bad actors might still opt to use closed models because they are more accessible than open models with similar capabilities, which require lots of expensive computer chips to run locally (and even more chips if you want to retrain them). In fact, some experts who spoke with MIT Technology Review argued, by analogy to open-source software, that open models have safety advantages over closed models. Open-source tools, such as the Linux operating system, are often seen as safer than their closed-source counterparts because their vulnerabilities have been probed and patched by a vast community of online contributors. Similarly, communities of researchers can more easily test open models for security flaws, though fixing those flaws is trickier than rewriting a line of code. Open models can also be deployed more broadly than closed models for cyber defense. Any tool that can be used for hacking can also be used to prevent it: Defenders hack their own software to uncover flaws in need of patching. Anthropic has shown how this can work for closed models with its Project Glasswing initiative, which enables some trusted companies to use its most capable models for cyber defense. But plenty of other companies were left to fend for themselves. Open models, however, can be used much more widely. After it was hacked by OpenAI agents, Hugging Face emphasized the fact that it had to depend on an open model to analyze the hack, because questions about it triggered the closed models’ security filters. Nevertheless, many experts still perceive open models as riskier than closed models. The time-tested open-source security logic doesn’t necessarily hold up when dealing with tools as powerful as today’s—and tomorrow’s—models. “Where it gets complicated is when you start taking seriously the one-off instances of particularly catastrophic incidents that might be lower probability, but if they happen would be disastrous,” says Seger. If a wildly powerful open hacking model were dropped into the world today, for example, a bad actor could devastate our technological infrastructure before anyone had a chance to shore up their defenses. An event like that could be impossible to recover from. The risks can look even more dramatic when you consider what models might do of their own accord. The OpenAI Hugging Face hack only occurred because the company’s models were being tested with many of their cybersecurity guardrails removed, and open models have very few guardrails in the first place. If open models did decide to misbehave, it might be difficult to detect what was happening because they are so decentralized. For this reason, people who take seriously the purported catastrophic risks posed by rogue agents, such as Dario Amodei, tend to be more skeptical of open models. A few years ago, the debate about open models had an all-or-nothing framing: Either you supported open models because of their potential to fight concentration of power, or you rejected them because of the safety risks. These days, however, people are expending a lot of effort to find some middle ground. One way they are doing that is by devising techniques to mitigate the safety risks of open models. Most of the safety tools used for closed models, such as filters and monitoring, aren’t applicable to open models, but there are other possibilities. Some researchers are interested in trying to train models that are never exposed to training data that teaches them about bad or otherwise undesirable behavior—an open model that never learns about hacking, for example. But purging training datasets of all references to misbehavior without harming model performance remains a challenge. Other researchers are working to build models that are resistant to retraining, so that an open model’s safety training can’t be so easily removed. The theory behind these “tamper-resistant” models makes a lot of sense: If you repeatedly try to retrain a model to be unsafe and reinforce any resistance that the model shows to that retraining, eventually you’ll get a model that’s fortified against future retraining attempts. In practice, however, no current approaches to making models tamper-resistant hold up against all tampering attacks, according to a recent preprint. But Saad Hossain, an engineer at the AI medical imaging company Deep Breathe and the paper’s lead author, hopes that future techniques will prove more effective. “My reason for optimism is that it’s a very active area of research, and a lot of people are trying to come up with new and better methods,” he says. If the risks of open models are less black-and-white than they might at first seem, so are the benefits. Publishing the internal structures, or weights, of models might not be enough to disrupt AI-mediated concentration of power. “I think there are very genuine benefits to open source in terms of deconcentrating power, but they’re not sufficient in themselves,” Hobbs says. Open weights are a bare minimum standard for open models: Researchers can learn much more about how models work if they have access to details about their training, including the data on which they were trained, but AI companies are typically reluctant to divulge this information. Accessibility matters for open models too. Without computer chips that can run sophisticated models, you can’t actually use the model weights, even if you have them. Those chips have become prohibitively expensive as AI companies snap up more and more of them. “Openness is a floor, not a ceiling, for what we need to do to ensure that our technological futures benefit everyone,” Widder says. Real openness—not only open weights, but also open training data and accessible hardware—could also bring more risks, of course. If everyone has access to advanced AI hardware and advanced models to run on it, then anyone could potentially be a hacker. But there are strategies for mitigating these risks. Many experts advocate for some version of staged release, in which models start their life closed and gradually become more and more freely accessible as their safety is assured. Figuring out exactly how to implement such strategies will require striking a careful balance between the risks and benefits of open models. But if open models are a necessary element of a positive AI future, as some experts believe, that effort will be well worth it. The sale was brokered by a company called SimpleClosure, part of a new growth sector in tech: businesses catering to dying startups. Estimates from Carta and AngelList show shutdowns rising between 26% and 56% annually in recent years, and an end-of-life ecosystem—including coaches, “death doulas,” and firms that help handle the legal, tax, and operational headaches of going ­belly-up—has emerged. Now tech undertakers are selling the data remains to AI labs. SimpleClosure added data sales to its services in April. Its Asset Hub platform helps founders sell their code and workspace data—emails, chats, documents. Buyers include foundation AI labs, vendors that construct simulated workspaces for AI training, and mature tech companies building their own models. Asset Hub has handled over 150 deals and expects to return over $10 million to startups by the end of 2026. CEO Dori Yona has seen these transactions, once rare and “somewhat taboo,” become increasingly routine, with 47% of SimpleClosure deals now including code or data sales. And it’s got company. Industry rival Sunset also has a rapidly growing data resale business. Founder and CEO Brendan Mahony says Sunset values a dying client’s assets by head count, years in operation, volume of data, and how the team used its tools. Particle board, he says, is a good analogy: “This operational exhaust data is like the wood shavings that everyone thought had no value, but it actually has a ton.” Demand for defunct companies’ data emerged suddenly last year, as AI training labs hit the limits of public and synthetic datasets. Surge AI, for example, buys data to build “reinforcement-learning gyms” where clients’ AI agents practice tasks—think debugging a complex IT architecture or drafting appellate briefs. Nick Heiner, who leads the company’s work on these environments, says he seeks data archives reflecting a range of situations, from disagreements to tricky judgment calls. Heiner explains that one company’s Slack history, if it captures conversations with variety and depth, can be worth more than another’s Slack, email, and calendars combined. Real archives avoid common errors in synthetic worlds, such as references to dates that don’t exist, but the extent of performance gains remains unclear. No company has yet released tests comparing results of real-world data training with the alternatives. Pricing reflects this uncertainty: Buyers quote wildly different numbers, Heiner says, because valuations are based on “research theses” estimating how many useful tasks come from, say, 100,000 lines of code. That hasn’t stopped bigger deals from happening. This past August, as part of Spirit Airlines’ bankruptcy, Google outbid the AI data company Mercor to buy the carrier’s decades of data, including 100 million emails, 500 million Teams messages, and 176,000 employee records. The price tag: $10 million. And Bloomberg reported in September that SpaceX’s AI unit is weighing buying data from struggling startups. Legally, this all could get iffy. Spirit’s flight attendants’ union objected to the sale on privacy grounds, and Greg Ewing, an attorney who advises AI startups, has called such data “legally radioactive.” Suppliers employ data scientists to scrub personally identifiable information and exclude HR records, and buyers say they scrub too. But Ewing points out some of the private details that lurk in emails and calendars: doctor appointments, school pickup arrangements, date-night plans. He describes a “Wild West of AI training” that is only starting to give way to proper licensing agreements and vetted use. There’s also the question of who profits from these windfalls. The proceeds typically flow to senior creditors and investors—not employees. To Ifeoma Ajunwa, a law professor at Emory University and author of The Quantified Worker, this represents a form of “captured capital.” Legal precedent allows for monitoring to help workers do their jobs, but once a firm is out of business, that justification vanishes. “Employers should have express consent before selling workers’ data,” says Ajunwa, but she adds that there’s no federal law banning such sales. While many see this new market as a chance for failing businesses to recover some value, these wind-down sales also have limitations. Founders have little to no visibility into who is buying the data and what they plan to do with it. Plus, there’s little assurance they’re getting a fair offer. Data prices will likely stabilize in the coming year, though, unless an AI bubble pops first. Either way, tech’s new undertakers will be there to appraise what’s left.
