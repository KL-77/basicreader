It’s unclear whether the pair chose to resign or were forced out, or what will happen to their planned slate of projects beyond 2028’s Superman: Man of Tomorrow, which is in post-production. Producer Jeremy Latcham—a protege of Marvel’s Kevin Feige who worked with Gunn on Guardians of the Galaxy among other MCU films—will replace them. Per Gunn and Safran’s joint statement: Four years ago, we set out to unite DC under one creative vision. In that time, we helped restore Superman to the heart of DC, while opening new doors and telling new stories in film and television with Peacemaker, Creature Commandos, Lanterns and Clayface. We’ve shared a creative history with Jeremy going back to Marvel, and we’re eager to see the perspective he brings to DC’s next chapter. We’re excited to return to what drew us to this job in the first place, which is telling the stories we love, including the next chapter of Clark and Lex’s journey in Man of Tomorrow. DCU fans are mourning the news and what it could mean for the franchise. My feelings are mixed. On the one hand, I’m on record that I’ve found the rebooted DC Universe films released thus far to be enjoyable but rather forgettable, even when the positives outweigh the negatives. (I have yet to see Clayface, which debuted to good reviews and a solid box office showing.) But HBO has done right by the DCU: I loved both seasons of Peacemaker; Lanterns is a shining beacon of what is possible when someone is allowed to get really creative within the genre; and I’m very much looking forward to next year’s Jimmy Olsen (Skylar Gisondo) series The People v. Discovery CEO David Zaslav—unusual for the industry—Latcham will report to Dana Goldberg and Josh Greenstein, co-chairs of the Skydance Motion Picture Group. “Jeremy brings a deep reverence for DC and its passionate fanbase, recognizing that they are the very foundation of this storied, multigenerational brand,” Goldberg and Greenstein said in a statement. “Under his leadership, every film and television show will be ushered from start to finish through the prism of the legions of fans who love and champion it. I can’t wait to work night and day alongside filmmakers, comic book creators, artists and storytellers of every kind, across film, television and animation, to build something ambitious for longtime DC fans and newcomers alike. Man who lost $308,000 on sports bets sues Kalshi for offering illegal gambling

A man who lost over $308,000 making sports wagers on Kalshi is suing the prediction market for violating the state of Washington’s ban on online gambling. He’s trying to turn those losses into an overall victory with a lawsuit seeking a payment of over $580,000. Bellevue, Washington, resident Scott Bright’s “wagering occurred at a time when it was unlawful in Washington and when the Washington Attorney General had already sued KalshiEX LLC in this Court alleging precisely that,” said the gambler’s lawsuit filed on September 24 in King County Superior Court. Kalshi has since filed a motion to dismiss the lawsuit. While state attorneys general throughout the US are trying to shut down Kalshi for allegedly violating state gambling laws, Bright’s case tests whether unsuccessful gamblers can obtain restitution from the company through individual lawsuits. “Washington has prohibited Internet gambling since 2006. Washington has also provided, since long before that, a statutory cause of action permitting any person who loses money at illegal gambling to recover it from the proprietor for whose benefit the game was conducted,” Bright’s lawsuit said. Even if Bright’s lawsuit fails, gamblers who lost on Kalshi could get money back through the lawsuits filed by state governments. For example, Washington’s lawsuit against the company said the attorney general “is entitled to recover all money Washingtonians have lost through Defendant Kalshi’s illegal gambling operation” under the state’s Recovery of Money Lost at Gambling Act. Washington already won a court order restricting the kinds of wagers Kalshi can offer in the state, but it took effect after Bright’s losses. Kalshi wants Bright’s lawsuit to be moved to US District Court for the Western District of Washington, saying it must be tried in a federal court instead of a state one. In an October 30 motion to dismiss Bright’s case, Kalshi argued that he did not lose any money on “gambling.” Kalshi said he lost money on “event contracts,” which Kalshi says can only be regulated by the federal government. “The contracts which Plaintiff mischaracterizes as gambling are event contracts offered on the Platform subject to the CEA [Commodity Exchange Act], in conformance with the rules and regulations of the CFTC [Commodity Futures Trading Commission], the federal agency which has statutory authority to oversee the CEA,” Kalshi said. We contacted Bright’s attorney, Michael Daudt, about Kalshi’s court filings and will update this article if we get a response. “Plaintiff alleges that his activity on the platform was gambling and that he ‘lost,’” Kalshi wrote. “He does not allege that anyone misled him about how the platform worked, what it charged, or the risk that any position might lose. Yet, he now sues KalshiEX and its parent, Kalshi Inc., to recover nearly double his own alleged net realized loss under Washington’s gambling loss-recovery statute, and a theory of unjust enrichment.” Between June 17 and August 14, 2026, Bright transferred $315,353.94 to Kalshi in 253 separate debits from his checking account, his lawsuit said. Kalshi allegedly collected $5,589.05 in funding fees on those transfers. Bright made numerous wagers on sporting events including professional baseball games and tennis matches. He apparently put any winnings into new bets, and alleges that “Kalshi’s platform cycled Plaintiff’s deposits through $14,396,457.30 in transactions and assessed and retained $84,062.71 in trading fees on them, equal to 26.7 percent of everything Plaintiff deposited.” After all his bets, Bright suffered a net loss of $308,781.62, and his account was left with a balance of $983.27. Kalshi said that Bright’s “alleged losses went to counterparties rather than” to Kalshi itself and that Bright’s “positions were matched with counterparties and resolved according to the outcomes of the underlying events.” “The Complaint does not allege that KalshiEX or Kalshi Inc. was the counterparty to any of Plaintiff’s positions,” Kalshi said. Kalshi calls itself “a neutral exchange platform” with no financial stake in the outcome of any event. It makes revenue by collecting fees on transactions. But while Kalshi describes itself as a neutral operator and collector of transaction fees, its business model also includes a trading affiliate that places wagers on the platform. Kalshi’s rulebook says its trading affiliate “participates on the Platform and provides liquidity to the Platform by placing orders on either (or both) sides of a market at competitive price levels.” Kalshi says its affiliate only has access to the same public information that other traders on the platform have. Bright’s lawsuit alleges that Kalshi’s affiliate “took the opposite side of a substantial portion of Plaintiff’s positions,” meaning that “Kalshi and the enterprise under common ownership with it were not a neutral venue but the dealer or player winning within the meaning of [state law], in addition to being the proprietor for whose benefit the wagering was conducted.” Bright doesn’t know for sure whether Kalshi’s affiliate was on the other side of any of his bets. “The identity of the participant on the opposite side of each of Plaintiff’s positions, and the profit each such participant realized, is information exclusively within Kalshi’s possession,” Bright’s lawsuit said. The US Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit ruled in August that Nevada can regulate Kalshi under gambling laws. Although that case involved Nevada, the Ninth Circuit’s jurisdiction includes the state of Washington. Bright’s lawsuit quoted the ruling’s finding that “placing sports bets, even when called by another name, is still gambling.” Although Bright’s lawsuit said his net loss was $308,781.62, he asked for $580,544.98 based on an “event-by-event” calculation derived from a 1950 ruling issued by the Washington Supreme Court. “Computed on the event-by-event basis required by Heitfeld, under which winnings in separate and distinct events may not be offset against losses, the sums Plaintiff lost total $580,544.98,” the lawsuit said. Bright’s lawsuit said that if the court declines to order $580,544.98 in restitution, it should instead award “all funds Plaintiff transferred to Kalshi and the $89,651.76 in funding and trading fees Kalshi collected.” That would amount to over $405,000. In addition to its motion to dismiss the case, Kalshi filed a motion to stay the proceedings at least until the US Supreme Court decides whether to take up a separate case on whether state gambling laws apply to prediction markets. “The orderly course of justice favors a stay because Supreme Court review will settle or simplify every claim and ensure uniform treatment of like suits,” Kalshi told the US district court. Kalshi has suffered some notable losses in US appeals courts, which found that states can regulate the platform’s event contracts under gambling laws. But federal courts have not unanimously sided with states, and New Jersey asked the Supreme Court to settle the question of whether states can regulate Kalshi under gambling laws. Kalshi’s motion for a stay in the Bright case noted that Washington government officials are “pursuing overlapping claims” against Kalshi. “The State of Washington is pursuing the same theory, and seeks the same recovery for Mr. Bright, in a case pending before the Superior Court of Washington for King County,” Kalshi wrote. Kalshi tried to get the state’s case moved to a federal court, but it was sent back to King County Superior Court in May. Washington won a preliminary injunction against Kalshi in August, when a King County judge ordered Kalshi to implement geofencing and stop allowing many types of wagers in the state. The county court’s order required Kalshi to stop offering or accepting event contracts “related to sports, elections, politics, entertainment, culture, tech and science, or mentions” in the state. The injunction did not affect wagers in the commodities, climate, economics, and finance categories. Bright transferred his funds to Kalshi before the geofencing order took effect. His lawsuit claims that on September 15, he tried to access the app but received a notice that “using this app in your current location is prohibited.” This article was updated to correct the date the lawsuit was filed.

A Sam Altman parody offers an amusing lesson in memory management

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-09

Visit Original Website
Sam Altman’s recent comments on the Cheeky Pint podcast inspired developer Iain Kay to create a satirical script that allows users to email the OpenAI CEO once per day if more than 10 percent of their system RAM is reported as available. Ram-Altman is described by its creator as a “parody project” launched in response to Altman’s viral remarks about RAM in your personal PC. As reported by The Verge, on the October 29 episode recorded with Stripe President John Collison, Altman said, “A lot of people want local inference. And somehow, they assume that the problem is solved now because we are going to have open source models, we’re going to be able to run them on our computers, and so we’re not going to need all the stuff in the data center. But from a global allocation perspective, actually, it’s quite terrible to have everybody have a bunch of the world’s limited supply of DRAM sitting in their own computer and being used 5 percent of the time.” Altman has since explained that his comments were taken out of context and briefly changed his display name to “RAM Altman” on X, which Kay calls on the project GitHub, “objectively excellent and where the project gets its name.” In an email to Ars, Kay said, “Ram-Altman is a bash script that takes MemTotal minus MemAvailable from /proc/meminfo, so the figure includes page cache and reclaimable slab but leaves out tmpfs and shared memory.” If the script only showed what Linux reports as “free” RAM, it would paint an incomplete picture. Linux often reports a surprisingly small amount of “free” RAM, as it doesn’t count memory that is currently claimable for use. Linux will fill available RAM caches with file data for quick access, but the kernel can reclaim that usage at a moment’s notice when needed. The MemAvailable figure is the kernel’s own estimate of how much memory it could hand over without swapping. Users can schedule the script to ask the kernel for a status update every five minutes, and if more than 10 percent of RAM is available, it triggers a joke email from one of several joke templates. The script limits outgoing emails to one per day. The GitHub page for the RAM-Altman project includes a dry-run option that any Linux user with a Bash terminal should be able to paste and run without the need for email trolling. When the threshold is exceeded, each dry run output features one generated joke email, so repeated runs produce different amusing examples. A test run of the script on my Fedora 44 KDE machine confirmed the author’s measurements. Inside the System – Info Center of my Fedora desktop, the system reports 14.9 GiB of total usable system RAM after the kernel and firmware take their needed slices of the 16 GiB of physical memory. This reflects the total amount of capacity the system has to work with, not the amount available at any given time. The Ram-Altman script shows that of that 14.9 GiB total pool, 10 GiB is used, with 4.9 GiB available (33.1 percent). That figure isn’t the simple sum of the “free” and “cache” lines that the script displays, but a kernel-calculated figure that accounts for the full amount of memory that can be reclaimed. The project concedes the nuance that Altman was referring to how much time memory spends being utilized, while the script measures how much capacity is free at the time of measurement. While the script might tell Altman that 33 percent of your system RAM is available, it doesn’t outline how often that’s the case. The concession steps on the joke slightly, but it keeps everyone honest. The GitHub is wise to point out that “--dry-run is the best way to enjoy this. It costs nothing and all the jokes are there.” Used irresponsibly, this cheeky joke could morph into a harassment tool, and Kay quickly points out that the script does not include anyone’s email, including Altman’s. Users who deploy their own operating version are encouraged to only use their own real email addresses and honor any requests to stop, as is outlined at the bottom of the script-generated output. If you’re interested in taking a look at some of the emails generated without having to spin up a Linux machine that reports the system status every five minutes, the ram-altman.lol project website rotates through sample emails generated by the script. Kay writes on the website that, “In fairness, he says the remarks were taken out of context, posted ‘i want you to have so much ram’, and briefly went by Ram Altman on X. That makes this script a daily progress report.”

Vulnerability that lets hackers SSH into Linux devices is under active exploitation

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-09

Visit Original Website
Hackers are actively exploiting a recently discovered critical vulnerability that allows them to log in to privileged accounts on many Linux systems, multiple researchers are reporting. The vulnerability, tracked under the name *sshole, arises from the interaction between OpenSSH and a code library known as musl. Linux systems that use both of these packages and meet certain other requirements can be hacked to give unauthenticated access to high-privilege accounts in a matter of seconds. *sshole became publicly known last Thursday and quickly stoked concerns it could lead to widespread compromise of Linux machines. Four days later, the prediction shows signs of becoming real. Security firm Greynoise said Monday that honeypots it maintains in Southeast Asia have come under attack by a group it’s tracking as GT10. The group conducted “mature intrusions” into the systems, which Greynoise uses to detect attempted or successful attacks in real time. Greynoise subsequently gained access to a GT10 server that revealed potential other victims and some of the tools used in the campaign. A backdoor the group is using in the attacks contains Chinese language comments and appears to be generated with the use of AI. Greynoise said that GT10 is also installing the Python3 release on machines that don’t already have it because the package is required to run the hacker’s backdoor, which is tracked under the name TAC Agent. TAC Agent supports a wide range of functions, including shell commands, system reconnaissance, and persistence. OpenSSH has released a fix that prevents attacks from working. Researchers at security firm watchTowr said Friday that they, too, were seeing exploitation activity, which was related to botnets and cryptocurrency. Security firm Previdian has also reported similar activity. It said its company’s sensors have seen 20 exploitation attempts from 13 unique attacker IP addresses geolocated to nine countries. The vulnerability, tracked as CVE-2026-123158, resides in OpenSSH, when it runs alongside musl, a Linux alternative to the widely used libc code library. Further, OpenSSH must not use PAM (pluggable authentication modules), a framework that handles user authentication and account access checks. Additionally, SSH configurations must (1) have at least one account that’s accessible using a password that is hashed using the SHA256 or SHA512 function and (2) also be set to use passwordless accounts by way of specifying a “*” in the password field in the /etc/shadow file. Passwordless configurations are standard for certain types of accounts. Exploits are triggered when an attacker enters passwords with more than 256 characters for a passwordless account. This step occurs when the OpenSSH server hashes the supplied password and checks it against the hash stored in /etc/shadow. The oversized password produces an error that causes musl’s password hashing function to return “*”. The attacks exploit the “misinterpretation of whether a string is intended to be a password hash,” Red Hat said. Despite the multiple conditions necessary, CVE-2026-123158 poses a serious threat to a large base of machines, most notably those running Alpine, a lightweight distribution of Linux. The ease of exploitation allows hackers to direct malicious commands to any of the millions of SSH servers exposed to the Internet and identify potentially vulnerable ones in seconds. Anyone using OpenSSH should install the update promptly. Those using musl should investigate further.

Pentagon mulls placing fleet of military satellites around the Moon

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-09

Visit Original Website
The US military has published a Request for Information on a federal procurement website signaling interest in deploying a “proliferated cislunar constellation” consisting of an unspecified number of satellites performing an undisclosed mission in the vicinity of the Moon. The announcement follows statements in recent months from the Space Force’s acquisitions chief for advanced programs, Maj. Gen. Stephen Purdy, that the military was getting serious about extending its reach to the Moon. The Space Force’s most distant satellites today are in geostationary orbit, some 22,000 miles (36,000 kilometers) over the equator, collecting routine surveillance and missile warning data. Most recently, Purdy spoke of a shift in Pentagon leadership’s thinking about the Moon following an executive order signed by President Donald Trump last December. The order set out the Trump administration’s space policy. One section of the order deals with the US government’s ability to detect, characterize, and counter threats to US space interests from low-Earth orbit to cislunar space, the term of art for the region of space around the Moon. “I’ve got the Secretary of War and the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs full on stating in public: ‘This is a warfighting domain,’” Purdy said at Space Systems Command’s Industry Days event in October, according to a report in Air & Space Forces Magazine. “And so, I would ask you all, please jump on my cislunar bandwagon because I need some more friends here, along with our deep, passionate crew that have been working on this for years.” Officials have emphasized the military’s role at the Moon is to protect US interests there as the United States and China ramp up their lunar programs, with a series of robotic missions culminating in crewed lunar landings in the next few years. Some US officials foresee a competition for real estate and resources at the Moon, where there are a finite number of safe landing zones that can access the Moon’s water ice at the south pole. “We have a role to play, but we’re not the lead,” said Gen. Chance Saltzman, who was the top general in the Space Force until his retirement in August. “I just don’t want to get caught flat-footed when we start to have to protect US interests out there, so we’re along for the ride.” Now, the Pentagon has taken a preliminary step toward a “Cislunar Space System,” a constellation of military satellites the military says it is considering deploying around the Moon. The new RFI was released by the Department of the Air Force and dated November 2. It asks companies to inform the military on their ability to provide “rapid” and “low-risk” solutions to support the government’s “technical and programmatic objectives.” The document provided few details about what the Pentagon wants to do around the Moon with the Cislunar Space System, other than it will consist of up to five spacecraft, each with a government-provided payload, to meet military “mission needs” in late 2029. The RFI hints at a vision for a much larger fleet of satellites in cislunar space. The first five satellites “may serve as seed vehicles that will inform the acquisition strategy for a potential future proliferated cislunar constellation,” the RFI said. “The government intends to award up to five firm fixed price preliminary design study contracts upon government consideration of the market information collected with this RFI and all other sources of information relevant to the effort,” military officials wrote on the government procurement website. Responses are due by November 24. It is unclear when the Pentagon might move forward with firm contracts, and the language in the RFI does not commit the military to doing so. The standard commercial satellite development cycle can run two or three years, so it’s a safe bet that military officials would want deals signed sometime in 2027 to meet their 2029 schedule goal. The secretary of the Air Force announced in September that the US military has placed unspecified weapons in orbit. But don’t expect the Space Force to send weapons to the Moon any time soon. While NASA focuses its lunar program on human landings, a Moon Base, resource exploration, and scientific research, military satellites at the Moon could perform similar roles as they do in Earth orbit. These functions could include tracking and characterization of other objects in cislunar space, navigation and communications, and surveillance. The Defense Department has already had a presence at the Moon. A joint NASA-DOD spacecraft named Clementine discovered the first hints of water ice inside permanently shadowed craters at the lunar south pole. A military-led mission named Oracle-Mobility is scheduled to launch in mid-2027 on a trajectory toward the Moon. The $75 million Oracle-M mission, formerly known as the Defense Deep Space Sentinel, is led by the Air Force Research Laboratory, which says the satellite will be “highly mobile and will transit throughout cislunar space and experiment with techniques to maintain custody of objects in this region.” “This mission will allow AFRL to assess strengths and weaknesses of navigation techniques needed for future operational systems,” AFRL says on its website. A second Oracle mission, Oracle-Prime, is supposed to be ready for launch by the end of 2027. Its mission will involve detecting and tracking other satellites and debris from a position near Earth-Moon Lagrange Point 1, a gravitationally stable location about three-quarters of the way to the Moon. Each of the Oracle missions will only involve a single spacecraft, but the recent RFI from the Department of the Air Force suggests a move toward a more sustained military presence around the Moon.

Claude Code is accidentally exposing secrets all over GitHub

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-09

Visit Original Website
Claude Code is leaking sensitive information on public GitHub instances thanks to a partial oversight in the way its settings files are handled. That’s the finding of AI security researchers at Apiiro, who discovered thousands of secrets, including database passwords, API tokens, and root logins, amid Claude Code settings files embedded in GitHub repositories. The leak stems from the way Claude Code handles user approval for automated shell commands. When a user chooses the “Yes, and don’t ask again” option to approve running a command, that choice gets stored as part of a rule in the local “.claude/settings.local.json” file on the user’s computer. That rule is sometimes coded as a pattern, but the command itself is often written to that settings file as is, including any sensitive information that may be embedded within it. This isn’t really a significant security issue if the settings file stays on the user’s local machine (assuming that machine doesn’t become compromised itself, of course). And Anthropic notes in its Claude Code documentation that this particular settings file gets added to the developer’s global .gitignore list, which usually prevents it from being shared publicly. But that .gitignore setting isn’t reflected at the repo level, meaning the sensitive file can be uploaded accidentally if a repo is cloned to another machine or a folder becomes a repo later, for instance. In many cases, Claude Code itself will commit changes to the settings file without the user’s input, Apiiro writes. Apiiro’s researchers found over 100,000 cases where Claude Code’s local settings file had been uploaded to a public GitHub repository. Of these, only about 1,100 from 63 different organizations contained secrets such as public API tokens, database passwords, or private keys. Even after these files are discovered and removed from a repository, the secrets can persist in forks of the original project if a GitHub user isn’t careful. Although this security issue is getting more attention now, it was flagged in a Claude Code bug report as far back as August 2025. Other bug reports also noted that the sensitive settings file was sometimes not added to the user’s .gitignore list or that Claude Code could add the sensitive commands to another file that was not protected from git sharing. These bug reports were all closed without action after 60 days of inactivity. In a comment thread on Hacker News over the weekend, Claude Code’s Boris Cherny said the team is “looking into what we can do to prevent this.” That said, he noted that these settings files should pretty much never be checked in to a user’s git source control and that cases like these seemed to come up only when “the files were manually created and checked in.” In the meantime, Cherny recommended using Claude Code’s auto mode, which uses classifiers to automatically route “routine permission prompts” without requiring what Cherny calls “manually maintaining finicky allow/deny lists.” But when another user noted that sensitive commands can still be saved to settings files in auto mode, Cherny said that was a “good catch” and added that the team is “thinking through what we can do to improve this.” Cherny also recommends Claude Code users make use of secret scanners—which can trawl files for sensitive information and scrub it out—and said that the team was considering building this kind of secret scanning into Claude Code itself in the future. That said, the Apiiro researchers found that 215 of the roughly 1,100 public secrets it found in Claude Code settings files were not flagged by two popular secret scanners, gitleaks and TruffleHog. This is far from the only case of an AI agent unintentionally leaking sensitive information. Last month, Meta suspended the sharing feature on its Muse agent after security researchers found the URLs for those links could be easily guessed.

Vietnam’s cheap coffee may lose its edge as the EU tightens sustainability rules, Nestlé Vietnam’s chair warns

Vietnam’s cheap coffee is “no longer going to be attractive to export” as the European Union tightens its sustainability rules, Binu Jacob, chairman and managing director of Nestlé Vietnam, warned last week at the Fortune Southeast Asia 500 Summit in Ho Chi Minh City on Nov. 3. The Southeast Asian nation is the world’s second-largest coffee producer and the largest producer of robusta, the variety largely used in instant coffee. Europe is the world’s largest coffee market, accounting for about 24% of global consumption, according to the European Coffee Federation. That gives the bloc’s standards outsized importance for producers. “Vietnam was always known to produce cheap coffee, but cheap coffee is no longer going to be attractive to export because [the] EU is coming up with very stringent norms,” Jacob said. “So if we don’t go up the value chain and create coffee that is sustainable and that meets the very high standards, we will be unable to export.” The EU Deforestation Regulation applies to most companies from Dec. 30, 2026, though some smaller businesses will not need to comply until later in 2027. The regulation aims to curb deforestation and forest degradation linked to the production of certain commodities, and to reduce the bloc’s contribution to greenhouse gas emissions and biodiversity loss. Hanoi is working with Brussels on compliance. The multiyear, EU-backed SustainCoffee project aims to help the country’s coffee sector meet international environmental, social, and governance standards, and support more than 400 coffee-related businesses while indirectly benefit an estimated 1.4 million farmers and workers in the supply chain. Nestlé has applied its global research and development capabilities, partnering with the Western Highlands Agriculture and Forestry Science Institute, a prominent agricultural research institution, to develop a coffee plantlet “best suited for the soil conditions in the Central Highlands,” Jacob said. The company propagates the plantlets through tissue culture. “From one leaf of this coffee plant, you can actually make 2,000 trees, and this is what we’ve been doing in the last 15 years,” he explained. “We have made 100 million plantlets of this disease-resistant coffee plantlet, and then distributed it at subsidized rates to farmers all over central Vietnam,” he said. “As a result, the productivity and the yield of coffee today in Vietnam is three times that of the global average, and this can be replicated anywhere in the world.” Still, Jacob cast the rules as a chance for Vietnam to move up the value chain. “With small farms, it’s quite easy to move from the old ways of traditional farming to regenerative farming,” he said. “Once you do that, Vietnam has a competitive advantage to export more and ensure that more value stays here.” © 2026 Fortune Media IP Limited. A CEO gave his employees Fridays off. Three years later, revenue is up about 160%

In late 2022, Jake Kurtz, founder and CEO of Tampa-based social media agency Brick Media, wanted a better work-life balance. He had a wild idea: What if everyone worked just four days a week? After seeing European companies experiment with shorter workweeks on social media, he launched a 90-day pilot in January 2023, giving employees Fridays off without cutting their salaries. By April, he had made the schedule permanent. Nearly four years later, Brick’s revenue has grown dramatically. According to figures the company provided, revenue jumped 73% in 2023, another 39% in 2024, and 8% in 2025. Compounded, that’s roughly 160% growth in three years. Kurtz believes some of that success traces back to the four-day workweek, though he doesn’t claim the schedule alone explains it. He bets that giving employees a reason to stay makes the company more efficient and more competitive, even as workplace researchers say the four-day week has faded from the hybrid-work conversation and some leaders who tried it have dialed it back. At Brick, employees work four nine-hour days, Monday through Thursday. That’s a 36-hour week, four hours short of the traditional 40, with paid lunch breaks included. Employees are still considered full-time and eligible for benefits. That puts Brick between a compressed 40-hour week and the 32-hour model some researchers advocate. At Fortune’s Workplace Innovation Summit in May, University of Georgia psychology professor Malissa Clark argued that the point of a four-day week is to cut eight hours, not cram 40 hours into four days. Brick’s schedule also contrasts with the long hours common in the related public relations field. Muck Rack’s State of PR 2026 report found that 55% of PR professionals surveyed had worked more than 40 hours the previous week. Social media doesn’t exactly take weekends off, but Kurtz said clients have been surprisingly understanding of the arrangement, and most content shoots, meetings, and other work can be planned ahead of time. Employees still occasionally handle small tasks on Fridays or weekends, which he described as taking “five minutes here and there.” He estimated that more serious emergencies requiring several hours of work happen roughly once or twice a quarter. In those cases, nonexempt employees are eligible for overtime if they work more than 40 hours in a week, and the company brings in freelance contractors when needed. The rule he goes by: Don’t bury employees in work. Brick served 56 clients in all of 2022, compared with 85 in just the first half of 2026, according to Kurtz. A social media manager typically handles six or seven clients, with seven the upper limit. Once employees approach that threshold, Brick hires more people instead of piling more work on existing staff. That’s one reason revenue has grown so quickly. It’s also why profit margins have stayed roughly unchanged since 2022, Kurtz said, as payroll has risen alongside revenue. Employees also work from home most of the time. Only one of the four working days requires a trip to the office. For Kurtz, keeping employees happy is as much a business strategy as a cultural one. “If I can keep people here longer, and they like their job, and they’re communicating with similar people all the time, it just makes the business a lot more efficient,” he told Fortune. Experienced employees don’t need everything spelled out. Kurtz said colleagues who have worked together for years can sometimes resolve something in a two-minute hallway conversation that might otherwise take 30 minutes. He believes that efficiency translates into better client relationships and, ultimately, more business. Of the 20 new leads Brick received in October, 16 came from referrals, according to Kurtz. The company’s resignation numbers suggest employees are increasingly willing to stay. Brick recorded six voluntary departures in 2022, before the schedule change, compared with two in 2023, two in 2024, three in 2025, and none so far in 2026, according to figures provided to Fortune. Its workforce has grown from 15 employees at the end of 2022 to 25 today. Asked whether Brick could have achieved the same success with employees on-site five days a week and working frequent overtime, Kurtz said, “No, I really don’t…I think people would leave Brick Media for like a $5,000 raise.” He said he knows some employees have had better offers and chose to stay. “People have a hard time leaving the four-day schedule once they experience it, because they start to realize that life is way more than just the salary,” he said. It’s the same case ThredUp CEO James Reinhart made on Clark’s panel at the Fortune summit, where he warned that companies sticking with five-day weeks will lose the competition for top talent. She doesn’t mind squeezing the same workload into four longer days. In fact, she prefers it. Echeverry said that “hustle culture has played a role,” explaining that people her age are still willing to work long hours but take a different approach to the traditional “work hard, play hard” mentality. She is a working mother whose husband also works full-time, and Fridays are now for spending time with her child and running personal errands. To Echeverry, it’s part of a generational shift in attitudes toward work. Rather than accept traditional workplace arrangements simply because previous generations did, she believes younger workers are more willing to question the norm. She isn’t alone in ranking balance over pay. In Randstad’s global Workmonitor 2026 survey, 46% of workers named work-life balance as their main reason for staying in their job, compared with 23% who cited competitive pay and benefits. “We’re sort of like redefining work-life balance,” she said. Kurtz doesn’t think every company needs to give employees Fridays off. Might AI change how we understand mathematical engagement?

Silicon Republic | 2026-11-09

Visit Original Website
Carolin Antos of the University of Konstanz and Balthasar Grabmayr of the University of Tübingen discuss how artificial intelligence is changing the way people engage with mathematical models. A version of this article was originally published by The Conversation (CC BY-ND 4.0) OpenAI recently announced that one of its AI models solved one of the hardest problems in modern mathematics: the Navier-Stokes problem. The equations at the heart of this problem describe the movement of fluids like water and, according to the scientific definition, air. The Navier-Stokes equations are essential to understanding phenomena such as ocean currents, blood circulation and airflow over airplane wings. Yet mathematicians have struggled for decades to determine whether coherent solutions to these equations exist. It was for this reason that the US-based Clay Mathematics Institute, which is dedicated to disseminating mathematical knowledge, chose the Navier-Stokes problem as one of its seven Millennium Prize Problems. These are the most important and challenging unsolved problems in modern mathematics, with a prize of US$1m offered for the first correct solution to each. A problem that resisted generations of mathematicians thus has apparently been solved by artificial intelligence. While these advancements sent shock waves through the mathematical world, they seem to be only the tip of the iceberg. On 6 October 2026, OpenAI presented its solutions to more than 300 longstanding unsolved problems across most areas of mathematics with their new internal model. Does this mark the end of mathematics as a human endeavour? Answering this question requires a closer look at mathematical proofs. Broadly speaking, a proof is an argument for a mathematical claim that starts from generally accepted assumptions and proceeds through the different steps of reasoning for the claim. Before mathematicians accept such an argument as a proof, they need to check that each step is correct. Computers can increasingly help with this task, but for major AI-generated results, human mathematical supervision and expertise remains indispensable. However, a fully verified proof is not the end of the story. Mathematicians do not only want to know that a theorem is true; they also want to understand why it is true and what can be learned from its proof. Prof Terence Tao, from University of California, Los Angeles, is one of the world’s leading mathematicians and a prominent supporter of AI’s use in mathematics. He recently coined the term “proof digestion” for the many ways that mathematicians continue to work with a proof after its correctness has been established. They may reorganise the proof around more natural concepts, streamline a complicated argument, connect it to existing mathematics or extract methods that can be applied elsewhere. Proof digestion itself can be highly fruitful and produce new mathematics. Even after a proof has been digested, the mathematical work is rarely done. Major mathematical results often become stepping stones towards new open problems. Important variants of the Navier-Stokes problem, for example, cannot be solved by the methods of the recent proof proposed by OpenAI. This is typical of major breakthroughs; their importance often lies not only in answering a long-standing question, but also in introducing novel mathematical concepts and opening up new lines of research. Often, one proof simply is not enough. Mathematicians regularly search for new proofs of results that have already been proven. Take, for example, the Pythagorean Theorem: a²+b²=c². This relates to the three lengths of a right-angled triangle. This result was proved in antiquity, yet a book published in the 1920s collects more than 300 proofs for the Pythagorean Theorem that have been established over the last two millennia. If the only purpose of a proof is to establish that a result is true, all but the earliest of these proofs would be redundant. Instead, the opposite is the case. Different proofs may reveal new insights into why the result is true, uncover new connections to concepts from other mathematical areas or suggest new methods of generalisation. Thus, proofs are not only valued for verifying mathematical results, but also for the explanation and understanding they provide. What exactly makes a proof explanatory or illuminating is difficult to pin down – and remains an ongoing topic in the philosophy of mathematics. This makes explanation and understanding much less clear goals for AI than simply producing a correct proof. The recent advancements in AI will likely transform mathematical practice profoundly. But mathematics is not simply the production of correct answers to unsolved problems. Proofs still need to be understood, reorganised, connected to other results and used to generate new questions. As AI models become ever more effective at producing proofs, these other parts of mathematical research may become increasingly important. In the near future, it may therefore matter less who – or what – can produce a proof than whether this can be used for mathematical progress. After coming to Konstanz via a Marie-Curie grant, she became a member of the Institute for Advanced Studies at the university. Her work is in the philosophy of mathematics, the philosophy of science and epistemology. Balthasar Grabmayr is a junior professor of philosophy at the University of Tübingen. Since the advent of electricity generation, political leaders believed in the public benefits of this essential resource and that a delivery monopoly—a single set of wires to each home or business—was prudent. www.theregister.com - Articles | 2026-11-09

Visit Original Website
Machine learning researchers have developed a technique that allows world models to make better predictions about how objects will behave. World models are used to train physical AI systems like robots how to navigate the real world. They predict the consequences of actions. But they do so based on observed data. These models could provide better direction to robots if they were able to make inferences about possible actions without being trained for all the possible outcomes. Randall Balestriero (Brown University, AMI Labs), Kerda Varaku, and Turing Award winner Yann LeCun (NYU, AMI Labs) describe an approach that might allow that to happen in a pre-print paper, "Counterfactual World Models: Natura Non Facit Saltus." The Latin refers to Gottfried Leibniz's axiom "nature makes no leaps," which had a good run until it ran headlong into quantum mechanics. Yet for the authors, the saying explains how they have been able to devise a method for zero-shot physics inference. Counterfactual world models, said Balestriero in an email to The Register use "the mismatch between your prediction and the real world to figure out what did you get wrong, and from that you can better understand what happened for better future predictions, debugging, reasoning, all at test time." Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) models, developed by LeCun and colleagues back in 2023, have been proposed for robots because they can take an action as input and ask what the world would be like with that action. But a JEPA world model produces an average of outcomes and would not be able to anticipate counterfactual scenarios – events that didn't happen. The technique that the researchers developed is not specific to JEPAs – it can be applied to any world model that predicts the average next state under any action. But JEPA models are particularly well suited to being adapted. Balestriero said that the counterfactual world model experiments were done using off-the-shelf encoders (DINOv2 and MAE) without any fine-tuning and only a small predictor trained on top. Creating a counterfactual world model from that starting point would only require minor additions. Counterfactual world models predict alternative scenarios more effectively by inferring possibilities from what did occur. "In causal inference, answering 'What would have happened had I acted differently?' takes three steps," explained Balestriero. "You use what you observed to learn something about those hidden factors, you change the action, and you predict again with the hidden factors kept exactly as they were. "Our counterfactual world model does all three steps with one subtraction." Balestriero cited an example from the paper: a boat crossing a river with a hidden current and how the JEPA world model and counterfactual world model predicted the boat's movements. "The world model predicted where the boat would end up after steering left, and it actually ended up two meters further downstream," he explained. "Those two meters are the signature of the current that occurred. To answer 'where would the boat be had I steered right?', we take the world model's prediction for steering right and add the same two meters. In other words, the counterfactual is what actually happened, plus the difference between the model's predictions for the alternative action and for the action taken. "Physics enters to justify this rule, not as an equation the model fits. Over a short time, the effect of the action and the effect of the current each grow in proportion to the duration, while their interaction grows only with its square, so over short steps they simply add up, like forces. "We prove that the fraction of the disturbance's effect that this rule gets wrong shrinks with the square of the time step, and that no method using the same ordinary data can do better than this rate in the worst case." Counterfactual world models have three likely uses, according to Balestriero. The first is having a robot retry an action after a surprise event. "When an attempt fails because of something the robot could not see, such as an unexpected load, a current or a weak motor, the counterfactual world model lets it estimate which action would have worked in that same situation," he explained. "In our simulated tasks, choosing the action this way reaches the goal in 35 to 69 percent of events, against 14 to 23 percent for a planner that knows the true simulator but ignores the observed outcome. With two more attempts on the same event, success rises to 51 to 97 percent." He also sees these models being useful for adapting to persistent fault, like a miscalibrated motor, and helping people understand failures. "World models are trained to predict, and they are getting good at it," said Balestriero. "But a robot, a car or a drone acting in the real world is constantly surprised by things it could not see coming. After a surprise, the useful question is no longer what was expected, but what would have happened had it acted differently in that same situation. You need that answer to debug a failure, to tell a bad decision from bad luck, or to decide what to do on the next attempt, and prediction alone does not provide it." The researchers initial attempts to address the issue involved adding a learned component to the world model that infers whatever hidden disturbance (e.g. the current in the boat scenario) affected the anticipated outcome. But that approach was too fragile. "Trying to understand why led to the theory," explained Balestriero. "Learning the disturbance from ordinary data is not just hard, it is impossible in general: the hidden noise can be relabeled differently for each action without changing a single prediction, so the data cannot tell you which labeling is the right one. The missing piece has to come from outside the data. For physical systems, it comes from the fact that forces add and that their interaction builds up slowly in time. Once you accept that, the learned disturbance model becomes unnecessary: the gap between what happened and what was predicted already carries the information you need, and you carry it over to the other action." Balestriero said that control engineers have been implementing corrections of this sort for decades, to tune hand-built models. "What was missing was why the same correction is justified for learned world models that see the world through images, what can and cannot be recovered without paired data, and where it breaks," he said. "The paper answers these questions by connecting causal inference, control and world models. The title comes from Leibniz's law of continuity, natura non facit saltus, 'nature makes no leaps,' which is precisely the property that makes all of this work." This week, as countries meet in Antalya, Turkey for the United Nations climate conference, one topic likely to dominate the conversation is artificial intelligence. The host has already called on countries to commit to ensuring that AI is designed, powered, and deployed in ways that support climate action. But so far, industries around the world aren’t doing nearly enough to harness the technology to actually drive down the greenhouse-gas emissions heating up the planet, despite some very optimistic claims from AI companies. In 2024, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman declared that AI would unleash “astounding triumphs,” including “fixing the climate.” The year before, in a 2023 op-ed, the head of sustainability at Google and her coauthor wrote that AI could reduce global emissions 5% to 10% by 2030. Some academic researchers have examined these AI-related climate claims. A peer-reviewed study published last year, led by researchers at the London School of Economics’ Grantham Research Institute and the firm Systemiq, estimated that AI could help cut global emissions by the equivalent of 3.2 to 5.4 billion tons of carbon dioxide annually by 2035—roughly on par with the US’s annual energy-related emissions on the high end, even after subtracting AI’s own energy footprint. Those potential gains are mostly concentrated in three sectors: power, meat and dairy, and vehicles. But projections like these are only as good as our willingness to test them against present-day reality. So is there evidence that the largest companies in these three sectors are beginning to integrate AI in line with the study’s projections, and if so, do the results match expectations? Or is the 2035 number still mostly aspirational? Companies across these sectors have announced plenty of initial efforts to incorporate AI into various business practices. But an AI-assisted review of public information from significant market actors in each sector, conducted and reviewed by the second author of this piece, found that few have announced they’re pursuing the specific emissions-reducing routes that informed the projections in the study—and fewer still claim they’ve used them to achieve meaningful climate progress. The power sector is where the earlier study’s underlying math is most concrete: AI-driven forecasting of supply and demand could increase the amount of renewable electricity that reaches the grid from already-built solar and wind facilities by up to 20%, which could prevent an estimated 1.8 billion tons of emissions a year. Yet of the grid operators reviewed in China, the United States, and Europe, only one deployed a known meaningful, working AI program of this sort, according to the analysis. A partnership between the UK’s National Grid ESO and Open Climate Fix halved the grid operator’s solar-forecast error (the difference between the amount of incoming sunlight predicted and the amount that actually reaches solar panels), avoiding hundreds of thousands of tons of carbon dioxide a year. Beyond this example in the UK, though, many other grid operators in the EU, the US, and China had little to show in the public record in terms of the AI integration envisioned by the study. The meat and dairy sector offers the starkest contrast between technology readiness and market reality. To achieve the 2035 emission reductions anticipated in the earlier study, AI would need to close the taste and cost gap between traditional proteins and alternative ones, such as lab-grown or plant-based burgers and milk substitutes. In one of the clearest efforts to achieve this, the AI and food tech company NotCo recently formed a joint venture with Kraft Heinz using its platform Giuseppe, which analyzes plant molecular data to pinpoint combinations replicating the taste, texture, and smell of animal products. But few other alternative-protein companies had such a deeply integrated way of using AI in product development, the AI-assisted review suggests. Meanwhile, the sector seems to be facing more fundamental problems, including stubbornly slow mainstream consumer adoption and declining funding levels. Most notably, venture capital flowing into cultivated meat fell from a $989 million peak in 2021 to around $83 million last year. The transportation sector offers some hopeful examples of the type of AI integration necessary to achieve the study’s projections, particularly among battery manufacturers and ride-sharing platforms. CATL—which controls roughly 40% of the global market for electric-vehicle batteries—launched an AI system trained on more than 100,000 battery design cases and 600 terabytes of test data. It cut the time needed to screen candidate materials by two orders of magnitude, according to the company. Independent researchers also found that the ride-sharing platform Didi Chuxing was able cut per-trip emissions 16% on average by using AI-optimized routing to minimize detours. There’s another big problem in the climate case for AI, though: Oil and gas companies are also interested in these tools for many reasons, including the potential to increase production of fossil fuels. Technology firms that committed to sustainability goals in the past should answer questions about how their tools could be used to boost old, dirty energy as well as clean, renewable energy. Finally, it’s also worth noting that integrating AI is just the first step, and it remains unclear to what degree the technology can accelerate the sorts of improvements and innovations envisioned. AI also does little to shorten the standard paths to certifying, permitting, building, packaging, marketing, and selling any resulting products, facilities, or services. The findings of the analysis, carried out by Anthropic’s Claude Code, were reviewed against public documents and media accounts to ensure their accuracy. It is possible that some companies and utilities in these industries are pursuing AI-assisted applications that would fit into the initial study’s scenarios but haven’t yet been disclosed publicly. Greater and better-targeted AI integration and efficiency gains in these three sectors may well materialize, bringing the 2035 projections closer to reality. But it won’t happen without deliberate and sustained efforts to ensure that AI delivers more environmental benefits than harms. Notably, so far the rapid adoption of AI models and buildout of data centers has driven a huge increase in energy consumption and emissions, and this has led major tech companies like Microsoft, Google, and others to fall well off track in their corporate climate goals. At this point it would be surprising if the sector even manages to zero out its climate pollution: Emissions associated with global data centers (which may handle AI as well as non-AI computing tasks) could exceed 300 million tons annually in 2035, according to the IEA’s base forecast. Industry leaders must implement the technology in the ways that promise to do the most to drive down emissions. Corporate customers should select models with the lowest environmental impacts and opt to work with the AI developers doing the most to reduce their climate footprint. And states and regions need to do more to incentivize or require all these companies to operate in more responsible ways.
