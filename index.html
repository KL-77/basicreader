Japan launches mission to bring home a piece of the Martian moon Phobos

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-20

Visit Original Website
Japan launched a $578 million mission Monday on a five-year expedition to land on the largest moon of Mars and retrieve samples for return to Earth, a daring international project that could yield new insights into the formation of the Solar System. The robotic Martian Moons eXploration mission, known as MMX, lifted off at 3:41 pm EDT Monday (4:41 am Japan Standard Time Tuesday) from Tanegashima Space Center in southern Japan. The MMX spacecraft rode into orbit atop a 207-foot-tall (63-meter) H3 rocket, Japan’s flagship launch vehicle. The rocket assigned to MMX debuted a new souped-up configuration with four strap-on solid rocket boosters, full-throttle main engines, and an extended payload shroud to accommodate the demands of Japan’s heaviest and most ambitious interplanetary mission to date. The rocket’s upper stage accelerated the MMX spacecraft to a top speed of more than 24,000 mph, fast enough to break free of Earth’s gravitational pull for the 10-month voyage to Mars. The nearly 9,900-pound (4.5-metric ton) spacecraft—two-thirds of its mass comprised of propellant—separated from the H3 rocket about 71 minutes after liftoff. Hiroshi Yamakawa, president of the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA), declared the launch a success. “A long journey of five years has just started,” Yamakawa said. MMX established contact with ground controllers, unfurled its solar panels, and was in a “very stable condition” soon after separating from the H3 rocket, according to Yasuhiro Kawakatsu, the mission’s project manager at JAXA. “So far, so good.” “The MMX [mission] we launched today is a challenging mission aiming for the world’s first sample return from the Martian moons,” said Yoshihiro Seki, Japan’s science minister. “We hope that the results obtained through this mission will contribute to the development of our nation’s space science and exploration technology.” The mission to the Martian moons builds on Japan’s success in leading the Hayabusa and Hayabusa 2 missions, the world’s first two spacecraft to bring back pristine specimens from asteroids. Scientists believe Phobos could itself be an asteroid that was captured into orbit around Mars billions of years ago. One of MMX’s science goals is finding out if that theory is correct. With the Hayabusa missions, sample returns from deep space have become a “speciality” for Japan’s space program, Kawakatsu said. For MMX, Japan attracted contributions from NASA and space agencies in Europe. “We have a proven track record of success in these areas,” Kawakatsu said. “Because of this, I believe they are participating with the understanding that if they want to conduct celestial body sample returns, joining a mission led by Japan is more certain to succeed than the US or Europe launching their own missions.” MMX will spend about three years in the vicinity of Mars after arriving there next August, conducting a series of encounters with the Martian moons Phobos and Deimos. Phobos is the larger of the two, with a lumpy, potato-like shape, an average diameter of 14 miles (22 kilometers), and a surface area comparable to the city of Houston. Upon reaching Mars, MMX will jettison its propulsion module, dead weight after burning all its fuel to enter orbit around the red planet. That will leave the main section of the MMX spacecraft and its four-legged landing gear, known as the exploration module, to move closer to Phobos. The moon’s gravity is too weak and the pull of Martian gravity too strong for the MMX spacecraft to enter into a stable orbit around Phobos. Instead, the spacecraft will fly in a so-called quasi-satellite orbit, remaining bound to Mars while still able to maneuver around Phobos. The mission’s reconnaissance of Phobos will culminate in late 2028 or early 2029, when the MMX mothership will release a small rover to drop onto Phobos. The four-wheeled robot, named Idefix, is a contribution from the French and German space agencies. Idefix, the size of a large shoebox, will attempt to drive around Phobos for up to 100 days while grappling with the moon’s rough topography and tenuous gravity, less than one-thousandth that of Earth’s. The climax for MMX will come later in 2029, when the main spacecraft will attempt up to two landings on Phobos. The probe’s landing gear is designed to absorb energy of each landing to mitigate the risk of tipping over or bouncing back into space, a real concern with the feeble and uneven gravity field on Phobos. The spacecraft will stay on the surface of Phobos for several hours, using two independent mechanisms to capture samples from the Martian moon. A coring drill on the end of the probe’s robotic arm will scoop out a cigar-shaped specimen, while a NASA-provided pneumatic sampler on one of the craft’s landing legs will fire a cartridge of high-pressure nitrogen gas to sweep bits of Phobos in a collection canister. All told, scientists expect MMX to retrieve at least 10 grams, or one-third of an ounce, of material from Phobos. After climbing back into space, MMX will discard its landing gear and fire its engines in 2030 to head for Earth, where it will guide a sample return capsule toward a remote landing zone in South Australia in 2031. Scientists will recover the samples for analysis in laboratories in Japan. US researchers will get 15 percent of the Phobos specimens in return for NASA’s contributions to the MMX mission, which include one of the spacecraft’s samplers and a gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer to measure the elemental makeup of the Martian moons. “One of the most intriguing questions about Phobos and Deimos is simply why these two little moons are there in the first place,” said Tom Statler, lead scientist for solar system small bodies at NASA. “In some ways, they resemble asteroids from the outer part of the asteroid belt, so they could be asteroids that were captured by Mars’s gravity. But there are other lines of evidence pointing to these moons being remnants of a giant impact early in Mars’s history.” “By going to the Martian moons, studying them up close, and bringing samples back to Earth, we’ll be able to determine whether Phobos material is more consistent with an asteroid composition or with debris created by a collision involving Mars,” Statler said. Phobos is roughly 40 times closer to Mars than our Moon is to Earth. Its proximity to Mars means there is a chance MMX might scoop up traces of the red planet itself that rained down on Phobos after its formation or capture. This could offer a taste of what’s to come when scientists finally get their hands on specimens directly from the surface of Mars, a significantly more difficult undertaking than a sample return from Phobos. The reconnaissance of Phobos by MMX could also help future mission planners consider whether the moon is a suitable location for human exploration, perhaps as a stopover between Earth and Mars, Kawakatsu said. MMX is not the first mission to attempt a sample return from Phobos. Russia’s Phobos-Grunt sample return mission failed soon after launch in 2011, before it could set off for Mars. The Soviet Union launched two spacecraft to Mars equipped with Phobos landers in 1988, but ground teams lost contact with both spacecraft before they completed their missions. With MMX, scientists hope to finally acquire the ground truth on the Martian moons they have sought for decades. “Basically, these objects look more like primitive asteroids than anything else, but the data is coming from remote sensing from far away,” said Patrick Michel, a French asteroid scientist and co-leader of the Idefix rover project. “That cannot really tell us exactly what are the properties, as we can do with a sample return in the lab.” “The goal of the mission is effectively to reveal the origin of the Martian moons,” Michel said. “If we understand the origin, that will tell us something about the early phases of the Solar System.”

Pennsylvania issues alert on congenital measles—something US hasn't seen for decades

Pennsylvania’s health department issued a health alert Monday as cases of measles in pregnancy and, in turn, cases of congenital measles have continued to increase. Before the current outbreak—which continues to rage across the state—congenital measles cases hadn’t been seen in decades, the department noted in its advisory. The alert included detailed clinical guidance for managing measles exposures and cases during pregnancy as well as cases in newborns. Many doctors have never seen such cases in their careers given the decades of successful use of the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine. But now, with vaccination rates slipping nationwide, the highly infectious virus is better able to get footholds in communities and spread rapidly through under-vaccinated pockets, as is happening in Pennsylvania. As of October 19, Pennsylvania had reported 1,286 measles cases this year across 42 counties. Of those, 247 have been hospitalized, and six people have reportedly died (though the latest reported death is still pending an investigation by the state health department before it is added to the state tally). The Pennsylvania health department did not immediately respond to a request for information from Ars Technica. But reporting from The 19th earlier this month put the number of measles cases during pregnancy at 53. At least one case of congenital measles led to a death; the first death reported in the current outbreak was in a newborn baby boy, who died immediately after birth while his mother was severely ill with measles. Pregnant people who have not been vaccinated against measles nor previously had the infection are susceptible. A case of measles during pregnancy increases the risk of hospitalization, pneumonia, the need for oxygen support or mechanical ventilation, and death. It also increases the risk of miscarriage, stillbirth, low birth weight, and preterm labor. The MMR vaccine is not recommended during pregnancy because it is a live attenuated vaccine and poses a theoretical risk of causing an infection in a fetus. If a pregnant person is exposed to the virus, they’re recommended to receive intravenous immunoglobulin to help protect against infection—but only if it is given within six days of exposure. If there’s a measles infection during pregnancy, the measles virus can pass to a fetus in utero or can transmit during or just after birth, leading to a congenital case. These cases can vary widely in symptoms and severity. Some babies will remain asymptomatic while others develop severe, multisystem disease and death. Some common symptoms of congenital measles include fever and jaundice. They can also develop the telltale measles rash, but it is sometimes delayed or atypical given a newborn’s underdeveloped immune system. Other symptoms can include low platelet counts, pneumonia, and neurologic complications. Even if a newborn survives the acute infection, they are at risk of long-term complications. These include immune amnesia, a type of immunosuppression in which the measles virus destroys immune cells and adaptive responses. They are also at risk of subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE), a progressive, fatal neurological disorder that can strike years after an infection. The alert warns that babies with congenital measles need long-term clinical follow-up and monitoring for these neurologic and immunologic complications, and parents need counseling on how to spot early signs of SSPE, which include seizures, regression in milestones, and academic difficulties in older children.

A Russian military satellite maneuvered in “extreme proximity” of a commercial Earth-imaging satellite in recent days in the first known example an unsanctioned rendezvous in low-Earth orbit, according to the orbital intelligence company LeoLabs. The Russian spacecraft, Kosmos 2612, “conducted a deliberate series of maneuvers toward ICEYE-X36, a US-operated commercial imaging satellite,” LeoLabs said. “Kosmos 2612 closed from hundreds of kilometers away to extreme proximity to ICEYE-X36. It then maneuvered around ICEYE-X36, remained nearby for an extended period, and then began moving away.” US military officials and independent analysts have tracked Kosmos 2612 since its April launch on a Soyuz rocket from a spaceport in northern Russia. Within weeks, it became apparent that at least four of the six satellites from that launch were stalking ICEYE-X36 after they adjusted orbits to line up with the orbit of the ICEYE spacecraft. The situation remained stable until last week, when Kosmos 2612 began rapidly moving toward ICEYE-X36. By Friday, Kosmos 2612 was holding station 2 to 3 kilometers away before shifting positions ahead of and behind the ICEYE satellite, LeoLabs said. The satellites were within 500 meters (1,640 feet) of one another over the weekend, and “likely quite a bit closer than that,” said Adam Marsh, LeoLabs’ vice president of technical solutions. LeoLabs operates a global network of radars to track objects as small as 2 centimeters, about the size of a marble, in low-Earth orbit. The proximity of Kosmos 2612 and ICEYE-X36 “occasionally made it difficult to distinguish the two spacecraft independently,” LeoLabs said. “This was not a routine conjunction or ‘fly by,’” LeoLabs said. “The sequence included a controlled approach, changes in relative position, close-proximity loitering, additional small maneuvers, and a controlled departure. The behavior is consistent with sophisticated rendezvous and proximity operations (RPO).” Satellites often fly near one another in orbit, usually by happenstance and at high closing velocities. Russia has previously deployed satellites in orbits close to the US government’s most sophisticated and secret spy satellites. The difference this time is that Russia’s target was a commercial satellite. Kosmos 2612 got much closer to ICEYE-X36, then stayed a while. The deliberate nature of the Russian maneuvers makes this weekend’s event “strategically important,” LeoLabs said. “To my knowledge, this is the first LEO rendezvous of two active satellites where the owner of one of the sats (satellites) did not give approval,” Jonathan McDowell, an astrophysicist who tracks global launch and satellite activity, wrote on X. “There are parallels that have happened in geosynchronous orbit, but they’re not even close to the same as this,” Marsh told Ars. “As far as this kind of event, rendezvousing in, staying there for a few days, and getting very, very close in low-Earth orbit, this is the first time we’ve ever seen this without consent. We’ve seen them test it with their own satellites before, but not like this.” As of Monday, Kosmos 2612 is moving away from the ICEYE satellite at a relative velocity of about 10 mph (17 kilometers per hour), according to Marsh. ICEYE is a Finnish company with a fleet of high-resolution radar imaging satellites capable of resolving features on Earth’s surface through cloud cover or darkness, which inhibit conventional optical reconnaissance satellites. The company has launched 80 satellites to date, and four more ICEYE satellites are set to launch this week on a SpaceX rideshare mission. The company’s US subsidiary, ICEYE US, owns the ICEYE-X36 satellite. ICEYE satellites provide imagery to numerous military users, including Ukraine, which has used ICEYE’s “near-real-time space-based intelligence” since the Russian invasion in 2022. A Russian government official in 2022 said civilian satellites supporting Ukraine’s military are a “legitimate target.” Taking out a single satellite in a constellation of dozens would have little effect on ICEYE’s capacity to support its customers, which also include the US military and several European defense ministries. “We are aware of the situation and tracking it closely,” an ICEYE US spokesperson told Ars. “Our constellation architecture is designed for continuity and resilience.” Short time-lapse of part of this morning’s first pass of ICEYE-X36 (59103) and COSMOS 2612 (68762) over South America under partly cloudy skies. https://t.co/t8RhwmKcV4 — s2a systems (@s2a_systems) October 18, 2026 Gen. Stephen Whiting, head of US Space Command, said in April that it was “evident” that Russia is operationalizing anti-satellite weapons. US government leaks have also suggested Russia is eyeing the placement of a nuclear weapon in orbit. The US military disclosed last month that it, too, has placed weapons in space, without elaborating. There were no reports of physical damage to the ICEYE-X36 satellite by the time Kosmos 2612 started its retreat Sunday. But getting so close to a US-owned satellite with military utility was a provocation, said retired Lt. Gen. John Shaw, a former deputy commander of US Space Command. “So far, the best interpretation of the Russian activity is clear messaging that they will intentionally conduct aggressive activity near platforms they perceive as actual or potential threats to their interests,” Shaw said. “A broader point is the acknowledgment this activity in the space domain is not at all different from what we often see Russia conduct in the other domains—close-in aggressive and provocative behavior.” Russian Air Force aircraft routinely fly in a buffer zone near US and Canadian airspace, and fighter jets downed a US military drone over the Black Sea in 2023. “If the Russians were willing to do this to an unmanned US military platform, it would seem their threshold for attacking an ‘unmanned’ commercial space platform cannot be too different,” Shaw said.

Batteries help reduce emissions, but how do we reduce their emissions?

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-19

Visit Original Website
The first order of business for the energy transition is electrifying applications like vehicles and heating and making sure that the electricity that powers them is made using renewables. But once the dominant slices of the emissions pie have shrunk, other categories with less potential to reduce emissions become important. A new study led by Haiwei Zhou at the University of Melbourne focused on the emissions associated with making lithium-ion batteries, which many parts of the energy transition rely on. Those emissions aren’t zero, so as we use more and more batteries, that number will grow. Right now, the researchers estimate that these emissions only account for 0.18 percent of annual greenhouse gas releases, but they could increase by a factor of 10 or more by 2050. There are a number of ways to reduce the emissions required to make a battery. Some elements used in various lithium-ion battery chemistries are more energy-intensive to mine and refine than others. The battery manufacturing process involves many steps that could be made more energy-efficient. And then there’s recycling, which can put battery materials back into the loop while requiring much less energy than mining. But the researchers wanted to compare options on the demand side of the equation. Rather than shaving the per-battery carbon footprint, this means reducing the number of batteries we need to make, avoiding their emissions entirely. The team used projections of emissions throughout the life cycle of batteries used for electric vehicles (EVs) and energy storage through 2050. This includes regional differences in demand and electricity generation, using International Energy Agency scenarios for the future. The estimates of emissions savings, then, aren’t just compared to the current state of industries, but to industries in a future world with more batteries, more end-of-life batteries to be recycled, and a cleaner electrical grid. In total, efforts to clean up battery production could reduce the carbon footprint of a given battery by 34 percent by 2050, they found. Between 2024 and 2050, that would avoid somewhere between 3.5 and 4.7 billion tons of CO2. Reducing demand isn’t a “just say no to batteries” story. There are simple strategies that limit the number of batteries you need to make. Ensuring that EV batteries last as long as the average vehicle reduces the need for replacements, cutting battery demand by 29 percent in this analysis. And limiting the ongoing growth in average EV battery capacity—driven by preferences for longer range and larger vehicles—could reduce demand by another 23 percent compared to the current trend. Additionally, adoption of services like ride-sharing could reduce demand by almost 10 percent, while repurposing end-of-life vehicle batteries for energy storage could contribute another 8 percent. Together, all of this would eliminate 6–7.2 billion tons of CO2 between 2024 and 2050—a fair bit more than the supply chain improvements. For context, burning fossil fuels produced about 38 billion tons in 2025. Battery production is a small slice of the emissions pie, but getting to net-zero emissions means tackling many small sources as well as the biggest ones. There is a lot we can do to produce cleaner electricity and to reduce the environmental footprint of batteries. But the battery you don’t make has zero footprint, so the benefits of any reduction in demand add up fast. One Earth, 2026. DOI: 10.1016/j.oneear.2026.101863 (About DOIs).

Recently fixed vulnerabilities in the Plex Media Server are under active exploitation in attacks that, when successful, install malware, including cryptominers and residential proxy apps, a researcher said. Plex fixed four vulnerabilities at the start of last month. Three weeks later, published CVE designations revealed five vulnerabilities, with one of them carrying a severity rating of 8.6 out of 10. A researcher last week revealed an exploit that chained four of them together to achieve remote code execution of the device running a vulnerable Plex version. The researcher named the chain “Plex2Shell.” The new details allowed a separate researcher to create a honeypot that could track attempts in the wild to exploit the vulnerability chain. Within a day, the researcher began seeing active scanning of the simulated Plex server and eventually successful attempts to infect devices. So far, successful attacks are installing two payloads. One is a cryptominer, which uses the device to mine cryptocurrency and deposit it into a wallet controlled by the attacker. The other payload is residential proxy software, which allows people to anonymize their Internet activity (much of it illegal) by connecting to the infected box and then using it to send the user’s traffic to a final destination. “Plex may be hosted on residential IP [sic] and I think threat actors would love to have residential IP proxy to access corporate VPNs and emails,” the second researcher wrote. The published exploit chain was part of a proof-of-concept attack that the first researcher included when submitting a bug report to Plex. The complex code completes a series of steps, including performing an unauthenticated SSRF, a loopback pivot, a security token theft, a payload delivery, and finally the execution of the payload. The bug finder said he used AI “heavily” to develop the exploit chain. Steps included: “This was not a matter of telling the LLM ‘go hack Plex,’” the researcher wrote. “It was hours of iterative work, dead ends, and reprioritization. All of the judgment calls on what to look for and what to prioritize were mine. The LLM speeds this process up significantly, allowing me to stay at a higher level and simply guide the process.” Plex representatives didn’t immediately respond to questions. Anyone using Plex should ensure it’s running version 1.43.3. The software can be configured to install updates automatically, or users can manually download and install them. Given the active exploitation, outdated versions should be updated immediately. Beyond that, users should devote care to properly securing the media servers. The safest option is to restrict its access to local networks with no exposure to the Internet. This option prevents being able to use many useful features, though. One option when connecting a device to the open Internet is to route traffic through a reverse proxy, instead of using the default port 32400. Users should also use only secure Plex apps. And of course, strong passwords are a must.

Good morning. Should we celebrate billionaires donating shares in their companies? Elon Musk announced on X yesterday that he’s going to give every kid under 18 in the U.S. a share of SpaceX next year. At $147.96 per share, that’s about $10.7 billion worth of stock. (SpaceX President Gwynne Shotwell pledged last week to give more than 2 million kids a share of her stock through a Trump Account.) For founders sitting atop a mountain of stock, that beats selling shares, paying tax, and then giving away the cash. (Recipients will pay income tax when the stock is sold.) But there are downsides to letting donors gift their shares. Leaders may donate stock to curry favor in certain districts. Pietro Pollo and Michael Kasumovic of UNSW explore the external factors that can often dictate which species and organisms are studied and which are ignored. A version of this article was originally published by The Conversation (CC BY-ND 4.0) Our knowledge about life on Earth is built through research on how organisms function, evolve and interact with each other and the environment. Yet there are far more species on our planet than scientists can study in detail. So how should scientists decide which species to focus on? Fairly representing all species seems like a useful approach. It’s like predicting an election result – we can’t interview every citizen, so we need a sample that truly represents the overall population. What drives scientists to uncover nature’s mysteries is curiosity. So, the organisms chosen for research are determined by what makes scientists most curious. And that’s causing a problem with fair representation. In our new research, we examined research in ecology and evolution (an umbrella term for biological sciences outside biomedicine) to understand which animals are most studied and why. Our results suggest emotion plays a bigger role in research choices than scientists usually admit. Our world is mostly filled with invertebrates: creatures such as bugs, spiders, worms and slugs. Vertebrates (amphibians, birds, fish, mammals and reptiles) make up only 5pc of all animal species on Earth. So, if scientists want to represent animals fairly in their work, most research should be on invertebrates. To understand how scientists’ time is spent researching the diversity of animals, we analysed almost 1.4m scientific articles from more than 600 journals. Unsurprisingly, we found that more than half of these articles focused on vertebrates, demonstrating a huge bias in what scientists prefer to study. Our results also demonstrate things haven’t improved in more than two decades (when this bias was first discovered), and in some cases have gotten worse. For example, the focus on fish in marine and freshwater biology research increased from 35.8pc of articles in 2001 to 49.7pc in 2025. This was all at the expense of the largest group of marine creatures, aquatic invertebrates such as molluscs, crustaceans, corals and more. The bias we found was compounded by social media and news coverage, where popular animal groups attracted more attention from other scientists and the general public. Many scientists are therefore no different to everyone else in which animals they deem interesting or important. Although many factors determine which research organisms scientists might select, emotions do play a significant role. Using an online survey, we asked 868 animal behaviour researchers about their experiences and preferences. We found that popular groups in animal behaviour research, such as birds and mammals, were regarded more favourably than others, especially arachnids (such as spiders). This is perhaps unsurprising, as perceptions of animals are shaped early in childhood. Ingrained societal values, such as which animals are cute, tend to persist by the time someone becomes a scientist. Fortunately, we found that familiarity breeds admiration. Participants that worked with a certain animal group tended to view that group more positively after their experience. In other words, they tried it and liked it. Studying the saliva of the Gila monster (a lizard) in the 1980s paved the way for recently developed weight loss drugs such as Ozempic. Research on snake, spider and scorpion toxins is being used for new pain-relieving drugs. The discovery of green fluorescent proteins from bioluminescent jellyfish in the 1960s led to the 2008 Nobel Prize because it sparked a scientific revolution by making it possible to tag individual cells. These examples show that scientific advancement often requires serendipity and time. It disregards whether the organisms involved are charismatic. Creatures commonly seen as ugly, disgusting, or dangerous (which they rarely are) can be as, if not more, useful for society as fluffy, pretty animals. Our work indicates that society’s negative perceptions of certain organisms, which are often rooted in disconnection from nature and misinformation, are shared by the very scientists responsible for understanding biological processes and patterns. Understanding scientists’ choices matters because these decisions shape the direction of scientific progress. We urgently need to shift our perception of animals because human-made effects are accelerating biodiversity losses. For example, insects are declining at unprecedented rates, but we know little (if anything) about most insect species. Many haven’t even been described and may go extinct before we even know they existed. This means we’ll lose not just the benefits they bring to ecosystems, but any benefits they may have held for humanity. There is hope. Our research shows that when people spend more time with the ‘ugly’ and ‘scary’ animals, they often appreciate them more. Once people realise that all organisms (even spiders) play a vital part in ecosystems, it becomes a lot easier to give these overlooked creatures the attention they deserve. Organisations like Invertebrates Australia and many social media influencers are already helping by showing just how fascinating and important invertebrates really are. But we still need more support to reduce research biases, especially from governments and universities. They can make a huge difference by funding research on never-before-studied species and neglected animal groups. If nothing changes, we risk slowing progress because we’re drawn to what looks cute. Next time an animal video pops up on your feed, try sharing something beyond the fluffy favourites. The less glamorous species are just as essential and just as worthy of our curiosity. Fears of an AI bubble have been growing for years. And now the International Monetary Fund is the latest to sound the alarm. In its Global Financial Stability Report published last week, the global financial institution warned that markets everywhere are increasingly at the mercy of precarious AI investments. The untold billions poured into AI has driven economic growth, the IMF says, but the boom could quickly invert if profits, which so far have largely proven elusive, never come to fruition — or at least not proportionately with the epic investment levels. AI stocks would tank, and the resulting market correction would send powerful shocks across the global economy. “Growing leverage could be a catalyst for such a correction,” the IMF warned in a blog post about its findings. “Hyperscalers, as the largest data center operators are known, are borrowing more and more, while investors have been leveraging to invest in AI stocks.” The report also casts a wary eye to the circular nature of AI financing, in which the biggest benefactors and beneficiaries of AI companies are other AI companies. Nvidia, for example, agreed to invest $30 billion in OpenAI and provide another $105 billion towards an OpenAI data center; OpenAI is one of Nvidia’s biggest customers, relying on its chips to power and train its AI models, so a lot of those billions will go straight back to Nvidia when its buys more chips. Besides creating a mirage of robustness, it also means that their financial health is inextricably tangled with each other. “Stress at key companies receiving investment could lower revenue forecasts and investment valuations, which in turn would weaken the value of the accumulated debt in those deals,” the IMF wrote. “Our review of 14 such transactions since 2023 found about $300 billion in equity investments and $650 billion in debt, in exchange for $1.4 trillion in future revenue commitments.” A disastrous AI-driven market correction isn’t the only economic catastrophe potentially looming. The IMF is also worried about an increase in government bond yields, or interest rates, or a potential major cyberattack targeting critical government and financial institutions — a strike that could feasibly be assisted by powerful AI models. The IMF is far from the only one to caution about an AI bubble, but as the stewards of the global economy, theirs is one of the weightiest warnings yet. Fears were freshly stoked again earlier this month when it was reported that OpenAI’s annualized revenue was $20 billion lower than anticipated, dampening hopes that it would soon be on the road to profitability. The conventional wisdom is that AI systems, with their tens of billions of parameters, are effectively indecipherable: the exact mechanics of how their basic building blocks of nodes or “neurons” function are an incomprehensible “black box,” even to the researchers who create them. But a new essay from AI researcher Nick Cammarata has stunned Silicon Valley by arguing the opposite. AI models aren’t impenetrable black boxes, he claims, and in fact there may be a way for us to see how they actually “think.” The nearly 26,000 word spiel was praised by figures across the industry, including OpenAI CEO Sam Altman. A prominent researcher who works at OpenAI called it a “major moment in scientific history.” It’s clear why they’re so compelled. In the eyes of AI leadership, it means that there may be a way of “solving” AI alignment. If we can understand how AI models think, the thinking goes, then we can figure out why AI agents go “rogue” and embark on hacking sprees, or why chatbots break their guardrails and say something dangerous — and put a stop to it. (It was “news so good even eliezer will have to lower his pdoom,” one commenter joked, referring to Eliezer Yudkowsky, an infamous AI “doomer.”) That’s all if Cammarata, a former OpenAI employee who now works at an AI startup called Goodfire, is correct. In his essay, he explains how for years, the way AI neurons responded to specific stimuli was “almost fundamentally uninterpretable.” Why the same neuron lit up in response to a picture of a cat or a car was unknown. The leading explanation was that neurons exhibited superposition: since AI models have a limited number of neurons, they stop “grouping related things together and instead begins folding unrelated things together, simply because they don’t interfere,” Cammarata explained. “The result is a kind of knot: the network could have folded in many almost equally good ways, so which things a neuron groups together tells you almost nothing,” he wrote. But the reality, he says, is that neurons aren’t “tangled knots” but are instead “clean.” In one example, the same neuron will light up to a picture of a cat face and the front of a car, which at first appear to be two unrelated concepts. But when you break it down, Cammarata says, the AI is actually seeing something we’re not: the same V-shape pattern in the images. “It’s not a cat and car neuron, it’s a V-shape neuron,” he wrote. In sum, he argues, AI models are more legible than ever previously thought. Not everyone agrees, at least fully, with Cammarata’s optimistic conclusions. “This is interesting work, but I don’t think it’s a significant update to the big picture of interpretability,” Neel Nanda, a Google DeepMind researcher who leads its mechanistic interpretability efforts, wrote on X. “I still think superposition is real and this work doesn’t really affect my cruxes.” Beyond that, it’s unclear if understanding what each neuron does amounts to being able to predict how they all work together. It begins to sound like a convenient technical solution to a much grander problem. Ensuring that AI models are safe goes beyond building guardrails — and that’s without getting into the entire legal and social frameworks that need to be in place, too. Still, based on the strong response Cammarata’s work’s elicited, it could well be the case that it meaningfully reshapes how AI’s inner mechanisms are understood. Estonia-based Blackwall, which builds web traffic security tools for hosting providers, said on Tuesday that it has raised $100 million at a $2.2 billion valuation. It builds products that helps websites grapple with the skyrocketing volume of bot traffic, especially AI agents. The Series C was led by AVP, with participation from existing investors Dawn Capital, MMC Ventures and Tera Ventures. Blackwall’s executive chairman, Roi Carthy, told TechCrunch this funding round increased the company’s valuation by 4 times since its previous fundraise in 2025. Over the past few years, web infrastructure companies like Cloudflare, Akamai, Imperva and Blackwall have found themselves in an increasingly valuable but difficult space: They’re on the frontlines, dealing with DDoS attacks, indiscriminate scraping, botnet farms, malicious requests, and these days, even swarms of AI agents going rogue. But it’s also a chance to make a killing by helping hosting providers and websites deal with all the potentially unwanted traffic. Blackwall, however, isn’t interested in competing with the bigwigs in its space. Instead of selling its platform directly to websites, the company uses a “channel” approach, working with web hosting, managed service and cloud providers to understand their needs, and then handing over a bundle of tools which they can customize. Those providers can then offer Blackwall’s security tools to their own clients under their own branding and retain control of that relationship. There’s money in it for both sides. Co-founder and CEO Nikita Rozenberg told TechCrunch that hosting companies can mix and match security components to build their own solution according to what they want to provide to their end customers, set the prices, and share a cut of the revenue with Blackwall. The company initially gives its customers a free tier showing the traffic and threats hitting their network, but charges for any protection or tools to address the threats. In addition to providing services like threat intelligence, certificate management, DDoS protection and traffic routing, Blackwall examines its customers’ web traffic to differentiate actual visitors and legit AI agents from malicious bots, intruders, and disruptive traffic. Rozenberg said the company uses techniques like digital fingerprinting to identify the software sending a request — for example, Microsoft Office or Codex — to filter out bots. In addition, the company uses its own AI models at customers’ endpoints to analyze the intent and behavior of the requests coming in to differentiate agents sent by a person for a real request versus a malicious bot. In the coming years, Rozenberg sees a big opportunity emerging as AI agents begin interacting and transacting with websites on behalf of their users. This should eventually require systems to manage which agents get access, and on what terms, and how they are authorized to make payments. Blackwall isn’t alone in eyeing that opportunity. Cloudflare’s AI Crawl Control lets website owners allow or block individual AI crawlers, and there’s also an option to charge them for access. But Rozenberg argues that Blackwall’s advantage lies in letting hosting providers offer such traffic management services themselves instead of handing the customer relationship to another security provider. Blackwall plans to use the funding to expand its operations across North America, Europe, Asia-Pacific and the Middle East, and develop more security tooling for hosting, cloud and managed service providers. Flipkart, the Walmart-owned Indian e-commerce giant, is preparing to challenge India’s food-delivery duopoly of Zomato and Swiggy. It is entering a fiercely competitive market that has defeated even previous attempts by Amazon and Google to break in. The Bengaluru-based company is testing its food-delivery service internally and has expanded the trial from a few hundred employees to upwards of 8,000, according to two people familiar with the matter. It has also deployed a dedicated fleet of riders through its logistics arm, Ekart, separate from the riders who handle its quick-commerce deliveries, as it prepares for a public launch. The employee-only service appears as a separate tab on Flipkart’s main shopping app, alongside its Minutes quick-commerce offering and travel bookings, TechCrunch has learned. It draws on restaurants listed through ONDC, a government-backed network that connects online buyers and sellers across different platforms, as well as restaurants that Flipkart signed up directly. Riders wearing jerseys bearing the “Eat-in by Minutes” branding have also begun fulfilling orders on the streets of Bengaluru, the southern Indian city where Flipkart is headquartered. As Flipkart expands its food-delivery operations, it is entering a market that the brokerage firm Bernstein estimates is growing 18% to 20% a year. Online delivery, though, still accounts for just over 15% of India’s broader food-services industry. Zomato, owned by Eternal, and Swiggy together serve an estimated 85 million to 95 million paying customers a year, per Bernstein. That figure shows how dominant the two are, and how much room newcomers have to grow online food ordering in the world’s most populous country, which has more than a billion internet subscriptions. Flipkart is not the only company trying to shake up India’s food-delivery market. Ride-hailing company Rapido, which has already given Uber and its Indian rival Ola a tough fight, has been expanding Ownly, its food-delivery service that charges restaurants zero commission. Meanwhile, Swiggy has launched Toing, a separate app aimed at customers looking for cheaper meals. Both moves reflect growing pressure on the industry’s existing pricing model and the commissions that restaurants pay. “Monopoly is bad, but duopoly is more pathetic,” said Sagar Daryani, president of the New Delhi-headquartered National Restaurant Association of India, which represents over 500,000 restaurants nationwide. “What one does, the other follows suit.” Daryani argued that restaurants have long needed alternatives to the two dominant platforms. Flipkart is not the first deep-pocketed company to try breaking into India’s food-delivery market. In 2020, Amazon launched a food-ordering service in Bengaluru, but it shut the service down two years later. Google’s Areo app, which offered food ordering alongside other services, also failed to gain traction. Uber also left the market in 2020, selling its Uber Eats business in India to Zomato. Daryani sees one important difference between Flipkart and the companies that tried food delivery before it. Zomato and Swiggy built their businesses around delivering meals before expanding into quick commerce. Flipkart is doing the reverse, using its established e-commerce business and growing quick-commerce operations as a springboard into food delivery. “Flipkart’s quick commerce benefit will get passed on to food delivery, so it will be leaner, more efficient,” Daryani told TechCrunch. Restaurants are also hoping that new entrants will bring down the cost of selling meals online. Daryani, also co-founder and CEO of the dumpling chain Wow! Momo Foods, said Flipkart has proposed a base commission of about 10%, compared with the 20% to 25% that restaurants typically pay Zomato and Swiggy. Commissions are only part of the expense, though. Once advertising, discounts, and other promotional costs are factored in, restaurants can end up spending 40% to 50% of an order’s value on the established platforms, Daryani told TechCrunch. He expects Flipkart to keep that total cost closer to 15% to 16%, which could mean restaurants keep more money from each order. Nonetheless, getting restaurants on board is one thing and winning over customers is another. Bernstein estimates that new entrants could save customers just ₹20 to ₹30 (around 20–30 cents) per order by operating more efficiently, which may not be enough to draw customers away from Zomato and Swiggy. The two incumbents could also offer deeper discounts to keep them. Flipkart is not planning to stay in Bengaluru for long. The company has told restaurants that it expects to launch its food-delivery service publicly by the end of October and to expand to five or six cities by the end of this year, Daryani told TechCrunch. The food-delivery push also comes as Flipkart’s long-awaited stock-market listing remains uncertain. Chief Executive Kalyan Krishnamurthy (pictured above) recently declined to give a timetable for an IPO, even as the company continues to expand into businesses that could bring customers back to its app more frequently. Flipkart’s expansion comes as competition in India’s e-commerce market intensifies. Quick-commerce platforms that started by delivering groceries in minutes now sell even the latest iPhones and other discretionary purchases, putting them in more direct competition with Flipkart and Amazon. AI labs have staked their reputations on model benchmarking — the supposedly neutral and accurate system of evaluations that are designed to measure whether one AI product is better than another. However, the methods behind these evaluations are complicated and still maturing, said Irene Solaiman, Hugging Face’s chief policy officer, during a discussion at TechCrunch Disrupt last week. Testing needs to come together into a more consistent science if the industry is to rely on them in the future. With that in mind, can we trust the current tests that are being released? “Trust is such a weighted term,” said Solaiman. “In terms of whether we can trust results, one big part [of that] is can you reproduce the results of an evaluation? Do you have the level of access that you need to the model itself — this is where open weights come in in a big way,” she added. Reproducibility in model evaluations has long remained one of the stickier aspects of benchmarking — and remains a topic of significant debate in the industry. Outside of an individual test’s validity, there’s also the added question of how that test compares to other evaluations. “They are an evolving science — where we don’t know how to meaningfully compare them to each other,” Solaiman said. “They start to get ineffective relatively quickly, and we don’t have as much of a thriving ecosystem as we need,” she added. “There’s this hunger for an embedded evaluator ecosystem now, but the qualifications that determine who is an effective evaluator across what tasks need a lot more standardization.” The idea of embedded evaluators — in which supposedly “neutral” third-party observers are placed inside AI organizations — has been popularized over the last several months, as AI labs grapple with a rash of safety incidents that have spurred concerns over the potential harms of their technology. Deep vein thrombosis, or DVT, occurs when a clot forms in a vein deep inside the body, often in the leg. Those veins carry blood back toward the heart. They’re different from superficial blood clots——Hurts’ original diagnosis—which form closer to the skin, explains Dr. Geoffrey Barnes, a cardiologist and vascular medicine specialist at University of Michigan Health. DVT often causes pain, swelling, and redness or a darker skin color. Patients commonly tell Barnes, “My entire calf and ankle are swollen.” One leg might look noticeably different from the other. An injury can make those symptoms easier to dismiss. “If you’re worried that you have a DVT, it’s important that you get medical care quickly,” Barnes says. Unexplained shortness of breath, chest pain, coughing up blood, or fainting require emergency care because they can signal a pulmonary embolism. A dry cough without a cold, pain when taking a deep breath, or a racing heart can also be clues, says Dr. Stephan Moll, a hematologist and professor of medicine at the University of North Carolina who runs an Athletes and Blood Clots Clinic. The dispute arises from the Veterans’ Judicial Review Act of 1988 and the Supreme Court’s decision in Johnson v. Robison. I tried a $99 plug that promises to track your car and send help when needed — without selling your data

The Miles Plug adds crash detection, roadside help, location tracking, and a warning-light decoder to your car for as low as $11 a month.﻿ My 15-year-old daughter just got her learner’s permit, so when I was pitched a new gadget tailored to teen drivers, I was intrigued. The Miles Plug is a small device that plugs into your car’s OBD-II port and monitors location, driving habits, and vehicle health, along with emergency accident response and roadside assistance. The idea is that knowing all these things should help me feel more comfortable when my daughter starts driving alone. I tested the (now discontinued) Ring Car Cam when my son was learning to drive, but he hated it and refused to turn it on; I wondered if the Miles could provide the same peace of mind without feeling as intrusive. Billed as “a wearable for your car,” Miles is a tiny cellular and GPS-enabled device that connects to an app to deliver real-time location, alerts, and driving insights (speeding, hard braking, trip recaps). The app also features an AI chatbot called the Miles Mechanic that decodes warning lights, tells you how urgent a fix is, and estimates repair costs. Miles was developed by cofounders Matt McGovren and Kit Klein, who have a solid smart home pedigree. They worked at Wink in the early days and bring combined experience from Amazon (Ring and Blink), Ubiquiti’s smart home line, and other companies. “We built Miles to support teens and parents together, and keep them on the same team,” said McGovren in a press release. “Parents and teens see the same trips and the same alerts with nothing hidden, building trust instead of suspicion.” You’re the customer, you’re not the product. Your data is not the product. Miles is available today at mileslabs.com and costs $99, plus $180 for a year of membership ($279 total, or $15 a month). A three-year plan is $396 with the Plug included ($11 a month). All plans are paid upfront with no auto-renewal. Anyone in the family can download and use the app, with each driver getting their own profile and alerts. Miles says it’s compatible with most gas and hybrid cars from 2008 onward and some EVs (there’s a compatibility checker). While the prices seem steep, McGovren’s pitch is that it covers several services you might already be paying for: “You could piece together a roadside service, a family tracking service … and then you’re looking at $30, $40 a month,” he said in an interview with The Verge. And, unlike your car’s own branded app, Miles isn’t selling your data. Automakers have been caught collecting and selling driving data, with GM being banned from selling data to brokers for five years. McGovren says Miles will never sell vehicle, trip, or location data, and the app has no ads: “You’re the customer, you’re not the product. Your data is not the product.” He says, “All the vehicle and trip data is encrypted,” adding that “the most sensitive data is end-to-end encrypted.” Additionally, the location data is tied to the car, not the phone, so it stops tracking when you walk away. The app lets you download your data, stop location collection, and delete your account and all its data. I set up Miles in my 2014 Toyota Highlander I nicknamed Tanya and have been using it for a couple of days. Setup was simple, and the app is clean, uncluttered, and easy to follow. It took me through a few setup screens, including asking to share my phone’s location to relay data from the Plug over Bluetooth when cell service is spotty. I plugged it into my OBD-II port, and it read my VIN and correctly identified my car. It logs every trip with route, distance, time, and driver initials (I haven’t had my daughter download the app yet; I’m interested to see whose phones it connects to when we’re both in the car). Arrived- and left-home alerts came through promptly, and the events tab flagged hard acceleration and a cluster of three hard-braking alerts when I was running a little late to collect my daughter from work. I like the home card, which shows a vehicle health summary: engine OK, fuel 63%, battery 13.5V, and the odometer reading. I played with the Miles Mechanic chatbot and asked how my car is and what upcoming maintenance is due; it answered in plain English, telling me the cabin air filter is due at about 140,000 miles, and the oil change and tire rotation at 142,500. I haven’t been testing it long enough to comment on battery drain, an issue with earlier products in this space. “It goes to sleep when your car is not driving or not on,” says McGovren. “We use a little bit of data to wake up and sync, but [that] doesn’t have a material impact on the battery.” He advises removing it if you won’t be driving the car for a while. Beyond car battery life, an obvious concern for prospective users is how long Miles the company will last. Startup Automatic had a similar idea back in 2016, launching the Automatic Pro for $130 that plugged into your OBD-II port, tracked mileage and location, and monitored for crashes over a 3G radio. The service shut down in 2020. Miles is basically Automatic but with a business model; Automatic didn’t initially charge a monthly fee, Miles does. McGovren says he and his team have seen a lot of “scar tissue from the smart home,” with companies that “thought they could make money later.” Both founders were at Wink during its outage, bankruptcy, and eventual sale to will.i.am’s company. Charging for membership upfront is meant to avoid that type of outcome. He says the company is “well capitalized” and offers a 60-day money-back guarantee to users. The car becomes part of this bigger ecosystem. We aren’t just trying to tie you into our app. Automatic also had several smart-home tie-ins, but at launch Miles doesn’t connect to your smart home. McGovren says they have an MCP server in beta that could deliver “all of this information … to your AI of choice, your home assistant of choice,” he says. “And now the car becomes part of this bigger ecosystem. We aren’t just trying to tie you into our app.” He envisions a future where you own your car’s data and can connect it to any service you want, and be able to ask Alexa, Siri, or Google, “When’s my next oil change?” or “How much gas is in my car?” Miles is pitched as a solution for monitoring your teen driver, including notifications of possible crashes and risky driving, without also having to monitor their phone. But it also feels like a good solution for anyone with an older car or who doesn’t want to rely on data-tracking apps from insurance companies or car manufacturers but wants easy access to their car’s data. I plan to test it more thoroughly, hopefully try out the MCP integration, and follow up with a full review. How to study a swiftly melting climate disaster

MIT Technology Review | 2026-10-20

Andrew Haverdink, a tall grad student in a rain bib and rubber boots, wrenched a double-handed drill from the frozen soil that underlies much of Alaska’s Denali National Park and then unclipped the hollow barrel from the end of the bit. He knelt in front of a blue tarp, lined the barrel up with a plastic tray, and pushed a rod through the opposite end. A perfect solid, cylindrical core of ancient soil emerged—dark, rich permafrost at least a foot long. On that late morning in early July, Haverdink was among a team of Northern Arizona University students and staff gathering samples and data from a research plot, divided by surveyor’s tape unspooled atop tussocks, alders, and fireweed. In late June of 2013, a lightning strike sparked a fire in this stretch of mostly treeless tundra, in the northern backcountry of the park. Whipped by the Chinooks, intense downslope winds created as moist ocean air surmounts and then descends the Alaska Range, the fire ripped across nearly 35,000 acres. It burned through much of the vegetation and deep into the topsoil, stripping away the insulating layer that preserves the frozen permafrost, a massive storehouse of carbon that underlies 85% of Alaska. During their first survey, in 2016, the researchers found that in places where the fire burned hottest and deepest, the thawed soil above the permafrost (known as the active layer) extended between a foot and nearly two feet deeper than in plots outside the burn scar—an indication of deep thawing. Crucially, the fire also crossed two sets of survey grids that the National Park Service established in 2001, including the one where the researchers stood that morning. That pre-fire snapshot offered the Northern Arizona group a rare opportunity to study how the blaze has altered the vegetation, carbon, and permafrost across time, says Xanthe Walker, one of the principal investigators on the project, who was watching over Haverdink’s work that morning. These direct observations from high-latitude fires are helping scientists to better understand the impacts of wildfire and permafrost loss on climate change, she says. “There’s a lot of uncertainty,” Walker says. “These are huge biomes with massive fires that no one’s ever been to or ever looked at.” They’re among a growing group of researchers striving to untangle what happens in the aftermath of fires in the Arctic-boreal region as they occur more often, burn hotter than before, and expose the landscape and frozen permafrost to a warming climate. The work is providing critical clues into the complex mechanisms driving cascading climate feedback effects: As warming temperatures fuel more frequent and severe fires, more permafrost thaws, releasing greenhouse-gas emissions that threaten to fuel more warming, fires, and permafrost loss. Understanding just how powerful these effects are, how they compound, and to what degree other transformations of the landscape may mitigate them is essential to gain a clearer picture of our climate future. Notably, past modeling suggested that the carbon dioxide uptake from expanding trees and plants may largely offset the release of carbon from warming soils and melting permafrost, at least over long periods and under moderate levels of warming. But the improving observations and analyses have revealed worrisome signs about the impacts of rising Arctic-boreal emissions in the immediate decades ahead, as the world struggles to keep global warming within relatively safe boundaries. Permafrost makes up 15% of the land surface of the Northern Hemisphere and holds about 1.5 trillion tons of carbon, roughly twice the amount in the atmosphere. Most of it formed during the last ice age, but it’s been thawing at an accelerating rate during the last half-century, amid rising temperatures and worsening fires. As it melts, microbes begin to break down that organic matter, releasing it into the air in the form of carbon dioxide and methane. Both are powerful greenhouse gases that fuel climate change. The impacts of permafrost loss, however, aren’t as straightforward as they may initially seem. Notably, there has been a long-running scientific debate over what would have the strongest effect on the exchange of greenhouse gases over the next few centuries: the loss of carbon in soil and permafrost or the carbon uptake from so-called “Arctic greening,” which describes the spread of carbon-absorbing plants across the landscape as temperatures rise. Thawing permafrost itself can contribute to the latter phenomenon as the unfrozen soil layer deepens and makes more nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients accessible to plants. Carbon-sucking trees and shrubs with deeper root systems can take hold and thrive in places where they wouldn’t have been able to in the past. But as more Arctic-boreal fires ignite and more permafrost melts, researchers have been able to closely study what exactly happens in the aftermath, using on-site surveys like those conducted by the Northern Arizona University team, satellite observations, lidar flights, a widening network of greenhouse-gas-measuring towers, and other tools. The findings have alarmed many researchers. In particular, permafrost seems particularly prone to abrupt thawing rather than the gradual version that major climate models have simulated. A 2020 paper found that this could release far more carbon than previously understood, doubling the emissions from thawing permafrost and offsetting the carbon uptake from future plant growth. In addition, even if carbon levels eventually net out through Arctic greening, the emissions from high-latitude fires and permafrost loss appear to be accelerating warming right now. Susan Natali, an Arctic climate scientist at the Woodwell Climate Research Center, and her colleagues have published several papers in recent years concluding that the combined emissions from these feedback effects will push the planet past 2 °C faster than currently predicted. She and other researchers  also found that about 40% of the Arctic-boreal zone has already flipped from a carbon sink into a carbon source, when the emissions from permafrost melting, fires and other forces are taken into account. “The more we learn about processes like abrupt thaw, deeper-burning fires, and cold-season emissions, and the more we see disturbances increasing, the more likely it is that the sink will weaken and source areas will expand,” Natali said in an email. But there’s still considerable uncertainty about the differences between the ways fires and thawing permafrost might alter landscapes, under different circumstances in different biomes. “This is just a challenge of the science, because it's really hard to measure such a huge area, which is not one system,” she says. “It’s many, many, many, many different types of ecosystems.” The Northern Arizona University researchers have been at the forefront of efforts to study the aftermath of intensifying fires across different parts of Alaska. Over the last two decades, Michelle Mack, a professor of ecosystem ecology who was previously Walker’s postdoctoral advisor, has overseen repeat survey work on fire sites across forests and tundras, from the relatively warm southern interior to the frigid North Slope. Her husband and frequent collaborator, Ted Schuur, is also a professor at the university and oversees several permafrost monitoring and experimental research sites at the Eight Mile Lake study area, just north of Denali National Park.If there’s a single takeaway from the body of science that the Northern Arizona scientists have produced, it’s this: It’s complicated. The local climate and ecosystem, the severity of the fires that have occurred, the type of permafrost, and numerous other factors can substantially shift the uptake or loss of carbon, the amount of permafrost that’s thawed, the extent to which it may recover, and the future flammability of the landscape. In one early study, the researchers and their collaborators surveyed dozens of stands dominated by black spruce trees in the aftermath of a series of large boreal fires in 2004, between the Brooks Range and the Alaska Range. One of the notable findings: Severe burns that left little behind of the decaying organic matter in the topsoil induced a shift toward faster-growing and less flammable deciduous trees. The sites where deciduous trees dominated stored about five times as much soil carbon as stands that returned to black spruce, Mack, Walker, and their coauthors noted in a 2021 Science paper. That means the subsequent uptake of carbon on those stands “more than compensated” for the amount released in the initial fire, Walker says. But the 2007 Anaktuvuk River tundra fire, which remains the largest fire on record in Alaska’s North Slope, offers a starkly different case study. In the initial analysis there, Mack, Schuur, and their coauthors found that the fire released more than 2 million tons of carbon to the atmosphere, basically negating the amount that tundra ecosystems around the world removed from the air that year. Subsequent studies found that more than a third of the scorched landscape sank, slid, and crumpled as the ice wedges below collapsed, the telltale signs of abrupt permafrost melting. Nearly two decades later, much of that thawed, decaying organic matter continues to release carbon dioxide and methane in the most severely burned patches of the fire scar, says Adrian Rocha, an associate professor at the University of Notre Dame. It will take decades to centuries to recover all the carbon lost there, if ever happens at all, according to Rocha, who continues to monitor the site using Eddy covariance flux towers, which measure the exchange of greenhouse gases between Earth and atmosphere. Mack says these on-the-ground measurements are critical to understanding what sorts of ecosystems recover, which shift into new states, whether they release a lot of carbon or a little, and when and where plant growth can offset that.That said, the stakes of thawing permafrost are clear. “If we step back as ecosystem ecologists, we know that there’s a lot more carbon stored below ground in permafrost than there is above ground in vegetation,” she says. “If permafrost carbon is released, it could swamp all of these other interesting details.” The Northern Arizona University research team shipped a pallet of soil samples back from Denali. The material included plugs of moss and decaying plant matter known as “brownies,” as well as permafrost cores and samples of the unfrozen soil layer. In the months since, they’ve cut, dried, weighed, combusted and analyzed various samples and inputted their field and lab findings into databases. Walker says they’re exploring a variety of research questions, including how much that top organic layer has recovered since the initial post-fire survey and how much carbon has been lost and recovered since the fire. The analysis is still underway, but in the most severely burned sites, it appears that the thawed layer of soil above the permafrost remain deeper some 13 years. “Thaw still reaches further into the soil each summer than it probably did before the fire, which exposes more soil to decomposition,” Walker explained in an email. They’re now hoping to use lab analyses to determine how much of that carbon is being lost. On my last afternoon in Healy, Alaska, I followed Schuur along a narrow walkway of wooden boards suspended above a tundra field just north of Denali, hoping to get a glimpse into our climate future. In 2008, he and his research team devised a way to subject permafrost to the temperature levels expected two or three decades from now by setting up snow fences around dozens of designated research plots. That allowed extra snow to build up—which, counterintuitively, acts like a blanket, adding thermal insulation during the winter that kept the test plots warmer than the controls. Over the course of the 13-year experiment, the soil carbon levels in those 10-by-10-meter plots declined by 23%, Schuur and his coauthors found. The land collapsed in many of the sites, forming craters several feet deep. As I stepped heel-to-toe along the planks into the middle of one plot, I descended about four feet, experiencing subsidence in three dimensions. Looking up at the edge from the walkway, I felt as if I were diaphragm deep in an empty pool. Schuur, though, had another thing to show me. As we crested a ridge between plots, he pointed down to a shallower—though no less obvious—pool on the other side. It was a control site that had melted and collapsed on its own, without any assistance from simulated warming or fires. “Every plot is just going off in its own trajectory,” he said. “This stuff is happening because the whole place is warming … and all these changes just go faster if you first burn the surface.” Schuur expects that the permafrost underlying the whole field will thaw deeply by the end of 2040. “There won’t be any surface permafrost based on our current warming,” he says. Unless the world radically cuts emissions and gets fires under control, this same phenomenon will creep up the state, decade by decade, until even the permafrost under Alaska’s North Slope sinks to similar depths and gives up the carbon it has held for millennia. Generative AI models run on powerful chips known as GPUs, which depend on stacks of specialized memory chips packaged with the processor. To meet growing demand for memory, Samsung is building a massive new semiconductor manufacturing complex in Pyeongtaek, South Korea. Three fabs already operate around the clock, and a fourth is coming online. Construction of a fifth was halted during a drop in demand for memory chips in 2024 but restarted when the AI boom sent demand soaring. In the fall of 2025, Jeff Dodge set up a six-foot folding table in the cafeteria of Cane Bay High School in Summerville, South Carolina, to conduct a taste test. When the first rush of about 150 hungry students hit, he was waiting with slow cookers filled with steaming marinara sauce and meatballs. Kids love a taste test, but they’re also discerning, and Dodge wasn’t sure what they would make of his product, a blend featuring both meat and mushrooms. As students swarmed the table and tapped out their opinions on an iPad or scanned a QR code to use their phones, Dodge asked those who lingered to guess what kind of vegetable might have been mixed into the meat. Few of them could—the meatballs had tasted like moist, flavorful beef. The school enrolls a few thousand students, and Dodge estimates that the majority of them came by. In the end, the survey showed that 92% of those who tried the meatballs wanted them on the menu; they easily cleared the bar to be added for the 2026–2027 school year. Comments described their flavor as “bussin’.” Through his consulting company, K–12 Food Solutions, Dodge was testing a new product called 50/Cut from the company Joyn. The proprietary blend of seemingly simple ingredients—available as meatballs, burgers, and ground meat—could help solve one of the most intractable challenges of the climate crisis, at least on the individual level: getting people to eat less meat, particularly beef. Livestock production accounts for somewhere between 10% and 20% of all greenhouse-gas emissions related to human activity, and nearly two-thirds of that comes from ruminants like cows raised for meat and dairy products. Beef herds create four times the emissions of dairy herds per kilo of food produced. And for US households, food makes up as much as 30% of emissions. Unlike other polluting sectors, such as electricity and transportation, cattle farming has little potential for a big fix. Ruminants produce about 30% of the methane associated with human activity, and this gas has 28 times more global-­warming potential than carbon dioxide. What’s more, it would be virtually impossible to raise them in a perfectly climate-­neutral manner. “We don’t have a technical solution to change the stomach of a ruminant,” says Fredrik Hedenus, a professor in sustainability and systems analysis at Chalmers University of Technology in Sweden. Technologies to dampen the impact of their burps have been in the works for a while but have yet to scale. They also fix only part of the problem with red meat: Cows need a lot of land for pasture and for feed, which has made livestock a top driver of deforestation in the Amazon and elsewhere around the world. Even if the world stopped burning all fossil fuels tomorrow, emissions from agriculture would push the planet past 2 °C over preindustrial temperatures by 2070, Hedenus says. According to his research, more efficient livestock farming methods would still leave agriculture eating up over half of humanity’s carbon budget by that time. Only with the addition of dietary changes does his model show a winning scenario. He’s far from alone in his conclusions: A 2024 survey of more than 200 climate scientists, conducted at Harvard, found that the vast majority believe reducing emissions from livestock is important to meeting climate goals. People have to eat less beef. “Essentially, you need a culture shift,” Hedenus says. Yet the culture has proved intractable. Not only do people like the taste of meat, but across most of the world, meat-eating tends to be associated with status. As societies have grown wealthier, it has increased. A United Nations report showed that global meat production quadrupled between 1961 and 2022, while vegetarianism has held steady at about 4% of the population in the US since 1999. What could move the needle is a carbon-light and delicious meat replacement. But from a food-tech standpoint, all we have to show is a menu of mostly failed solutions. Lab-grown meat has generated endless headlines since the 2000s but has yet to become commercially available (and affordable) at scale. And while breakthroughs in taste and texture inched plant-based meats from companies like Impossible Foods and Beyond Meat toward mass adoption, the hype—and the appetite—has somewhat fizzled. So instead of asking people to change their preferences, startups like Joyn are trying a different tack. They’re creating what are broadly referred to as blended meat products, which combine plant proteins with the real deal. The logic: The best meat replacement is … better meat. Paul Shapiro, cofounder of the startup BMC Ingredients, which markets a plant protein related to mushrooms, says the math is easy to understand: If we imagine that Impossible Whoppers at Burger King account for about 2% of sales, that’s only a 2% reduction in meat. But if a conventional Whopper were 20% plants, that would be a 20% cut right off the bat. Those aren’t quite the numbers climate models are calling for, but some estimates say they could lead to outsized gains relative to the status quo: A 2022 study in Nature found that replacing 20% of beef globally could halve the annual rate of deforestation. And unlike the other solutions tried thus far, this particular Hail Mary is one a lot of omnivores seem open to giving a try. The idea of blending meat and vegetables together is, of course, nothing new. Food cultures from around the world boast an array of dishes that combine small amounts of meat with plants. Today, home cooks still add veggies and legumes to dishes to stretch tight grocery budgets or to sneak extra greenery into their children’s supper. But in the mid to late 2010s, however, the focus was largely on companies that appeared to have cracked the code for ditching meat entirely. When Impossible debuted its namesake burger in 2016, it impressed with an ingredient called heme, an iron-rich molecule in the protein soy leghemoglobin that allowed all-plant burgers to “bleed” like beef. Impossible and its biggest competitor, Beyond, swaggered through the media and the markets. In 2019, Impossible’s founder told the New Yorker he intended to wipe out all animal agriculture by 2035. Beyond’s 2019 IPO was the most successful since the 2008 financial crisis. A flood of imitators followed. But within a few years, tastes shifted. In 2023, Impossible employees’ stock values tumbled 89% from a 2021 high. That same year, Beyond laid off 20% of its staff, and a bruising Businessweek feature labeled the entire sector a fad. Sales have been slipping for years, with a 10% year-over-year drop in 2025, according to the Good Food Institute, a think tank focused on alternative proteins. Any quest to wipe out animal agriculture could now seem farcical. In 2025, Americans downed $45 billion worth of beef. Several factors were likely at play. Megan Krogius, a spokesperson for Impossible Foods, says the dip is a “correction not a crisis” after “an outsized pop in demand” between 2020 and 2022. She also says that a flurry of plant-based products muddied the category’s reputation. Indeed, some disruptors (and their imitators) have struggled to compete on the sensory level. Nectar, an organization that conducts research on meat alternatives, has found that taste and texture are among the major factors that keep people from adopting plant-based meats. “Our consumers identify products as having bad aftertaste and off flavors five to six times more than in animal counterparts,” says Caroline Cotto, the group’s director. At the same time, discourse around processed foods tarnished plant-based meat’s halo. In 2019, the Center for Consumer Freedom, a since-dissolved lobbying group backed by the meat industry, launched an ad campaign against plant-based alternatives, demonizing them as “ultra-­processed imitations.” While Impossible’s Krogius warns against conflating processed food with junk food (she points, for example, to a study in The Lancet finding mock meat to be nutritionally preferable to red meat), some of the bad press stuck. Now, the term “ultra-­processed” has become something of a political cudgel—one wielded by the meat-loving MAHA movement and the US secretary of health and human services, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. In 2025, he flipped the food pyramid and stacked a steak on top. Any quest to wipe out animal agriculture could now seem farcical. In 2025, Americans downed $45 billion worth of beef, and consumption rose by 4.3% over the previous year, according to an industry report. Rising prices could be slowing this down a bit, but cost can dampen only so much demand. Blended meat, which has meat’s own juiciness and texture, rose alongside the plant-based superstars, but it did not get New Yorker profiles or headlining IPOs. At the height of mock-meat hype, companies like Tyson and Perdue launched blended products, and firms working on lab-grown meat explored cutting their products with plants. The big advantage of blended meat is that large numbers of consumers—what some call “flexitarians”—are curious about it, and when they try it, they like it. A blind taste test co-funded by the USDA in 2022 showed that diners preferred blended burgers to fully plant-based ones. And a report from the major food processor ADM found that large proportions of every generation of Americans were interested in trying blended meats, including 75% of millennials. “This is a solution that we can do today, that consumers will accept today,” says Tim Dale, a director at Food Systems Innovations, the nonprofit group that oversees Nectar. At this point, even Impossible has acknowledged the potential. In a wide-ranging interview with the Wall Street Journal in 2025, the company’s former CEO Peter McGuinness admitted the brand’s struggle to win over consumers, and suggested a hybrid burger could bridge the divide. Plenty of newcomers are entering the fray. “Shiitake-infused beef burgers” from Australia-based Fable Foods are in restaurants and moving “incredibly well” at the high-end grocery store Central Market in Austin, Texas, says CEO Michael Fox. Cammarata’s claim is that LLMs might in fact be far easier to understand—and thus to monitor and control—than people previously thought. If true (and we’ll come back to that), it’s a huge deal. After a summer filled with revelations that swarms of agents built by top firms like OpenAI and Anthropic have been breaking their containment and causing havoc on the public internet—often while those companies weren’t aware of what their agents were up to—the AI industry is desperate to get a better handle on the technology it has built. That’s one reason the blog post landed with such force. “I think this may be remembered as a major moment in scientific history,” one AI researcher posted on X: “I feel like my understanding of what a neural net is has been catapulted into the future by five years or something.” Another called it “the most important AI paper of the year.” Not everyone is convinced, however. What Cammarata has shared is little more than a hunch, says Chris Potts at Stanford University, who leads a team that is trying to understand how LLMs work. “As far as I can tell, nothing about this blog post makes the problems any easier,” he says. “It would not have occurred to me to say this at all, but very prominent people on social media seem to believe that it is an all-encompassing breakthrough.” I talked to Cammarata the day after his post blew up. And after looking a lot, I have a hopeful story.” It has long been accepted that the mechanisms inside neural networks, the software that underpins most AI models, are very hard—perhaps impossible—to unpick fully. That’s because when the networks are trained, they appear to take on weird, tangled structures that make no sense to a human. Researchers like to say today’s AI is grown like a garden, not built like a bridge. Cammarata has done more than most to establish that view. He previously worked at OpenAI, where he helped pioneer techniques that allowed researchers to peer inside models, including tools that can probe individual neurons to see what they represent. It’s a little like how neuroscientists use MRI to study brains. (MIT Technology Review chose this approach, known as mechanistic interpretability, as one of its 10 hottest technologies of 2025.) Back in 2020, Cammarata and his colleagues were studying a small image-recognition model called Inception v1. When they looked at its neurons, it seemed to be using them to capture multiple things at once. For example, they found that one neuron responded to both images of cars and images of cats. The researchers concluded that the model was getting at least some of its neurons to multitask, perhaps so that it could fit in more data. Looking at other models appeared to back this up. The phenomenon became known as superposition: Models folded unrelated concepts into single neurons. The upshot was clear: When you looked inside a model, what you saw was a vast, tangled knot. Most mechanistic interpretability research has therefore focused on ways to untangle that knot. But despite a few breakthroughs, progress has been slow. Today’s models, such as the latest LLMs, are so large and complex that researchers can make sense of only tiny pieces at a time. Some leading figures in the field doubt if it will ever be possible to understand an LLM in fine-grained detail. Cammarata was one of them. “I was very pessimistic,” he says. But then he took the trouble to really look. At Goodfire, Cammarata spends nearly all of his time peering inside neural networks. He has built new tools and deploys teams of AI agents to help him make sense of what he sees. “I spent like, I don't know, 10 hours a day, seven days a week, for most of the last year just looking at neurons,” he says. What he claims to have realized is that there is a kind of logic to neural networks after all, even if they seem to make no sense at first. “We were looking at it wrong,” he says. “I just think you want to try slightly harder; you don't want to give up too quickly.” Cammarata almost makes it sound like a conversion of faith. Those early experiments with models such as Inception v1, which suggested that neural networks were tangled up, fostered a kind of confirmation bias. When researchers looked at a new model, they expected to be flummoxed, he says. “Every time I couldn’t understand something, I was like, ‘Wow, this untangling process is even more important than I thought, because I can’t make any progress without it.’” But the more Cammarata looked at neurons, he says, the more things started to fall into place. “Not all of them are flawless, but I expected nothing to be legible, or very little to be legible,” he says. “And actually, I was able to, like, map out whole circuitry.” Cammarata took his new approach—and his new agents—and went back to Inception v1. “I’d studied that for probably thousands of hours,” he says. But with fresh eyes, Cammarata found things that he now thinks they got wrong the first time around. Take the car-and-cat neuron. Rather than asking why multiple unrelated things were packed into a single neuron, he now tried to figure out what those things might have in common. It hit him: It was a V shape. “I was looking at the same images; there was no technical change,” he says. “But I now believed that neural networks defined patterns, and so I looked at it differently. It probably took me five seconds.” It turns out that the image-recognition model was packing cars and cats into the same place because pictures of both those things often feature a prominent shape that looks like a V: the face of a cat; the front of a car seen from the side. The model had stored cats and cars in the same place because at some level they look the same, says Cammarata. “And I was like, ‘Whoa, okay, I think I might just be misunderstanding neural networks in general.’” Cammarata is not saying that he yet fully gets how LLMs (or any models) work. Not even close. He is saying that he thinks full understanding is at least possible. “I previously thought there would be some really hard problems to solve,” he says. “I now feel like if I had 100,000 years to go look through a frontier language model just one circuit at a time, there’s, like, a pretty good shot I would come up with a good understanding.” He adds: “And if that's true, things are really good, because you’re about to get insanely smart agents at a ridiculous scale. And what would have taken me, you know, 100,000 years, we will be able to do much faster. Automated scientists are getting really good.” Built by Google in 2014, Inception v1 is a far simpler model than anything at the cutting edge today. Cammarata and his colleagues have started looking at modern LLMs and plan to publish the technical results of that work soon. A lot of researchers are looking forward to seeing those details. “I appreciate that he looked at thousands of examples, but even a large number here is not a substitute for rigorous evaluation,” says Stanford University’s Potts. In the meantime, some are pushing back hard on the hype. Jacob Hilton at the Alignment Research Center, a nonprofit research lab based in Berkeley, who has collaborated with Cammarata in the past, doesn’t think his claims are as important as people are making out. In a series of posts on X, Hilton notes that researchers understand less than 0.01% of the inner workings of top-tier LLMs. “I always appreciate Nick [Cammarata]’s boundless optimism when we hang out, but unfortunately this is giving the public an unrealistic picture of the state of the field,” he wrote. “For a full understanding, we need to go beyond neurons, and explain how they connect together to form circuits. If we are aiming for a full understanding, things are not looking good!” Neel Nanda at Google DeepMind, who helped pioneer mechanistic interpretability alongside Cammarata, is also skeptical. “This is interesting work, but I don’t think it’s a significant update to the big picture of interpretability,” he wrote on X. Nanda agrees that understanding what individual neurons do could help researchers figure out why specific models misbehave.
