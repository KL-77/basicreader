Switch 2 security takes a hit as hackers unlock homebrew access

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-22

A security researcher has demonstrated a software-only partial jailbreak of the Switch 2, gaining “userland” access that he says “lets us run homebrew, reverse engineer and mod Switch 2 games, back up save files off the Switch, and access most of the Switch 2’s peripherals through MMIO.” The hack relies on the original Switch version of SmileBasic 4, the latest in a long-running series of programming environments and interpreters that run on Nintendo hardware. The so-called “tethered” hack also requires access to a macOS or Linux PC on the same network to run a node.js package that helps enable privileged system access. Researcher Sammy Hajhamid writes that he used OpenAI’s Codex to poke at SmileBasic’s language interpreter, using a custom harness that could send USB commands to the system and view results via HDMI (as well as power cycling via a Bluetooth light switch flipping device). After “substantial effort for ~2 weeks,” that harness discovered a 32-bit integer overflow that occurs when removing items from an array in SmileBasic. That error gives access to an array pointer, which in turn allows read and write access to portions of system memory. From there, Hajhamid writes that he started poking around for access to the Switch 2’s internal sysmodules, looking for ones that could provide more privileged access to the console’s core functions. Eventually, he found a buffer overflow in the console’s FileSystem services module that exposed a cached data pointer, granting arbitrary read and write privileges. From there, Hajhamid writes that he could execute “privileged filesystem actions” and eventually achieve native code execution through a faked filesystem process. Hajhamid, who goes by Pepsipu online, showed off a host of new modded characters in Mario Kart World as a proof of concept for the exploit. Others are similarly showing off simple demos and in-progress developer tools running on exploited hardware. Full Vulkan GPU working in userland by u/Icy-ConcentrationC in switch2hacks Still, the current “userland” exploit keeps any Switch 2 homebrew developers trapped in a relatively small sandbox, and falls well short of the kernel-level access that would provide full control of the hardware. If the SmileBasic name sounds familiar to you, it’s probably because the 3DS version of the same software was key to the so-called smilehax exploit on that handheld back in 2016. Nintendo removed SmileBasic from the 3DS eShop shortly after that hack became public, and SmileBasic 4 has similarly already been delisted from the Switch eShop worldwide (though a Japanese demo version that may work similarly is still accessible as of this story’s publication). That delisting could help slow the spread of this exploit to the wider base of Switch 2 owners that haven’t previously purchased and/or downloaded SmileBasic. But others in the Switch 2 hacking community are already teasing some similar exploits that appear to work using other games. And while the current exploit works on the latest Switch 2 firmware version (23.0.1), this particular vulnerability seems likely to be patched out in imminent firmware releases, which will be required for continued access to many Nintendo services and future game releases. So while this exploit is definitely unwelcome news for Nintendo, it’s not nearly as problematic as the “unpatchable” original Switch exploit that surfaced in 2018. That hack required a complete hardware redesign to ensure that future systems were not susceptible. Still, the fact that Switch 2 hackers have gotten this far just over a year after the console’s release is not a good sign for the overall impregnability of Nintendo’s system software. Get ready for another never-ending cycle between console hackers and the hardware makers trying to keep them out of their console’s deepest recesses.

Moderna and Merck say their mRNA cancer vaccine cut death, recurrence by 42%

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-22

Visit Original Website
Personalized mRNA vaccines against melanoma—the deadliest form of skin cancer—reduced the risk of death or recurrence by 42 percent compared to standard treatment in a late-stage trial, pharmaceutical partners Merck and Moderna announced Thursday. The results are a significant advance in melanoma treatment; Merck and Moderna are already engaging with regulatory authorities to get the vaccine approved. More broadly, the vaccine’s success bolsters hope that mRNA vaccines and individualized cancer-targeting medicines offer a new paradigm for cancer therapies. “From the beginning, we believed mRNA technology could make it possible to develop cancer treatments uniquely matched to each patient’s tumor,” Moderna CEO Stéphane Bancel said in a statement. The trial results “mark a defining moment for that vision and show what’s possible when we rethink how medicines are made.” They also “reinforce our conviction that mRNA has the potential to fundamentally change how we treat cancer,” he said. In August, Moderna and Merck reported that the vaccine has succeeded in the trial, but held off reporting details until the data could be presented at an upcoming scientific conference. That conference is the European Society of Medical Oncology, starting now in Madrid, Spain. The data was scheduled for presentation on Saturday, but after an oncology news site reported the results early, breaking the embargo, the companies released the top-line results. The full data has been accepted for publication in the New England Journal of Medicine. As Ars reported earlier, the Phase 3 trial included 1,137 patients who had stage IIB–IV melanoma that had been surgically removed prior to entering the trial. Patients were randomized 2-1 to receive either a combination treatment including the personalized mRNA vaccine with a standard treatment, or the standard treatment alone. The standard treatment was Merck’s established monoclonal antibody cancer treatment Keytruda. The tailored mRNA vaccine is intismeran (or mRNA-4157), which uses a synthetic mRNA that holds the unique genetic coding for up to 34 mutations in a patient’s own cancer cells. To produce the personalized vaccines, researchers compare the genetic sequences of a patient’s cancer cells with those of healthy cells and pick out cancer-specific mutations that could help the immune response selectively target just the cancer cells. In the trial, researchers assessed “recurrence-free survival” or RFS, which is the amount of time a patient has without the cancer returning, and “distant metastasis-free survival” or DMFS, which is the amount of time a patient has without the cancer returning and occurring in a different part of the body than where it began. According to the rushed-out press release, after two years, participants in the trial who received the intismeran-Keytruda treatment had 42 percent reduction in their risk of recurrence or death compared to the participants who just got Keytruda and a placebo. What that looked like after two years was that 82 percent of the intismeran-Keytruda treatment group were still alive without recurrence, while only 70 percent of the Keytruda-placebo group were alive without recurrence. For distant metastasis-free survival, intismeran-Keytruda treatment reduced the risk of distant metastasis or death by 38 percent compared with the Keytruda-placebo group. At the end of the trial, 88.5 percent of the intismeran-Keytruda treatment group were alive without distant metastasis, while 80 percent of the Keytruda-placebo group were alive without distant metastasis. The treatment effect was not as strong as seen in a five-year Phase 2 trial, which showed a 49 percent reduction in the risk of recurrence or death and a 59 percent reduction in the risk of distant metastasis or death. Still, the findings are an exciting advance for melanoma patients. The trial “is the first Phase 3 study to demonstrate a clinically meaningful improvement in RFS and DMFS over standard-of-care KEYTRUDA in patients with resected melanoma, setting a new benchmark in the adjuvant setting and representing one of the most significant advances in melanoma treatment since the advent of immunotherapy,” Dean Li, president of Merck Research Laboratories, said in an announcement.

Latest estimate for Starship launches required to support a Moon landing? 22.

NASA does not have, nor want, nuclear weapons. But when it comes to a particular metric that is part of the Artemis Program to land people on the Moon, NASA guards a particular number like they are trying to prevent outsiders from cracking nuclear codes. I am speaking of the number of Starship launches required to fuel a Starship-based lunar lander in low-Earth orbit such that it can fly out to the Moon, land there, and return to lunar orbit. This number has been a moving target over the years. The general response from NASA has typically been, ‘We don’t know for sure, so we’re not going to say.’ In 2023, for example, the then-director of NASA’s Human Landing System, Lisa Watson-Morgan, told Ars, “We have a general idea, but I’m reluctant to say exactly what that is.” Later, she said the number was in the “high single digits to the low double digits.” That suggests about a dozen launches, which is a lot. SpaceX would need to launch a “depot” Starship, and then 11 “tanker” Starships to fully load it with liquid oxygen and methane propellant for a lunar voyage. Other officials have said “high teens.” The hope has always been that SpaceX would dramatically improve the lift capacity of Starship to bring down the number of tanker flights. But now there are concerns among some NASA officials that this might not be happening, or at least not happening soon enough to allow SpaceX’s Starship rocket to play a meaningful role in returning humans to the Moon for NASA as part of Artemis this decade. On Thursday, the NASA Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel held its quarterly meeting, during which panel member Charlie Precourt (a former NASA astronaut) spoke about risks associated with the Artemis Program and landing humans on the Moon. “With respect to Artemis IV, however, we’ve got a large number of firsts that reduce the likelihood of mission success,” Precourt said. “We need to keep our eyes on numerous required fuelings for the lander. I think the last estimate is around 22 flights.” Precourt did not name Starship, but he could only be referring to the SpaceX vehicle. NASA’s other lunar lander for human missions, Blue Origin’s Blue Moon, will not be using orbital refueling as part of its initial mission design. The safety panel almost certainly would have obtained this estimated number of refuelings, 22, from discussions with NASA officials as part of its deliberations during quarterly meetings. After I posted Precourt’s estimate on the social media site X, SpaceX founder Elon Musk replied that NASA’s safety panel estimate was not accurate. “That’s way too high,” Musk said. “Actual number will be more like 6 to 8.” To be fair to NASA and SpaceX, there remains a lot of uncertainty in how much cryogenic methane and liquid oxygen will boil off in orbit and how efficient the process of transferring propellants from one ship to another will be. But NASA can take the current performance of the lift capacity of Starship and determine the mass of propellant needed to support a lunar landing. For now, the space agency’s answer to the Starship question appears to be something on the order of two dozen Starship launches. SpaceX, of course, is working hard to increase the flight rate of Starship. But even if SpaceX achieves “twice weekly” launches by the end of 2027, the company would still need about three months to fuel a depot for a Starship landing. Precourt said the problem is a challenging one for NASA, and he praised the space agency chief, Jared Isaacman, and his team for trying to find ways to help SpaceX and Blue Origin be successful in their development of landers. NASA is still working toward a human lunar landing in 2028. “With respect to schedule, I personally really appreciate the focus the administrator and the leadership team is putting on how to press schedule by bringing in resources to enable it to look for places where there are barriers,” he said.

Armstrong said Comcast is “converting 70 percent of those free lines into paying relationships.” Armstrong also discussed Comcast’s plan to increase use of AI agents to interact with customers. It’s up to customers to determine whether this improves service or makes things worse, but it will apparently be cheaper for Comcast. “AI is already embedded across our network to optimize performance, and we’re expanding its application across customer experience through partnerships with Sierra and Cresta,” Armstrong said. “These capabilities are central to our new operating model, enabling us to bring products to market faster, simplifying customer interactions, and deliver a better experience more efficiently. Disgraced eMudhra drops out of TLS business after using cert private key of 4

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-22

Visit Original Website
eMudhra, the certificate authority that issued eight intermediate TLS credentials so weak that anyone could mint certificates for any website or mail server—one had a private encryption key of just 4—said it’s getting out of the business. V. Srinivasan, chairman of India-based eMudhra Limited, cited increasing costs and complexities of compliance requirements—paired with the business generating just 1 percent of its revenue. He also said that the emergence of organizations providing TLS certificates free of charge further reduced returns on investment when compared to other products and services the company offers. “However, the level of social media amplification around this incident has brought scrutiny that is out of proportion to SSL/TLS’s place in our business,” the company continued in a Thursday post. “In view of this, after detailed deliberations our board has concluded that withdrawing from global SSL/TLS is the right decision for eMudhra and its shareholders.” The announcement came two days after researchers revealed eMudhra had issued eight certificates with private keys so defective they were trivial to discover. Anyone who took the time—which was measured in milliseconds—could crack them and then use them to generate TLS certificates that would be trusted by all major browsers, as well as an untold number of email servers, IoT devices, Docker containers, forgotten Linux machines, and unmaintained CLI tools that ship their own copy of the roots. Whereas NIST P-384 elliptic curve cryptography private keys—used in the type of certificates eMudhra issued—are supposed to contain roughly 384 bits of entropy, the eight compromised certificates had key sizes ranging from 3 to 8 bits. Stated differently, whereas there are supposed to be 2384—a number with 116 digits—different key possibilities, the strongest of the defective keys had a maximum of just 256. The private keys were 4, 56, 66, 115, 155, 156, 230, 237. Browser makers blocked certificates that were issued with the intermediate certificates within a day or two of them being discovered. eMudhra almost certainly faced the prospect of its certificate authority status being pulled by the CA/Browser Forum, an industry-wide group that sets rules for admittance into the TLS certificate program. On Wednesday, eMudhra provided a brief explanation for the debacle. The certificates were “created under eMudhra’s ECC root in a Key Generation Ceremony (KGC) held on April 30, 2025, using FIPS 140-2 Level 3 Hardware Security Modules (HSMs),” the company said. “A flaw in the KGC caused a wrap/unwrap step to produce a second set of weak keys alongside the original strong keys. The weak keys were the ones published to the browser root stores. eMudhra is working to establish the root cause.” HSMs are dedicated devices for storing encryption keys and other highly sensitive data. They are generally segregated from most of a network to prevent leakage. Wrapping and unwrapping refers to the process that occurs when keys are being transferred from one HSM to another. Before the handover, the HSM storing the key wraps it in a separate key. Once the encrypted key is moved to a new HSM, the latter unwraps the key. In a thread on Mozilla’s Bugzilla forum, eMudhra provided more details: Among them: [T]here appears to be a flaw in the KGC script that was used on that specific day to create these ECC issuers. As for these ECC keys, our review of the KGC, assessment of the code and view of HSM keys so far indicate that a) a set of original ECC keys were created onboard as expected b) an approved deviation to our usual KGC to test a wrap/unwrap was introduced as these keys were meant for export for this instance c) a flaw in the code resulted in the second pair of keys being created onboard which were weaker d) these second pair of weaker keys were used by mistake to create the csr [certificate signing request] (because the key labels were similar) and signed by our root e) the CSR from these weaker keys were also sent over to [fellow certificate authority] Sectigo for cross signing (we verified the files sent over to Sectigo). The CRL’s were signed with the original private keys (which were supposed to be the correct keys with appropriate entropy) but given that this resulted in incorrect signatures, they were resigned with flawed keys. There were human errors in multiple steps of the process that resulted in significant flaws in the key lifecycle integrity process for this KGC. We are now digging deeper for code flaws, process gaps, maker/checker controls specific to this issue. Over the next week, we will reverify every key ceremony we have done. A CRL, short for certificate revocation list, is a digitally signed list published by a certificate authority identifying certificates that have been revoked before their expiration dates. The revelation that the initial signing resulted in incorrect CRL signatures raises the question: why didn’t eMudhra investigate the cause of the failure? With the intermediate certificates blocked by browsers, much of the threat is gone. But plenty of other devices that rely on the same certificates require much longer windows to take the same action. Still other devices, such as embedded devices, may never be able to block them.

When Edsel died in 1943 and Henry Ford in 1947, the money they left vaulted the Ford Foundation to the top of the world’s foundations, according to a 2024 Fortune commentary co-written by Walker. The Ford Foundation frames its new plan as its answer to rising autocracy, widening inequality, and fading public trust. “Conditions have changed since our last strategy review, and we are marshaling all of our resources to respond to these new challenges while staying true to our legacy as a social justice foundation,” Gerken said in a statement. European customers increasingly want to understand what providers can guarantee when political circumstances change. The answers depend on how services are operated and the commitments companies are prepared to make. Those business decisions are shaping the credibility of corporate promises long before they are communicated. Three areas show how these expectations are changing. The first is the control European customers retain over their technology. As sovereignty becomes measurable, claims about European control are becoming easier to challenge. Companies that describe their services as sovereign without being clear about those dependencies risk accusations of sovereignty-washing. This is where reputation and communications become part of the business decisions. The credibility of a company’s European commitments depends on the operating arrangement and protections behind them. Communications leaders can help organisations anticipate the expectations those decisions will need to meet, bringing an understanding of customer concerns and public trust into the discussion before commitments are made. American technology companies have established what their products can deliver. Europe’s changing expectations mean they now need to demonstrate what they can guarantee when circumstances change. Their reputation will increasingly depend on how clearly they explain those guarantees and how well the underlying business decisions support them. But among Ramaswamy’s fellow South Asians, a demographic that votes broadly Democratic but has drifted a touch rightward in recent cycles, there is also widespread doubt and suspicion—inconvenient for a campaign that needs every vote it can find. The community ties and social connections that Ramaswamy and his family have established across Cincinnati and Columbus could be helpful, in theory. But for many of his would-be constituents, the question is what those connections mean when the candidate has embraced a movement that sees them as the enemy. If you want to understand why Ramaswamy seems on course for a historic crashout in Ohio, those voters are a good place to start. In 2024, Amjad Baig, a real estate agent based in the Columbus suburbs, joined an unusual coalition of Americans in making a counterintuitive choice: He voted for Donald Trump. Baig, who supported Hillary Clinton in 2016 and organized a Pakistanis for Biden group in 2020, considers himself a fervent patriot. After emigrating from Lahore to the United States in the early 2000s and settling in the Ohio town of Dublin, the 54-year-old father of four got involved with its burgeoning South Asian blocs, eventually becoming president of the local Community Association of Pakistani Americans, and partnering frequently with the Ohio branch of the Federation of Indian Associations. I met him in September for dinner at Neehee’s, a regional Indian chain, where he recounted his Trump-era political journey. “Joe Biden should have resigned earlier. Six months before the election, they decided Kamala Harris would run as a candidate. There was no primary,” he said. “We could have had a better candidate. So I encouraged people in my network to vote against Harris.” They weren’t hard to persuade, Baig added: Many South Asian Ohioans were already angered by the Biden administration’s indifference toward the atrocities in Gaza, and partial to Trump’s publicly professed skepticism of Middle Eastern wars. That’s not quite how things have turned out two years later. Israel is still bombing Gaza, despite the ostensible ceasefire with Hamas; the war in Iran and its price shocks continue, hitting everything from fuel to food; Trump 2.0 is persecuting immigrants far more thoroughly, and aggressively, than even in his first term. The normally independent-minded South Asians across Ohio’s cities, Baig told me, are so furious that they’re again ready to vote against an Indian American candidate, just to hold Trump to account, even though many of them liked Ramaswamy when he first emerged on the scene. This western stretch of the Columbus metropolitan area, the fastest-growing and most economically vibrant region in the Buckeye State, is not coincidentally home to its largest South Asian populations. Twenty years ago, as Baig related to me, the Noor Islamic Cultural Center had opened its doors in Dublin, welcoming the burgeoning South Asian American groups who had settled in and around the state’s capital. About a 15-minute drive away stands Ohio’s oldest Hindu temple, founded by white Hare Krishna disciples, now home to hundreds of worshippers of Indian origin. The Noor’s opening similarly attracted an influx of Ohioans with family roots in Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Afghanistan. It’s a collective of freethinking, independent, educated constituents who, across their respective backgrounds and countries of origin, have a lot of thoughts about the fact that one of their own is running to become governor of their state. As Ohio State University professor Pranav Jani has written, Ramaswamy may be “skinfolk, but so not our kinfolk.” Unfortunately for the campaign, there are many others who feel this way. “This is a country I need to protect, which is harder to do these days,” Baig told me. He was distressed about quite a few matters: the economic burdens facing Ohio’s small-business owners, national and local restrictions on the voting process, state-level efforts undermining the local hemp industry. Yet one matter was paramount: the attacks on first-generation immigrants like himself. “We’re scared,” he added. “Just being an immigrant, you’re a target, thanks to ICE and all these bigots who’ve been given permission by Donald Trump. Vivek is going to follow what the Republicans say, and they’re so anti-immigrant now that that’s where he’ll land.” Shabana Siddiqi, a conservative-leaning but, she said, nonpartisan retired physician who’d appreciated Ramaswamy’s presidential campaign for foregrounding his family background and opposing transgender education in schools, sounded nothing less than contemptuous of him now. “Racism is so common here, especially for Pakistanis and Indians. Vivek is brown, and we are all citizens with the same rights, but he doesn’t care about that,” she said. “I’ve met with Amy. She listens to us. She feels our pain. And I don’t think these Republicans understand, they’re losing a large chunk of votes the way they’re going.” Aziz Taqdeer, an Afghan immigrant and social worker who’s lived in the United States since 2015, told me he’s hitting the polls for the first time ever because of his concerns around the Trump administration’s immigration crackdowns. “Over the past three years, it’s become impossible to get by. I’ve never been more worried,” Taqdeer said. “I know people who’ve been detained by ICE, and it does something horrible to your mental health, to know these agents can come for you at any time.” Also weighing on Taqdeer’s mind: inflation, his children, the driver who yelled at him the other day to “go back to your country,” and the ongoing betrayal of the many Afghans he knew who assisted U.S. forces against the Taliban. Mike DeWine in 2022, said he will now be voting Democratic across the ballot in large part because of inflation. “I’m right in the center, not on the extreme left or right—I vote for whoever is good for the country,” Mehmood said. “Right now, the war and the economy are in such a horrible place. It’s making things harder for all us.” Rajnikanth Katte, a Hindu Indian immigrant who serves as chairman of the Federation of Indian Associations in Ohio, agreed with this diagnosis and suspected that Democrats would win, even as he proudly displayed to me a photo of himself standing with LaRose, the secretary of state, at a recent federation event. “So many people are struggling and hurting right now,” Katte said. “I’m always in favor of whoever can help us make incremental improvements—whoever it is, whether that’s a Democrat or a Republican, I’ll support them.” This will be the first election in Ohio’s history in which neither of the major-party gubernatorial candidates is Christian. He owns multiple Teslas, and neither Elon Musk’s white supremacist turn nor his massive donations on behalf of Ramaswamy’s campaign will get Baig to join liberals’ Tesla boycotts. (His Dublin neighborhood is, incidentally, located next to a Tesla collision center.) He counts a few local GOP operatives, including volunteers for the Ramaswamy campaign, among his personal friends. He’s likewise realistic about what he’ll be voting for when he votes for her. “Acton is very genuine and very liberal,” he said. “But the only reason she will win is because people don’t want Vivek.” That can go multiple ways. People don’t want Vivek, meaning onetime GOP voters are fed up and sticking it to the party. People don’t want Vivek, meaning strict Christians don’t want a Hindu in office. People don’t want Vivek, meaning they think immigrants and their citizen-by-birth children are ruining America. In Ohio, I saw all versions in action. You don’t know who they are, or what they believe.” At least, until she met Acton for herself and made her own judgment. So, yes, Ramaswamy’s Hindu faith may not be welcomed by many of Ohio’s Christians. Sudarshan Pyakurel, a Columbus resident and Hindu Bhutanese refugee, wrote a column for the Columbus Dispatch back in January where he called out Ramaswamy over that. “Many Hindu families in Ohio live with fear—fear of ICE raids, profiling based on accent or appearance and the constant anxiety of family separation,” Pyakurel wrote. “In such a moment, a public figure who claims Hindu values but offers only rhetoric and silence does not represent us.” A lot of people don’t want Vivek. Over the past few months, an upstart political polling firm kept delivering good news for Democrats—and especially those on the left. Across more than a dozen surveys, it found progressives leading in key primary races and showed other Democrats in striking distance of winning traditionally Republican states. Except, it turns out, the surveys were fake. The deception, perpetrated by an organization called Wedgewood Polls, doubles as a cautionary tale about how people who follow politics should think about polls—not just fake polls, but also ones that are on the level. A data-journalism outlet called FiftyPlusOne contacted Wedgewood Polls this week with some troubling allegations: The firm, which claimed to have started conducting polls over the summer and didn’t list any employees on its website, appeared to have manipulated survey data to produce fake results. The details of the deception are a little in the weeds, but Wedgewood ultimately came clean. In a public statement on Tuesday, the firm described itself as a “fictional entity” that had deliberately published “fabricated numbers.” It then deleted its social media accounts and website. Yikes. But why would someone want to fake poll results? Wedgewood claims it did so as part of “an experimental project to demonstrate the vulnerabilities, lack of oversight, and overall low barrier to entry in the modern political polling industry.” Yet Wedgewood also lied to media outlets that did ask probing questions about its methods, so that explanation is hard to take at face value. Money is one potential motive. Wedgewood appears to have charged clients to conduct surveys on their behalf, a standard practice for aboveboard polling firms that could in this case amount to fraud. We still aren’t 100 percent clear on who the people behind Wedgewood are, but they may have wanted to make money by manipulating betting markets on the races they claimed to survey. Wedgewood claims its deception “was never intended to sway an election or permanently mislead the public,” but it may also have wanted to make certain Democrats look more electable—either to boost progressives or get Democrats to spend valuable resources in red states. Did Wedgewood’s surveys affect those races? Polls can influence who voters, donors, and party committees decide to support, and there’s some evidence that Wedgewood’s did. Mallory McMorrow, a Michigan Democrat whose U.S. Senate campaign flopped, says a Wedgewood survey showing her support cratering helped throttle donations and increased calls for her to drop out. “It killed us,” she wrote this week. On the other side of that coin is Francesca Hong, a democratic socialist who nearly won the Democratic primary for Wisconsin governor. Before her narrow loss, Hong referenced a since-deleted Wedgewood poll in a fundraising pitch to argue that her campaign had “momentum.” And as Politico reports, Democrats stepped up funding to Scott Colom, who’s running in deep-red Mississippi, after a Wedgewood poll last week showed him within 4 points of Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith. OK, but how much impact can one fake polling firm really have? Actually, Wedgewood’s deception is part of a pattern. A group called Median Strategies also confessed to fabricating survey data earlier this year, similarly describing itself as a “social experiment examining how purported polling information could enter and spread through the political information ecosystem.” And on Thursday, FiftyPlusOne uncovered a third political polling firm that may have cooked some of its data. That firm, SoCal Strategies, has since deleted its website and social media accounts. So does this mean political polls are just made up?? That’s going a step too far. It’s true that political polling has fallen on hard times. Americans are increasingly less likely to answer surveyors’ cold calls, making high-quality polls more expensive. Major polling misses in recent elections have also caused some universities, survey firms, and media outlets to abandon political polling entirely. But does that mean most polls are fake? Emphatically no. Legitimate pollsters strive to be transparent about who’s conducting them, who’s funding them, and the methods they use. They’re also among the people most upset about what Wedgewood did. “People like the idiots behind Wedgewood, and, to a lesser extent, Median, make it harder for the rest of us who are absolutely busting our butts to do good public opinion research,” the polling expert Natalie Jackson wrote on Substack today. But if that’s true, does this Wedgewood incident really matter? What’s the bigger picture? Unless you’re some kind of oracle, election outcomes are unknowable in advance. In that sea of uncertainty, it’s natural to grab onto any shred of data that seems to offer clues, and polls have become an outsize part of how many Americans think about politics. It’s also becoming more common for campaigns to blast out poll results—sometimes of questionable provenance—on social media or in fundraising emails. Some of that is understandable. When it comes to predicting elections, polls remain much better than person-on-the-street interviews, lawn signs, or crowd sizes at rallies. But polls have always been imperfect, their accuracy subject to reaching a representative sample, assumptions about who will actually vote, and mathematical laws of probability. None of that justifies Wedgewood’s deception, and it’s a frustrating reality that the stakes of American politics have gotten higher even as the availability of high-quality polling data has declined. But the next time you see a polling result that seems too good to be true, just remember: As the cliché goes, the only poll that really matters is on Election Day. In an unsigned order on Wednesday, the Supreme Court denied the Trump administration’s request for permission to implement what is, even by the Trump administration’s standards, a remarkably barbaric element of its anti-trans agenda: a blanket denial of gender-affirming healthcare to transgender people in federal prison for the first time in three decades. Only Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito noted their dissent, because for them, nothing is more important than their solemn duty to give their favorite president whatever he wants. Shortly after taking office in January 2025, President Donald Trump issued a sweeping anti-trans executive order aimed at purging the government of “gender ideology.” This case, Trump v. The next competitive advantage in enterprise AI has less to do with which model you choose than with what you build around it. That’s an important shift. For the past several years, much of the AI conversation has centered on model capability: bigger models, better reasoning, larger context windows, higher benchmark scores. Those advances have been extraordinary. But as AI moves from experimentation to production, the question facing enterprise leaders has changed—it’s no longer, “what can the model do?”—it’s now, “Can we deliver that intelligence reliably, economically, and at scale?” Increasingly, the answer depends on the serving harness—the engineering system surrounding the model, including the architecture, memory management, scheduling, and optimization decisions that determine how efficiently model intelligence becomes a production capability. For technical executives, that changes where AI investment should go—and what they should demand from the architecture supporting it. There is no universally “best” model or AI architecture. There is only the right solution for the business problem. A customer-facing assistant optimized for fast responses has fundamentally different requirements from a document-processing pipeline that can run overnight. An internal knowledge application and a high-volume customer service platform may use the same foundation model but require very different serving architectures. The workload defines the constraints. The constraints should determine the architecture. That sounds simple, but it runs counter to the way many organizations have approached early AI deployments. During a proof of concept, model accuracy naturally dominates the discussion. Production introduces constraints that are much easier to ignore when only a handful of users are involved: latency, concurrency, memory pressure, GPU utilization, infrastructure cost, and unpredictable traffic. A model that produces marginally better answers is not necessarily the better production choice if it doubles inference costs or cannot meet the application's latency requirements. Not every workload needs a Ferrari. Sometimes the smarter engineering decision is the model that delivers exactly the performance the business needs without paying for capability it doesn't. The objective should be to deploy the right intelligence at the right cost for the workload. That makes serving architecture more than an engineering concern. It becomes an investment decision: where to spend on compute, where to optimize, and where additional model capability simply isn't worth the cost. Once AI reaches production, seemingly isolated engineering decisions begin affecting one another. Serving architecture influences memory utilization. Memory management affects concurrency. Concurrency shapes batching and scheduling, and scheduling affects GPU utilization and latency. Only then can techniques such as quantization, speculative decoding, or optimized attention mechanisms deliver their full value. This interdependence matters because organizations can spend considerably more on infrastructure without solving the constraint limiting performance. GPU memory is a good example. During inference, the model's weights remain fixed, but the key-value (KV) cache grows with active requests and context length. As concurrency increases, a system can run short of usable GPU memory even when compute capacity is still available. Adding more compute without understanding that bottleneck may simply make an inefficient system more expensive. The lesson for technology leaders is broader than memory management: Measure before you optimize. Identify the dominant constraint. Address it. Measure the result. Then determine if another bottleneck emerged. Repeat. Don't think of optimization techniques as Pokémon cards: The goal isn't to collect them all. The goal is to apply the technique that removes the highest-impact constraint while preserving the application's business requirements. For technology leaders, that demands a different governance question. Instead of asking which AI capabilities the organization can deploy, ask which constraints stand between a proven capability and sustainable business value. Perhaps the most important reason to invest in the serving harness is that today's leading model will probably not be tomorrow’s. Models will improve. Hardware will evolve. New optimization techniques will emerge. The architecture surrounding them should make those changes easier to absorb, not force the enterprise to rebuild its AI platform every time technology advances. That makes adaptability an increasingly important outcome of AI strategy. Organizations that build serving systems around workload requirements, clear service-level objectives, and measurable production economics gain the ability to evaluate new models and infrastructure against what the business actually needs. A model change can become an evaluation and configuration decision rather than an architectural reset. While technically material needs to be frozen for only two consecutive years to count as permafrost, most of our current permafrost formed during the last ice age. That means there’s organic material that’s been frozen, and essentially locked away, for thousands of years. But as global temperatures tick up and this layer thaws, microbes are able to break down that organic matter, releasing carbon dioxide and methane—powerful greenhouse gases that drive climate change. Those emissions form a feedback loop, accelerating warming further. The steady rise in global temperatures has already shrunk the footprint of the Arctic permafrost by about 12% since 1850, and the pace has been picking up in the last 50 years. The wild thing is, only a minority of the models that have been used in UN climate reports actually take into account the gradual loss of permafrost. And none of them include the abrupt thaw and collapse that can happen after events like heat waves and fires (more on those fires in a moment). There are efforts to make sure future UN reports reflect the growing science, but for now, all this means that we’re probably underestimating how much and how fast the planet is going to warm. Fire is a natural part of boreal forests’ life cycle, but they’re getting more intense because of climate change. And crucially, fires can accelerate the thawing of permafrost. By the end of the century, this effect could release the equivalent of 80 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide. Today, the policy across remote parts of Canada and Alaska is generally to let wildfires burn as long as they’re not threatening human habitats. But some researchers say we should explore fighting these fires to save the carbon trapped in the forests and the permafrost beneath them. Researchers at the Woodwell Climate Research Center are developing a map of burn risk and carbon vulnerability across Alaska, essentially highlighting areas where fires are most likely to spread and which areas are home to certain types of permafrost that would release the most emissions. (Yes, apparently there are different types of permafrost. Yedoma is a particularly deep, carbon-dense form from the late Pleistocene era.) That’s all just the beginning—once we figure out potential targets, the question is what to do about it. Some suggest setting more prescribed fires, something Indigenous groups have done for thousands of years, partly to reduce the danger of massive blazes. Or resources could go to early detection and efforts to put out fires before they grow. There’s about 1.5 trillion tons of carbon locked away in permafrost, about twice the amount in the atmosphere. We know that permafrost thawing can release greenhouse-gas emissions and drive climate change. But there are complications. One particularly big wrinkle: Other effects from permafrost thawing and fire recovery might counteract the carbon lost from those events. For example, as temperatures warm, plants can gradually move north and take hold where they couldn’t have before. Those plants soak up carbon from the atmosphere as they grow, at least partly locking away what was emitted. Researchers are working to better understand this ecosystem. And the more we learn, the more complicated the picture looks. How much carbon is lost from permafrost, and how much a given area will recover its carbon, depend on a huge array of factors. How severe are the fires that have accelerated local thawing? What’s the local climate and environment? And what kind of permafrost is at stake? The higher we push the planet’s temperature, the more we mess with complicated natural phenomena that we’re only beginning to understand.
