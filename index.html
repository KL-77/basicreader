Polestar tries to quantify what it’s like to drive a performance car

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-20

The human desire to go fast is old, dating back at least as far as the first time some adventurous Yamnaya saw a horse on the Pontic-Caspian steppe and thought, “I bet if I jumped on that thing’s back, it would be faster than walking.” A thousand years later, someone else invented the wheel—and the chariot—and humanity was ready to roll. Now, about five millennia since that first horseback ride, equestrianism is mostly a leisure activity, and we drive cars, propelled by motors with more power than an entire herd of beasts could muster. Acceleration, top speed, and lap times are the usual ways we quantify that performance, and performance electric vehicles are currently in an arms race to see which one can hit 60 mph in the least amount of time. But measuring a car’s performance is easy—all you need is a stopwatch and a track. How about measuring the effect of car performance on the driver? That’s a question Polestar is trying to answer with researchers at the University of Oxford. “As electric cars redefine what performance can be, we wanted to understand something that can’t be captured by a specification sheet alone: the human experience behind the wheel,” said Christian Samson, Polestar’s head of product attributes. “Working with University of Oxford allows us to look at performance through a different lens—connecting the engineering of the car with the experience of the person driving it.” The pilot study involved 12 drivers who track-tested both rear- and all-wheel-drive versions of the Polestar 4 at a track in Sweden. The Polestar 4 is the OEM’s midsize crossover, a compelling Porsche Macan alternative that you can sadly no longer buy here in the US. The drivers were instrumented with heart rate monitors and skullcaps studded with electrodes, plus the cars’ gaze-tracking driver monitoring system to gather physiological data, which was combined with telemetry from the cars and subjective interview assessments after the fact. While being measured, drivers lapped the Gotland Ring circuit gently, then dynamically. You won’t be surprised to learn that the AWD performance Polestar 4 was able to generate higher G loads under cornering and acceleration, thanks to having double the power output (536 hp/400 kW) of the single-motor version. Peak acceleration was 32 percent higher, and drivers needed 15 percent less steering effort compared to a matched fast lap with the single-motor car. Interviews also revealed the drivers’ preference for the more performant Polestar: On a scale of 0 to 100, the AWD version was rated 89.2 for performance and 93 for the overall experience, versus 67.7 for performance and 77.9 overall for the rear-drive version. But their subjective descriptions were a little more informative. The more performant car engendered feelings of trust in the vehicle and a sense of control and kept drivers’ attention deeply engaged in the task at hand (driving). More than one compared the experience to a “controlled roller coaster.” “Performance is not simply about going faster. It is about giving the driver a sense of confidence and control through predictable vehicle responses,” said Max Klapow, a researcher at Oxford. “Our research takes a step toward measuring that human experience, by connecting what the car does with what happens to the driver.” Polestar acknowledges that a study of 12 participants is only a pilot, but its findings support a simple idea: Even if people don’t think handling matters, they still prefer a car that handles well to one that doesn’t. The late Richard Parry-Jones, who rose to become CTO at Ford, was in charge of signing off vehicle handling and was a stickler for it. In the foreword to a book written shortly before his untimely death, Parry-Jones explained why: Some in the company would say, “Richard why are you doing that? Spending all the money on chassis and steering? Customers never ask about things like that.” But they were wrong. People got into a Focus and said, “I really like driving it.” You’d ask why and they’d say, “I don’t know; I just do.” Understanding why they like it is our job. I tracked customer satisfaction against attributes like steering, brakes, spaciousness, and so on. To people’s amazement, I was able to prove that steering was the most strongly correlated, because bad steering is tiring… That’s what I learned about vehicle dynamics and linear responses: it’s tiring if it’s bad but rewarding if it’s good. I heard a similar take from General Motors’ Ken Morris, whose expansive job portfolio includes all the automaker’s product programs as well as motorsport. “There are people that think no one can tell the difference [with handling], you know, and I’ve always said they absolutely can tell the difference,” Morris told me last year. “They don’t know what it is. And the structural feel of the car going down the road, you know, people might explain, ‘It feels like a vault.’ Well, I can tell you exactly what’s going on, physically, from the parts and the tuning, and it’s an outcome that we strive for.”

Muse, the new general-purpose AI agent from Meta, can expose sensitive information when users share conversations through web links, researchers reported Monday. In response to questions from Ars about the behavior, Meta said it’s suspending the sharing feature while the company reviews it and considers making changes. Like many online services, Muse has allowed users to share searches, conversations, images, and similar content with other people. Such share features usually involve clicking an option to create a unique URL that anyone can use to load a page hosting the content. To keep potentially sensitive information from being accessible to unintended people, the URLs typically contain a long sequence of text that can’t be easily guessed. Muse, which Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg said last month was “built from the ground up for privacy and security,” seems to take the same precautions. But a central design decision for constructing URLs has made the links easy to discover. As a result, researchers from security firm Claroc said they were able to enumerate 6,800 links Muse users created for sharing conversations with others. After opening a few hundred of them, the researchers found information that was clearly not intended to be publicly available, including: “If you had asked us beforehand, we would have guessed that gathering these would take real effort,” the researchers wrote. “It took one query.” In an emailed statement responding to questions from Ars, Meta said: We opened the Muse bug bounty program so researchers could raise different scenarios and help us continue improving the product. People have the ability to share specific Muse artifacts, which creates a page that can be accessed by those with the link. However, we’ve paused this functionality while we review this report and consider changes to how these links can be discovered. Asaf Aprozper, the co-founder and CTO of Claroc, said he reported the findings through Meta’s bug bounty program on October 8 and also tried reaching people at Meta on LinkedIn, but got no reply either way. The easiest way to discover the URLs is to perform a passive DNS search through one of many available commercial services. Passive DNS records store the historical resolution of domain names to IP addresses, as well as nameserver, and mail-server records over time. Passive DNS systems typically collect DNS responses from sensors positioned at recursive DNS resolvers or other infrastructure. A passive DNS record is generally created when a monitoring system observes a DNS response. In the case here, once the intended party clicks on the share link, the DNS information is recorded in a searchable database. Claroc researchers were able to discover the Muse share links because all of them use the domain name cf.metaaiusercontent.com. Discovery is further made easy by the decision to add the conversation name to the URL, along with a long string of text. Simplified examples of such links are home-sale-budget-9f21c8a3.cf.metaaiusercontent.com, q3-revenue-review-7b1e42f0.cf.metaaiusercontent.com, and loan-application-draft-a4c9e2.cf.metaaiusercontent.com. It’s this last design decision, putting the conversation title in the subdomain rather than in the URL path, that makes public discovery possible. “Plenty of AI assistants share by public link,” Claroc said. “What makes Muse’s shares easy to find is the title sitting inside the web address, where public DNS records it for anyone to search. We call these GhostShares: pages that open for people who were never handed the link, and that those people can find on their own.” Muse quickly catapulted to one of the top spots in Apple’s App Store. The assistant autonomously fills out forms, books appointments, makes purchases, creates documents, and carries out all kinds of other actions. To work as intended, Muse requires access to most of a user’s accounts. Meta has heavily marketed the assistant as a platform that’s worthy of the extraordinary trust it needs to work as intended. Three weeks after the Muse launch, macOS security expert Patrick Wardle discovered a zero-day vulnerability that allowed any other app installed on the same device to gain complete control of the user’s Muse account. Meta downplayed the severity of the vulnerability on the grounds that exploitation wasn’t possible unless a device was already compromised. The company didn’t acknowledge Wardle’s finding that a simple ClickFix attack was all that was needed to hijack an account. The ease in discovering share links further justifies asking if Muse is worthy of the access it requires. With Meta’s suspension of the feature, Muse users no longer face this particular threat, at least for now.

Japan launches mission to bring home a piece of the Martian moon Phobos

Visit Original Website
Japan launched a $578 million mission Monday on a five-year expedition to land on the largest moon of Mars and retrieve samples for return to Earth, a daring international project that could yield new insights into the formation of the Solar System. The robotic Martian Moons eXploration mission, known as MMX, lifted off at 3:41 pm EDT Monday (4:41 am Japan Standard Time Tuesday) from Tanegashima Space Center in southern Japan. The MMX spacecraft rode into orbit atop a 207-foot-tall (63-meter) H3 rocket, Japan’s flagship launch vehicle. The rocket assigned to MMX debuted a new souped-up configuration with four strap-on solid rocket boosters, full-throttle main engines, and an extended payload shroud to accommodate the demands of Japan’s heaviest and most ambitious interplanetary mission to date. The rocket’s upper stage accelerated the MMX spacecraft to a top speed of more than 24,000 mph, fast enough to break free of Earth’s gravitational pull for the 10-month voyage to Mars. The nearly 9,900-pound (4.5-metric ton) spacecraft—two-thirds of its mass comprised of propellant—separated from the H3 rocket about 71 minutes after liftoff. Hiroshi Yamakawa, president of the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA), declared the launch a success. “A long journey of five years has just started,” Yamakawa said. MMX established contact with ground controllers, unfurled its solar panels, and was in a “very stable condition” soon after separating from the H3 rocket, according to Yasuhiro Kawakatsu, the mission’s project manager at JAXA. “So far, so good.” “The MMX [mission] we launched today is a challenging mission aiming for the world’s first sample return from the Martian moons,” said Yoshihiro Seki, Japan’s science minister. “We hope that the results obtained through this mission will contribute to the development of our nation’s space science and exploration technology.” The mission to the Martian moons builds on Japan’s success in leading the Hayabusa and Hayabusa 2 missions, the world’s first two spacecraft to bring back pristine specimens from asteroids. Scientists believe Phobos could itself be an asteroid that was captured into orbit around Mars billions of years ago. One of MMX’s science goals is finding out if that theory is correct. With the Hayabusa missions, sample returns from deep space have become a “speciality” for Japan’s space program, Kawakatsu said. For MMX, Japan attracted contributions from NASA and space agencies in Europe. “We have a proven track record of success in these areas,” Kawakatsu said. “Because of this, I believe they are participating with the understanding that if they want to conduct celestial body sample returns, joining a mission led by Japan is more certain to succeed than the US or Europe launching their own missions.” MMX will spend about three years in the vicinity of Mars after arriving there next August, conducting a series of encounters with the Martian moons Phobos and Deimos. Phobos is the larger of the two, with a lumpy, potato-like shape, an average diameter of 14 miles (22 kilometers), and a surface area comparable to the city of Houston. Upon reaching Mars, MMX will jettison its propulsion module, dead weight after burning all its fuel to enter orbit around the red planet. That will leave the main section of the MMX spacecraft and its four-legged landing gear, known as the exploration module, to move closer to Phobos. The moon’s gravity is too weak and the pull of Martian gravity too strong for the MMX spacecraft to enter a stable orbit around Phobos. Instead, the spacecraft will fly in a so-called quasi-satellite orbit, remaining bound to Mars while still able to maneuver around Phobos. The mission’s reconnaissance of Phobos will culminate in late 2028 or early 2029, when the MMX mothership will release a small rover to drop onto Phobos. The four-wheeled robot, named Idefix, is a contribution from the French and German space agencies. Idefix, the size of a large shoebox, will attempt to drive around Phobos for up to 100 days while grappling with the moon’s rough topography and tenuous gravity, less than one-thousandth that of Earth’s. The climax for MMX will come later in 2029, when the main spacecraft will attempt up to two landings on Phobos. The probe’s landing gear is designed to absorb the energy of each landing to mitigate the risk of tipping over or bouncing back into space, a real concern with the feeble and uneven gravity field on Phobos. The spacecraft will stay on Phobos’ surface for several hours, using two independent mechanisms to collect samples from the Martian moon. A coring drill on the end of the probe’s robotic arm will scoop out a cigar-shaped specimen, while a NASA-provided pneumatic sampler on one of the craft’s landing legs will fire a cartridge of high-pressure nitrogen gas to sweep bits of Phobos in a collection canister. All told, scientists expect MMX to retrieve at least 10 grams, or one-third of an ounce, of material from Phobos. After climbing back into space, MMX will discard its landing gear and fire its engines in 2030 to head for Earth, where it will guide a sample return capsule toward a remote landing zone in South Australia in 2031. Scientists will recover the samples for analysis in laboratories in Japan. US researchers will get 15 percent of the Phobos specimens in return for NASA’s contributions to the MMX mission, which include one of the spacecraft’s samplers and a gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer to measure the elemental makeup of the Martian moons. “One of the most intriguing questions about Phobos and Deimos is simply why these two little moons are there in the first place,” said Tom Statler, lead scientist for solar system small bodies at NASA. “In some ways, they resemble asteroids from the outer part of the asteroid belt, so they could be asteroids that were captured by Mars’s gravity. But there are other lines of evidence pointing to these moons being remnants of a giant impact early in Mars’s history.” “By going to the Martian moons, studying them up close, and bringing samples back to Earth, we’ll be able to determine whether Phobos material is more consistent with an asteroid composition or with debris created by a collision involving Mars,” Statler said. Phobos is roughly 40 times closer to Mars than our Moon is to Earth. Its proximity to Mars means there is a chance MMX might scoop up traces of the red planet itself that rained down on Phobos after its formation or capture. This could offer a taste of what’s to come when scientists finally get their hands on specimens directly from the surface of Mars, a significantly more difficult undertaking than a sample return from Phobos. The reconnaissance of Phobos by MMX could also help future mission planners consider whether the moon is a suitable location for human exploration, perhaps as a stopover between Earth and Mars, Kawakatsu said. MMX is not the first mission to attempt a sample return from Phobos. Russia’s Phobos-Grunt sample return mission failed soon after launch in 2011, before it could set off for Mars. The Soviet Union launched two spacecraft to Mars equipped with Phobos landers in 1988, but ground teams lost contact with both spacecraft before they completed their missions. With MMX, scientists hope to finally acquire the ground truth on the Martian moons they have sought for decades. “Basically, these objects look more like primitive asteroids than anything else, but the data is coming from remote sensing from far away,” said Patrick Michel, a French asteroid scientist and co-leader of the Idefix rover project. “That cannot really tell us exactly what are the properties, as we can do with a sample return in the lab.” “The goal of the mission is effectively to reveal the origin of the Martian moons,” Michel said. “If we understand the origin, that will tell us something about the early phases of the Solar System.”

Pennsylvania’s health department issued a health alert Monday as cases of measles in pregnancy and, in turn, cases of congenital measles have continued to increase. Before the current outbreak—which continues to rage across the state—congenital measles cases hadn’t been seen in decades, the department noted in its advisory. The alert included detailed clinical guidance for managing measles exposures and cases during pregnancy as well as cases in newborns. Many doctors have never seen such cases in their careers given the decades of successful use of the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine. But now, with vaccination rates slipping nationwide, the highly infectious virus is better able to get footholds in communities and spread rapidly through under-vaccinated pockets, as is happening in Pennsylvania. As of October 19, Pennsylvania had reported 1,286 measles cases this year across 42 counties. Of those, 247 have been hospitalized, and six people have reportedly died (though the latest reported death is still pending an investigation by the state health department before it is added to the state tally). In a response to a request for comment, a spokesperson for Pennsylvania’s health department said officials have confirmed that 66 of the state’s measles cases were in pregnant women. The department does not keep a specific tally of congenital measles cases because the definition of these cases is not currently standardized. However, it noted that that it does track cases by age, and 157 cases have been recorded in children under the age of 4. At least one case of congenital measles led to a death; the first death reported in the current outbreak was in a newborn baby boy, who died immediately after birth while his mother was severely ill with measles. Pregnant people who have not been vaccinated against measles nor previously had the infection are susceptible. A case of measles during pregnancy increases the risk of hospitalization, pneumonia, the need for oxygen support or mechanical ventilation, and death. It also increases the risk of miscarriage, stillbirth, low birth weight, and preterm labor. The MMR vaccine is not recommended during pregnancy because it is a live attenuated vaccine and poses a theoretical risk of causing an infection in a fetus. If a pregnant person is exposed to the virus, they’re recommended to receive intravenous immunoglobulin to help protect against infection—but only if it is given within six days of exposure. If there’s a measles infection during pregnancy, the measles virus can pass to a fetus in utero or can transmit during or just after birth, leading to a congenital case. These cases can vary widely in symptoms and severity. Some babies will remain asymptomatic while others develop severe, multisystem disease and death. Some common symptoms of congenital measles include fever and jaundice. They can also develop the telltale measles rash, but it is sometimes delayed or atypical given a newborn’s underdeveloped immune system. Other symptoms can include low platelet counts, pneumonia, and neurologic complications. Even if a newborn survives the acute infection, they are at risk of long-term complications. These include immune amnesia, a type of immunosuppression in which the measles virus destroys immune cells and adaptive responses. They are also at risk of subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE), a progressive, fatal neurological disorder that can strike years after an infection. The alert warns that babies with congenital measles need long-term clinical follow-up and monitoring for these neurologic and immunologic complications, and parents need counseling on how to spot early signs of SSPE, which include seizures, regression in milestones, and academic difficulties in older children. A Russian military satellite maneuvered in “extreme proximity” of a commercial Earth-imaging satellite in recent days in the first known example an unsanctioned rendezvous in low-Earth orbit, according to orbital intelligence company LeoLabs. The Russian spacecraft, Kosmos 2612, “conducted a deliberate series of maneuvers toward ICEYE-X36, a US-operated commercial imaging satellite,” LeoLabs said. “Kosmos 2612 closed from hundreds of kilometers away to extreme proximity to ICEYE-X36. It then maneuvered around ICEYE-X36, remained nearby for an extended period, and then began moving away.” US military officials and independent analysts have tracked Kosmos 2612 since its April launch on a Soyuz rocket from a spaceport in northern Russia. Within weeks, it became apparent that at least four of the six satellites from that launch were stalking ICEYE-X36 after they adjusted orbits to line up with the orbit of the ICEYE spacecraft. The situation remained stable until last week, when Kosmos 2612 began rapidly moving toward ICEYE-X36. By Friday, Kosmos 2612 was holding station 2 to 3 kilometers away before shifting positions ahead of and behind the ICEYE satellite, LeoLabs said. The satellites were within 500 meters (1,640 feet) of one another over the weekend, and “likely quite a bit closer than that,” said Adam Marsh, LeoLabs’ vice president of technical solutions. LeoLabs operates a global network of radars to track objects as small as 2 centimeters, about the size of a marble, in low-Earth orbit. The proximity of Kosmos 2612 and ICEYE-X36 “occasionally made it difficult to distinguish the two spacecraft independently,” LeoLabs said. “This was not a routine conjunction or ‘fly by,’” LeoLabs said. “The sequence included a controlled approach, changes in relative position, close-proximity loitering, additional small maneuvers, and a controlled departure. The behavior is consistent with sophisticated rendezvous and proximity operations (RPO).” Satellites often fly near one another in orbit, usually by happenstance and at high closing velocities. Russia has previously deployed satellites in orbits close to the US government’s most sophisticated and secret spy satellites. The difference this time is that Russia’s target was a commercial satellite. Kosmos 2612 got much closer to ICEYE-X36, then stayed a while. While stationed in Cold War–era Germany, Army infantryman Floyd Johnson experienced a training exercise that turned deadly. Decades later, while incarcerated in Florida, he was finally diagnosed with service-connected post-traumatic stress disorder from his experience. The Department of Veterans Affairs assigned him a 70 percent disability rating, later increasing it to 80 percent on appeal. However, because Johnson is incarcerated, the VA only pays his disability compensation at a 10 percent rate under a federal law governing benefits for incarcerated veterans. Johnson is challenging that law as unconstitutional. But first, the U.S. Supreme Court has to decide whether a district court will hear the case at all. At oral argument earlier this month, the government argued that it cannot. It said instead that Johnson must first pursue his claim through the VA’s administrative review process and then seek review in the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims. This process can take years and requires veterans to navigate multiple layers of review before reaching a court, which is problematic enough. Worse though, the conventional understanding is that VA administrative bodies cannot declare an act of Congress unconstitutional, so Johnson would have to spend years in waiting for guaranteed administrative denials before ever setting foot in a court. The dispute arises from the Veterans’ Judicial Review Act of 1988 and the Supreme Court’s decision in Johnson v. Robison. In 1974, the court held that an earlier statute restricting judicial review of veterans’ benefits decisions did not prevent federal district courts from hearing constitutional challenges to a law governing veterans’ benefits. In response, Congress established the current system, routing most disputes through the VA and its specialized appellate courts. Johnson argues that in doing so, Congress did not eliminate district court jurisdiction over constitutional challenges to the statutes themselves. There are good reasons for specialized review systems. Veterans’ benefits law is governed by a complex constellation of federal statutes and regulations, and adjudicating claims often requires expertise in military records, medical evidence, and the connection between military service and disability. Congress established the current system to promote consistency and provide veterans with a dedicated process for challenging adverse decisions, but the benefits of specialized review are difficult to defend when a claimant must navigate years of administrative proceedings before a court can even consider resolving the legal question at the heart of the dispute. I know how much that delay can cost. I left the Army in 2009 with a general discharge under honorable conditions after behavior related to PTSD from my time in Iraq. Like it did Johnson, the government told me that I needed to go through a time-consuming administrative process with little chance of success before I could bring the case to a district court. About six years into trying to navigate that process with no success, by chance, I mentioned my case to the clinical professor in charge of the Veterans Legal Services Clinic at Yale Law School. The clinic took up my claim, bringing a class-action lawsuit challenging the Army’s handling of discharge upgrade applications. Even then, it took a small army of law students, professors, and pro bono attorneys, along with the direct intervention and oversight of the district court, for me to receive my honorable discharge and access to desperately needed benefits—nine years after being discharged with severe PTSD. As in Johnson’s case, the government moved to dismiss my lawsuit in part because I had not exhausted my administrative remedies. But my claim was that the administrative process itself was broken. The Army Discharge Review Board had repeatedly denied my applications without considering legal guidance from the Secretary of Defense to give liberal consideration to veterans whose service-connected mental health conditions contributed to their misconduct. I was told to keep pursuing the very process that I knew was not being conducted in accordance with the law. Fortunately, the judge disagreed with that demand. He rejected the government’s motion to dismiss and agreed to hear the case in district court. In response, the Army granted my discharge upgrade with no additional evidence and then moved to dismiss the class action because I had already been upgraded, which would have allowed it to continue illegally ignoring the secretary’s guidance. The judge again rejected the motion to dismiss, and then, only under the threat of further court intervention, the Army agreed to settle and allow new reviews for almost 60,000 veterans, now following the proper guidelines and giving liberal consideration to the impact of mental health conditions in each veteran’s conduct. The Army denied wrongdoing, but the settlement required it to reconsider cases and change procedures. Despite the Army’s earlier protestations, the sky didn’t fall when the court was allowed to get involved. Veterans just got another chance to have their cases fairly heard. Johnson’s case raises a different legal question since he is challenging the constitutionality of a statute rather than the administration of an existing policy, but my experience shows why courts should be wary of rules that require veterans to exhaust administrative remedies even when they allege that the system itself is failing them. Specialized processes can offer expertise and consistency, but they should not become barriers to judicial review when the costs of delay are high and the claimant’s legal argument cannot possibly be resolved through ordinary administrative decision making. The Supreme Court doesn’t need to dismantle the veterans’ review system to recognize that some cases warrant a more direct route to federal court. Johnson’s case asks whether Congress intended to foreclose district court review of constitutional challenges to veterans’ benefits laws. My case showed what can happen when the government insists that veterans keep navigating an administrative process even when that is the very system that they take legal issue with. Specialized review can provide expertise and consistency, but those benefits cannot justify making access to justice contingent on years of procedural hurdles. No court can give someone back years spent without healthcare, housing, or the education benefits they earned through military service and desperately need. Imagine your company just discovered electricity. At first only a few machines run on it, and nobody worries about efficiency — consumption is low and the value is obvious. But once electricity becomes infrastructure, every process depends on it, and the question changes from "can we use it?" to "how efficiently are we using it?" Enterprise AI is going through the same shift. What began as isolated chatbot and copilot experiments is becoming a foundational layer of the business, with models, agents, and data increasingly interconnected in production workloads. The important question stops being what a token or GPU-hour costs. It becomes how efficiently the AI environment converts paid capacity into useful business outcomes. That's the metric worth tracking: effective cost per useful outcome — the total platform, model, operations, and capacity cost required to complete work the business can actually use, whether a resolved request or a completed workflow. Utilization is central to the economics. Directing more work to paid-for capacity lowers effective unit cost; idle GPUs, fragmented workloads, and oversized models all raise the cost of every useful result. Enterprises generally choose among three models: A bespoke stack — procuring infrastructure and controls in-house. This approach is flexible, but it creates a continuing burden, since teams must revalidate the environment as models and policies change. On-demand consumption — hyperscalers reduce the assembly burden and suit low or uncertain demand, but charges rise with every prompt or agent action. An integrated private AI platform — owned or dedicated capacity paired with a validated stack. The advantage isn't free inference; it's control over efficiency levers like scheduling, model selection, GPU partitioning, and cache management. The right model depends on the workload. APIs can be rational for experimentation or variable demand. As workloads become sustained and business-critical, enterprises need to weigh not just what they consume, but how efficiently they operate the system. Buying a Formula 1 race car and parking it in your garage means owning one of the most sophisticated vehicles ever built — but ownership alone doesn't make you competitive; you still need drivers, mechanics, and track time to justify it. AI infrastructure works the same way. One of the biggest misconceptions in enterprise AI is that owning infrastructure automatically improves economics. Owning GPUs creates the opportunity for better economics, but only if the organization has sufficient demand and operational discipline to keep resources productive. The infrastructure that returns the best ROI isn't a reference architecture or a bespoke build-out — it's a fully integrated, qualified stack that lets teams route tasks to the least costly model that meets quality requirements and reuse context through techniques such as KV-cache management, reducing wasted capacity while maintaining responsiveness. Without these practices, committed infrastructure can become stranded capital. With them, rising demand improves economics as fixed costs are amortized across more useful work — increasingly important in agentic systems, where economics are shaped less by any single prompt than by the efficiency of the complete environment. Andrew Haverdink, a tall grad student in a rain bib and rubber boots, wrenched a double-handed drill from the frozen soil that underlies much of Alaska’s Denali National Park and then unclipped the hollow barrel from the end of the bit. He knelt in front of a blue tarp, lined the barrel up with a plastic tray, and pushed a rod through the opposite end. A perfect solid, cylindrical core of ancient soil emerged—dark, rich permafrost at least a foot long. On that late morning in early July, Haverdink was among a team of Northern Arizona University students and staff gathering samples and data from a research plot, divided by surveyor’s tape unspooled atop tussocks, alders, and fireweed. In late June of 2013, a lightning strike sparked a fire in this stretch of mostly treeless tundra, in the northern backcountry of the park. Whipped by the Chinooks, intense downslope winds created as moist ocean air surmounts and then descends the Alaska Range, the fire ripped across nearly 35,000 acres. It burned through much of the vegetation and deep into the topsoil, stripping away the insulating layer that preserves the frozen permafrost, a massive storehouse of carbon that underlies 85% of Alaska. During their first survey, in 2016, the researchers found that in places where the fire burned hottest and deepest, the thawed soil above the permafrost (known as the active layer) extended between a foot and nearly two feet deeper than in plots outside the burn scar—an indication of deep thawing. Crucially, the fire also crossed two sets of survey grids that the National Park Service established in 2001, including the one where the researchers stood that morning. That pre-fire snapshot offered the Northern Arizona group a rare opportunity to study how the blaze has altered the vegetation, carbon, and permafrost across time, says Xanthe Walker, one of the principal investigators on the project, who was watching over Haverdink’s work that morning. These direct observations from high-latitude fires are helping scientists to better understand the impacts of wildfire and permafrost loss on climate change, she says. “There’s a lot of uncertainty,” Walker says. “These are huge biomes with massive fires that no one’s ever been to or ever looked at.” They’re among a growing group of researchers striving to untangle what happens in the aftermath of fires in the Arctic-boreal region as they occur more often, burn hotter than before, and expose the landscape and frozen permafrost to a warming climate. The work is providing critical clues into the complex mechanisms driving cascading climate feedback effects: As warming temperatures fuel more frequent and severe fires, more permafrost thaws, releasing greenhouse-gas emissions that threaten to fuel more warming, fires, and permafrost loss. Understanding just how powerful these effects are, how they compound, and to what degree other transformations of the landscape may mitigate them is essential to gain a clearer picture of our climate future. Notably, past modeling suggested that the carbon dioxide uptake from expanding trees and plants may largely offset the release of carbon from warming soils and melting permafrost, at least over long periods and under moderate levels of warming. But the improving observations and analyses have revealed worrisome signs about the impacts of rising Arctic-boreal emissions in the immediate decades ahead, as the world struggles to keep global warming within relatively safe boundaries. Permafrost makes up 15% of the land surface of the Northern Hemisphere and holds about 1.5 trillion tons of carbon, roughly twice the amount in the atmosphere. Most of it formed during the last ice age, but it’s been thawing at an accelerating rate during the last half-century, amid rising temperatures and worsening fires. As it melts, microbes begin to break down that organic matter, releasing it into the air in the form of carbon dioxide and methane. Both are powerful greenhouse gases that fuel climate change. The impacts of permafrost loss, however, aren’t as straightforward as they may initially seem. Notably, there has been a long-running scientific debate over what would have the strongest effect on the exchange of greenhouse gases over the next few centuries: the loss of carbon in soil and permafrost or the carbon uptake from so-called “Arctic greening,” which describes the spread of carbon-absorbing plants across the landscape as temperatures rise. Thawing permafrost itself can contribute to the latter phenomenon as the unfrozen soil layer deepens and makes more nitrogen, phosphorus, and other nutrients accessible to plants. Carbon-sucking trees and shrubs with deeper root systems can take hold and thrive in places where they wouldn’t have been able to in the past. But as more Arctic-boreal fires ignite and more permafrost melts, researchers have been able to closely study what exactly happens in the aftermath, using on-site surveys like those conducted by the Northern Arizona University team, satellite observations, lidar flights, a widening network of greenhouse-gas-measuring towers, and other tools. The findings have alarmed many researchers. In particular, permafrost seems particularly prone to abrupt thawing rather than the gradual version that major climate models have simulated. A 2020 paper found that this could release far more carbon than previously understood, doubling the emissions from thawing permafrost and offsetting the carbon uptake from future plant growth. In addition, even if carbon levels eventually net out through Arctic greening, the emissions from high-latitude fires and permafrost loss appear to be accelerating warming right now. Susan Natali, an Arctic climate scientist at the Woodwell Climate Research Center, and her colleagues have published several papers in recent years concluding that the combined emissions from these feedback effects will push the planet past 2 °C faster than currently predicted. She and other researchers  also found that about 40% of the Arctic-boreal zone has already flipped from a carbon sink into a carbon source, when the emissions from permafrost melting, fires and other forces are taken into account. “The more we learn about processes like abrupt thaw, deeper-burning fires, and cold-season emissions, and the more we see disturbances increasing, the more likely it is that the sink will weaken and source areas will expand,” Natali said in an email. But there’s still considerable uncertainty about the differences between the ways fires and thawing permafrost might alter landscapes, under different circumstances in different biomes. “This is just a challenge of the science, because it's really hard to measure such a huge area, which is not one system,” she says. “It’s many, many, many, many different types of ecosystems.” The Northern Arizona University researchers have been at the forefront of efforts to study the aftermath of intensifying fires across different parts of Alaska. Over the last two decades, Michelle Mack, a professor of ecosystem ecology who was previously Walker’s postdoctoral advisor, has overseen repeat survey work on fire sites across forests and tundras, from the relatively warm southern interior to the frigid North Slope. The local climate and ecosystem, the severity of the fires that have occurred, the type of permafrost, and numerous other factors can substantially shift the uptake or loss of carbon, the amount of permafrost that’s thawed, the extent to which it may recover, and the future flammability of the landscape. In one early study, the researchers and their collaborators surveyed dozens of stands dominated by black spruce trees in the aftermath of a series of large boreal fires in 2004, between the Brooks Range and the Alaska Range. One of the notable findings: Severe burns that left little behind of the decaying organic matter in the topsoil induced a shift toward faster-growing and less flammable deciduous trees. The sites where deciduous trees dominated stored about five times as much soil carbon as stands that returned to black spruce, Mack, Walker, and their coauthors noted in a 2021 Science paper. That means the subsequent uptake of carbon on those stands “more than compensated” for the amount released in the initial fire, Walker says. But the 2007 Anaktuvuk River tundra fire, which remains the largest fire on record in Alaska’s North Slope, offers a starkly different case study. In the initial analysis there, Mack, Schuur, and their coauthors found that the fire released more than 2 million tons of carbon to the atmosphere, basically negating the amount that tundra ecosystems around the world removed from the air that year. Subsequent studies found that more than a third of the scorched landscape sank, slid, and crumpled as the ice wedges below collapsed, the telltale signs of abrupt permafrost melting. Nearly two decades later, much of that thawed, decaying organic matter continues to release carbon dioxide and methane in the most severely burned patches of the fire scar, says Adrian Rocha, an associate professor at the University of Notre Dame. It will take decades to centuries to recover all the carbon lost there, if ever happens at all, according to Rocha, who continues to monitor the site using Eddy covariance flux towers, which measure the exchange of greenhouse gases between Earth and atmosphere. Mack says these on-the-ground measurements are critical to understanding what sorts of ecosystems recover, which shift into new states, whether they release a lot of carbon or a little, and when and where plant growth can offset that.That said, the stakes of thawing permafrost are clear. “If we step back as ecosystem ecologists, we know that there’s a lot more carbon stored below ground in permafrost than there is above ground in vegetation,” she says. “If permafrost carbon is released, it could swamp all of these other interesting details.” The Northern Arizona University research team shipped a pallet of soil samples back from Denali. The material included plugs of moss and decaying plant matter known as “brownies,” as well as permafrost cores and samples of the unfrozen soil layer. In the months since, they’ve cut, dried, weighed, combusted and analyzed various samples and inputted their field and lab findings into databases. Walker says they’re exploring a variety of research questions, including how much that top organic layer has recovered since the initial post-fire survey and how much carbon has been lost and recovered since the fire. The analysis is still underway, but in the most severely burned sites, it appears that the thawed layer of soil above the permafrost remain deeper some 13 years. “Thaw still reaches further into the soil each summer than it probably did before the fire, which exposes more soil to decomposition,” Walker explained in an email. They’re now hoping to use lab analyses to determine how much of that carbon is being lost. On my last afternoon in Healy, Alaska, I followed Schuur along a narrow walkway of wooden boards suspended above a tundra field just north of Denali, hoping to get a glimpse into our climate future. In 2008, he and his research team devised a way to subject permafrost to the temperature levels expected two or three decades from now by setting up snow fences around dozens of designated research plots. That allowed extra snow to build up—which, counterintuitively, acts like a blanket, adding thermal insulation during the winter that kept the test plots warmer than the controls. Over the course of the 13-year experiment, the soil carbon levels in those 10-by-10-meter plots declined by 23%, Schuur and his coauthors found. The land collapsed in many of the sites, forming craters several feet deep. As I stepped heel-to-toe along the planks into the middle of one plot, I descended about four feet, experiencing subsidence in three dimensions. Looking up at the edge from the walkway, I felt as if I were diaphragm deep in an empty pool. Schuur, though, had another thing to show me. As we crested a ridge between plots, he pointed down to a shallower—though no less obvious—pool on the other side. It was a control site that had melted and collapsed on its own, without any assistance from simulated warming or fires. “Every plot is just going off in its own trajectory,” he said. “This stuff is happening because the whole place is warming … and all these changes just go faster if you first burn the surface.” Schuur expects that the permafrost underlying the whole field will thaw deeply by the end of 2040. “There won’t be any surface permafrost based on our current warming,” he says. Unless the world radically cuts emissions and gets fires under control, this same phenomenon will creep up the state, decade by decade, until even the permafrost under Alaska’s North Slope sinks to similar depths and gives up the carbon it has held for millennia.
