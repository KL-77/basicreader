Top Republican lawmakers have asked Jensen Huang to appear before Congress to answer questions after it was reported that US intelligence officials believe Nvidia’s chief executive has been targeted by a Chinese spying operation. The chairs of the House committees on China, foreign affairs, and intelligence wrote to Huang this week asking him to appear before a joint closed-door briefing for their committees on November 18. “Recent reports raise significant national security concerns regarding the People’s Republic of China’s access to sensitive US technology and information,” representatives John Moolenaar, Brian Mast, and Rick Crawford wrote. “The issue of PRC infiltration and access to cutting-edge US technology is core to the investigative work of our three committees this Congress. It is vital that Congress determines the extent to which US national security has been placed at risk,” the lawmakers added. The New York Times last week wrote that US intelligence officials believed Huang had been the target of a Chinese spy operation. The concerns center on a Chinese woman named Alice Huang, an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based cloud company that has bought billions of dollars of Nvidia’s chips. Nvidia has rejected the claims and on Thursday would not say if Huang would appear before Congress. “Meetings with customers are part of a CEO’s job. The New York Times said Huang had not received a “defensive briefing,” which the intelligence community usually, but not always, provides potential espionage targets to warn them about the activity. Elon’s Edsel is no more. Last night Tesla CEO Elon Musk revealed on social media that his car company is calling time on its failed attempt to wrest control of the American pickup truck market. The Cybertruck will cease production in the middle of 2027, liberating that space in Tesla’s cavernous factory for other uses—more Cybercabs, most likely. Musk first revealed the Cybertruck in late 2019, back when Tesla was still trading at a theoretically understandable price-to-earning ratio. The pickup’s unusual angular shape owed more to low-res video game models than any concessions to practicality. At the time Musk made fanciful claims that the truck would use a stainless steel monocoque construction, telling his audience that “the body and bed don’t do anything useful” on a normal truck. While some race cars use carbon-fiber monocoques for strength and lightness, making a pickup truck with a stainless steel monocoque chassis would only make sense if someone had an awful lot of stainless steel lying around and needed something to do with it all. The truck was supposed to go on sale for less than $40,000, and Tesla made its usual stratospheric sales predictions that (had they been accurate) the bare metal EVs would be as ubiquitous as the Model 3 and Y. But development of the Cybertruck was more troubled than usual for Tesla, which struggled heavily to bring both the Model 3 and Y into mass production. Musk demanded “Lego-like precision” from the workers assembling the trucks, but achieving the 10-micron tolerances required by the Danish toy company was not evidently successful, based on what we saw when we experienced our first Cybertruck in reality. That was in 2023, when Cybertruck headlines were mostly about recalls and stop sales. By then, the truck used a conventional unibody chassis—conventional for Tesla, not for full-size pickup trucks. Toward the end of that year, Musk told his audience that “we dug our grave” with the truck, perhaps the first public acknowledgment that annual sales of a quarter of a million Cybertrucks probably wasn’t going to happen after all. Sales never took off, and the divisive shape of the Cybertruck became an all-too visible reminder of the CEO’s role in reshaping the federal government to his liking. Even selling thousands of Cybertrucks to SpaceX couldn’t save the pickup. Now, with America having vociferously rejected Musk’s vision of governance, those wishing to drive something that reminds everyone around them of that vision will only have a few months more to purchase one.

KENNEDY SPACE CENTER, Florida—Blue Origin announced two significant upgrades to its New Glenn rocket on Thursday, reducing the cost of manufacturing the vehicle and expanding its capacity for payload delivery to geostationary orbit and the Moon. With these upgrades, the company aims to eventually position New Glenn to launch more than 1,000 times a year. “There has never been higher demand for launch, ever,” Dave Limp, chief executive officer of Blue Origin, said during the UP.Summit conference at the Shuttle Landing Facility in Florida. Blue Origin has already begun test firing a new engine, called Northstar, that will eventually replace the BE-4 engine that powers the first stage of New Glenn. Separately, the company also said it was developing a hydrogen-oxygen third stage for the rocket, GS3, to give it an extra kick. The Northstar engine is a simplified but more powerful version of the BE-4 engine, Limp said. It has half the number of parts, 30 percent less mass, and about 65 percent can be 3D-printed. Blue Origin believes it can manufacture the engine at 25 percent of the cost of a BE-4 engine, and eventually reach the point where each engine can be assembled in less than a day. The company is deep into development and testing of Northstar, having already begun a test-firing campaign. “To get access to space at a lower cost, we have to look at every part of the vehicle,” Limp said. “This is going to be a game-changer.” The BE-4 engine powering New Glenn today has a thrust of 640,000 pounds, and Limp said Northstar will have a starting thrust of 715,000 pounds. Like the BE-4 engine, Northstar burns methane and liquid oxygen using an oxygen-rich staged combustion cycle and a single-shaft turbopump architecture. Limp said the company will continue to manufacture the existing BE-4 engine for United Launch Alliance, which uses two of the engines to power its Vulcan rocket. Blue Origin is developing a third stage for New Glenn called GS3 (creatively named Glenn Stage 3) to offer direct injection to geostationary orbit and Earth-escape trajectories. The third stage fits within an interstage, preserving the full fairing volume for customer payloads. The stage can fly on both the smaller 7×2 variant (as in seven first stage engines and two second stage engines) of New Glenn as well as the larger 9×4 variant. According to Blue Origin, a GS3-equipped New Glenn 7×2 can carry 8 metric tons directly to geostationary orbit and 10 metric tons to translunar injection. Those figures increase to 14 tons and 20 tons, respectively, for the larger 9×4 variant. As the rocket’s third stage, it needs to be light, Limp said. For this reason, the third stage is made mostly of composites, which are high-performing and lightweight. But there’s a problem with that. The stage will be powered by the company’s BE-7 engine, already used by its lunar landers. This engine is optimized for in-space operations and uses liquid hydrogen and oxygen propellants. But hydrogen is the smallest atom and therefore leaky. With composites, thermal cycling and mechanical strain can cause cracking, exacerbating losses of molecular hydrogen. Limp said the company was not sure a large composite tank, 5.5-meter diameter in this case, could be constructed that would contain hydrogen at high pressures. The company recently had a breakthrough at Launch Site One in Texas, when it completed its first composite liquid hydrogen tanking test. During the test, the tank was pressurized to 100 percent of the maximum force expected during operation for 2.5 hours. It was even successfully tested at 150 percent of this maximum pressure for 10 minutes. Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-05

Visit Original Website
Devices running some configurations of Alpine Linux, and possibly other distributions, are affected by a decade-old vulnerability that allows attackers to log into devices with no password. The vulnerability exists in the interaction between OpenSSH, the code library implementing SSH connections, and musl, a lightweight alternative to the libc library that, unlike most other Linux distributions, is used in Alpine. Further, OpenSSH using musl (pronounced muscle) must not use PAM (pluggable authentication modules), a framework that handles user authentication and account-access checks. Alpine is a lightweight, widely used Linux distribution. Other platforms may be affected if they use the same configurations. There are two other conditions necessary for successful exploitation. First, a system must have at least one user account that’s accessible using a password that is hashed using the SHA256 or SHA512 functions. Second, the target system account must be configured to be passwordless, by way of specifying a “*” in the password field in /etc/shadow, the Linux file where password hashes are stored. Passwordless accounts are commonly used for accounts that use certificate-based authentication. This setting is common for certain types of accounts. When all the conditions are met, the consequences are high. “An attacker who can reach your SSH port can log in without knowing any password,” Alpine maintainers said Thursday. “It takes one connection, works every time, and needs no retries.” When logging into a passwordless account, the attacker can gain access by inputting more than 256 characters in the password field. Security firm Checkpoint said that the use of the SHA256 or SHA512 functions “matters because sshd borrows that account’s salt. Without it, the attack fails.”  Company researchers said their testing also showed that the account must be active, not just hold a hash. Checkpoint further reported that root accounts are safe under the default PermitRootLogin prohibit password setting, though reachable accounts with doas or sudo rights can still become root. Despite the prerequisites, *sshole—the vulnerability name given by the person who discovered it—likely threatens large numbers of systems. The Docker Hub base image for Alpine has more than 1 billion downloads. Alpine has long been popular in routers, smart TVs, industrial controllers, and other types of embedded devices, many of which can’t be easily updated, if at all. More recently, Alpine has been adopted for use in desktops and servers. Alpine is also used in many container images. According to security firm Detectify, Shodan lists around 22 million systems with SSH open on port 22. Only a fraction meet the conditions necessary for exploitation, but given the ease and speed of testing each one, locating vulnerable devices won’t require much effort or time. It’s not entirely clear where the vulnerability resides. OpenSSH, which issued an update Thursday, said: “We consider the root cause of this bug to be outside sshd, but it is exploitable via sshd and can be avoided there.” Musl developer Rich Felker, meanwhile, wrote: “It is my position that this is not a bug in musl just an erroneous use by the caller.” Felker went on to say that musl developers have wanted to make changes to the mechanism “for a while now” and planned to do so in an upcoming release. Alpine has also issued an update. It’s not yet known what other packages may be vulnerable. The person who discovered *sshole said OpenWRT is affected, but by default, that OS uses the Dropbear package, not OpenSSH, for SSH connections (though maintainers provide instructions for installing the latter). The discoverer also names the packages buildroot and yocto as being affected, but that isn’t yet confirmed. Despite the uncertainty, anyone using a Linux system that uses both musl and OpenSSH should take time to immediately investigate if they’re affected. A good starting point is here. Both Alpine and OpenSSH patches should be installed on all affected systems. There is no known CVE designation for the vulnerability.

Almost every plan to lower food prices ends up putting the government in one of two businesses, either running a grocery store, as New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani is now doing, or telling grocery stores what to charge. A new proposal from the Kitchen Table Project does neither, which is why I’ve spent more time with it than I expected to. Instead of working on what shoppers pay, it works on what the store pays. The federal government already buys an enormous amount of food. USDA runs competitive auctions to supply school lunch and other nutrition programs, and it has been doing so for decades. The proposal would let any grocer, whether a bodega in the Bronx or a Kroger in Lansing, buy a basket of 20 staples at a similar auction price. The one condition is that the store has to sell them at a markup that’s set in advance and posted where customers can see it. This is like a Costco membership where Washington is the buying club and the grocer is the member. The government never owns a store, never runs a warehouse, and never writes a check to cover the gap between what food costs and what a politician would like it to cost. The report estimates a family of four would save about $315 a year. I’m more sympathetic to this than I usually am to grocery proposals, and the reason is purchasing power. Small stores pay more for the same case of eggs because they buy fewer cases. Price surveys consistently show the big discounters undercutting most supermarkets, and the Atlanta Fed has found that food inflation ran hottest in lower-income metro areas, where shoppers have fewer stores to choose from. If a corner store could buy at something close to Walmart’s price, that’s a form of relief neither a price cap nor Mamdani’s city-run supermarkets can offer. New York is paying for a 30 percent discount at five stores, while this proposal would reach every store that signs up. And since the markup is public, shoppers would have a number to hold their regular supermarket against. This also gives other supermarkets a reason to watch their prices. But for six of the 20 items, which include eggs, milk and ground beef, the report shows no savings at all. I don’t think that’s a flaw in the design so much as a reminder of where food prices come from. Ground beef is close to $7 a pound because the U.S. cattle herd is the smallest it has been in 75 years. No auction or purchasing plan, however large, produces more cows. I’d also bet against the federal government out-negotiating Walmart and Costco, which have spent decades on procurement and are very good at it. If Washington simply matched what the big chains pay, that would still be a meaningful help to independents, and it’s worth doing. It’s just a smaller promise than the headline number suggests. Which brings everything back to the markup. The report doesn’t say who sets it. Rent and wages in the Bronx are not rent and wages in Lansing, and a single national margin could end up either squeezing out the stores the program is meant to help or leaving so little room that the discount barely shows up on the shelf. The chains that already get good terms may look at the paperwork and decide it isn’t worth it. There’s a second-order problem too. A grocer selling 20 items at a capped margin still has to make payroll, and the obvious way to do that is to increase prices on the other several thousand items in the store. A family could save on the program staples, lose it on everything else, and never know the difference. Even so, this is the first grocery proposal I’ve seen in a while that tries to lower prices through competition rather than by having taxpayers absorb the difference. That alone makes it worth piloting. Name the markup. Say who can join. And then judge it by what a family actually saves on the whole cart, not on a handful of staples. In academic discourse, use of the correct name is an important means of establishing professional credibility. For example, if within the fields of computer science and information science, a specific term of art, such as artificial intelligence, has gained widespread acceptance, the use of an alternative term—a term, like superintelligence, not widely accepted within the discipline—would seriously undermine the credibility of academic work (and the author’s more general standing within the field). President Donald Trump has notoriously attempted to rechristen the Gulf of Mexico as the “Gulf of America.” So too has he attempted to rename Lake Ontario to “Lake America,” and he is threatening to fashion new MAGA names for an ocean, a major river, and the state of New Mexico. Trump also recently signed an executive order requiring all federal government programs and communications to use the terms superintelligence and S.I. rather than the broadly accepted nomenclature artificial intelligence and A.I. One might shrug all of this off and say, “Well, so what? It’s a free country, and all of us can still use the generally accepted names of places (Gulf of Mexico) and things (A.I.).” Unfortunately, after Oct. 1, 2026, this is no longer the case for academics at state-sponsored colleges and universities in Alabama. H.B. 2 officially bans the use of the phrase Gulf of Mexico by any and all state employees. As a professor at the University of Alabama School of Law, I find this appalling. This bizarre law provides that “all state and local entities and their employees shall be required to … [r]efer to the body of water that borders the coast of Alabama as the ‘Gulf of America,’ ” including “in all newly created maps, documents, educational materials, websites, official communications, and other resources.” Section 3 contains a limited exception to this general rule: “Nothing in this section shall prohibit a public K–12 school or public institution of higher education from using the term ‘Gulf of Mexico’ within a larger course of academic instruction when reasonable for historical purposes.” Imagine, for a moment, a marine biologist at Auburn University preparing to present research at a major global academic conference on the marine mammals of the “Gulf of America”? Or a geologist at the University of Alabama writing about oil deposits in the “Gulf of America” in a scholarly research paper? H.B. 2 plainly renders unlawful the use of the term Gulf of Mexico in any and all professional endeavors, including teaching and scholarship, save for using the generally accepted name within national and international academic discourse solely for “historical purposes.” This is bad enough, of course, but future legislation could compel Alabama’s public college and university professors to abjure using Lake Ontario or artificial intelligence in both their classrooms and academic publications. Moreover, Trump’s “superintelligence” executive order significantly raises the stakes for compelled academic speech and does so on a nationwide basis. The federal government sponsors billions of dollars of academic research annually. Now government-funded research into A.I. will presumably require grant applicants to use the term superintelligence in both their grant applications and subsequent published research. (It bears noting that the leadership of Facebook and X, Mark Zuckerberg and Elon Musk, dumped artificial intelligence in favor of superintelligence in recent quarterly earnings calls with investors.) Efforts to control speech in Alabama’s educational institutions constitute just one part of a larger national effort to standardize speech and, by necessity, thought. Happily, however, the First Amendment not only protects the use of particular words but also extends to a person’s choice of language. Government efforts to compel speech are facially inconsistent with the First Amendment. Cohen v. California, which blocked California’s effort to criminalize the use of the word fuck in public, clearly establishes this rule. Writing for the Cohen majority, Justice John Marshall Harlan explained, “We cannot indulge the facile assumption that one can forbid particular words without also running a substantial risk of suppressing ideas in the process.” The power to control words involves the power to control thought, and “governments might soon seize upon the censorship of particular words as a convenient guise for banning the expression of unpopular views.” Cohen squarely forecloses government efforts to ban the use of particular words—presumably including Gulf of Mexico, Lake Ontario, and artificial intelligence. Under the First Amendment, neither the federal nor state government possesses the constitutional authority to put words in the mouths of citizens. Instead, the First Amendment “is designed and intended to remove governmental restraints from the arena of public discussion, putting the decision as to what views shall be voiced largely into the hands of each of us,” and this holds true “because governmental officials cannot make principled distinctions in this area.” What is true generally for all Americans has special force in the context of education. As Justice Felix Frankfurter explained, in his concurring opinion in Wieman v. Updegraff, “to regard teachers—in our entire educational system, from the primary grades to the university—as the priests of our democracy is therefore not to indulge in hyperbole.” But teachers, at all levels of our educational system, “cannot carry out their noble task if the conditions for the practice of a responsible and critical mind are denied to them.” Accordingly, “they must have the freedom of responsible inquiry, by thought and action, into the meaning of social and economic ideas, into the checkered history of social and economic dogma … to assure which the freedoms of thought, of speech, of inquiry, of worship are guaranteed by the Constitution of the United States against infraction by National or State government.” Forcing computer scientists to use the term superintelligence in federally sponsored research or environmental scientists to use Gulf of America in their teaching and scholarship squarely violates both general free speech principles and the more specific First Amendment protection afforded to academic freedom. This provision was added to the state constitution in 1879, as an alternative to a proposal to forbid the governor from using their clemency power in cases where an offender had committed more than one felony. As the robots clean, they collect data that Tau will use to train up their more independent descendants. Every counter successfully wiped and T-shirt successfully folded will help the models that power the robots learn to do better on the next go-round. Because this data is so valuable to Tau, the company charges less for cleaning than one might expect for such a cutting-edge product.
