Meta shocked by $25B fine after N.M. refused to join multistate settlement

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-22

Meta must pay $25 billion to New Mexico after a jury found the tech giant guilty of nearly 44 million violations of state consumer protection laws. The largest fine to ever hit Meta came after New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez declined to join an $18 billion settlement Meta reached earlier this year with attorneys general in nearly all states. In his opinion Wednesday, US district court judge Francis J. Mathew said there was no “bright line rule” for determining the appropriate penalty for Meta’s repeated and willful deception of users since 2010 about their “control of data, third-party data misuse, misinformation, hate speech, and equal enforcement.” New Mexico had asked the court to impose even higher fines, up to $40 billion. The entire state of 2 million people were harmed by Meta’s conduct, Torrez argued, and it seemed dubious that Meta would make meaningful changes unless the penalty impacted Meta’s stock price. Mathew found that the trial record—which showed that top leadership, including Mark Zuckerberg, were aware that the company, then known as Facebook, was making misleading public representations—supported “the highest level of penalty allowed by the NW Unfair Practices Act.” “The jury was unequivocal: Facebook systematically misled the public” and “took advantage of the public’s lack of knowledge respecting Facebook’s practices to a grossly unfair degree,” Mathew wrote. To Mathew, it seemed that Meta made a choice to avoid transparency with users in a bid to capitalize on data they didn’t realize they were sharing and better compete with one of its main rivals, the platform then known as Twitter. “The violations the jury found were not the product of a single misleading statement or isolated incident,” Mathew noted. “They arose from repeated public representations over more than 11 years, concerning multiple core aspects of Facebook’s platform and business.” Meta had hoped that New Mexico’s penalty would be more in line with a $5 billion settlement the company made with the Federal Trade Commission to address the Cambridge Analytica fallout. At most, they expected to pay $3.4 billion, while arguing that anything more would be “unconstitutional,” AP News reported. But Mathew said that the FTC settlement “cannot be viewed in a vacuum” while noting that so far, “penalties, consent agreements, and decrees have not deterred Meta from using deceptive trade practices” since the Cambridge Analytica scandal was first exposed. “To induce users to provide their personal information to Facebook, Facebook made many false promises respecting the security and control of that data over the course of more than a decade,” Mathew wrote. In a press release, Torrez celebrated the ruling, calling it “a historic day for New Mexico and for consumers across the country.” “For too long, powerful technology companies have operated as though their size and influence put them beyond the reach of the law,” Torrez said. “A New Mexico jury found nearly 44 million violations of our consumer protection statute, and this penalty order demonstrates that those violations carry real consequences. We took on one of the largest companies in the world because New Mexicans deserve honesty, accountability, and the protection of their personal information. Today’s result makes clear that no corporation is too powerful to be held accountable.” A Meta spokesperson confirmed to Ars that the company plans to appeal. Legal experts told Reuters the company has a decent chance at slashing the fine on appeal based on constitutional claims. On top of likely claiming that the penalty is unconstitutional, Meta also feels that “this ruling, and the Attorney General’s approach to enforcement, makes it all but impossible to do business in the state of New Mexico,” the spokesperson said. The eye-popping fine following the multistate settlement appears to be making the company uncomfortable, despite critics claims that New Mexico’s penalty is just a “drop in the bucket” for a company that makes $200 billion annually. On Thursday, the Verge highlighted Meta’s third-quarter earnings report, which showed the company paid close to $10 billion for the multistate settlement. Without that expense, Meta’s income from operations would have been $22 billion higher, the report said. The excessive penalties come as Meta attempts to reboot its struggling AI strategy, and Meta appeared to signal to Donald Trump’s administration to possibly support its appeal by nodding to its AI wing in its statement opposing the ruling. “At its core, this lawsuit seeks to impose a tax on speech to enforce new rules for what people can say online,” Meta’s spokesperson said. “We are confident we will prevail on appeal. In the meantime, Meta will continue to build a safe and free platform to connect millions of Americans, grow small businesses, and help the country maintain its competitive edge.” Nidhi Hegde, executive director of a watchdog nonprofit called the American Economic Liberties Project, told AP News that Meta users want more enforcers willing to take the company to trial rather than settle matters in ways that the FTC settlement suggested weren’t genuine. In his opinion, Mathew noted that the jury found that Meta internal messages and investigation results showed that “rather than seek to educate Facebook’s users… Zuckerberg chose instead to capitalize on that lack of understanding in order to manipulate users into sharing more of their personal information publicly.” In one message, Zuckerberg was found laying out a plan to deceive users, saying, “Give them a chance to change their privacy settings but obviously don’t encourage it.” The company also failed to notify users when apps misused their data, only flagging two of 2,000 unique apps associated with data misuse, Mathew said. Following its investigation, Meta’s “decision was deliberately made to narrow Facebook’s internal definition of data misuse so that Facebook would not have to notify as many people due to the operational burden of doing so,” he wrote. Additionally, Facebook continued knowingly profiting off of pages with misinformation and hate speech, Mathew wrote. Internal research found “substantial amounts of misleading, false, and possibly false political, health, and science content,” with Meta documenting a “greater concentration of false content in top performing advertising content than in top performing Facebook content.” Rather than share that research, Meta buried it, while also deceiving users in dozens of other ways, including by misrepresenting that the average Facebook users gets “equal treatment” to celebrities and influencers. While Meta claimed everyone was treated the same, one class of users received warning to remove violating content before strikes were applied and frequently got harmful or sketchy content decisions overturned in cases where most users would not. Hegde told AP News that she thinks as Meta and other company’s AI products grow more popular, it will be increasingly important for states to remain watchful and protect residents from unknown harms. “Right now, they are rolling out Muse, an AI assistant encouraging people to trust it with everything from their bank accounts to their medical information,” Hegde said. “Which is why we need more enforcers with courage and laws with actual teeth to rein them in.” Torrez vowed to keep watch in New Mexico and to continue using the blueprint from the Meta case to enforce consumer protection laws and prevent tech giants’ misuse of data. “This case has always been about more than one company or one scandal,” Torrez said. “It is about whether corporations can mislead consumers about what happens to their personal information without facing meaningful consequences. New Mexico stood its ground, took this case to trial, and secured a verdict establishing millions of violations of our law. This outcome reinforces a fundamental principle, companies that do business in our state must follow our laws and respect the rights of our people.”

US-backed vaccine trial on babies canceled after international outcry

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-22

Visit Original Website
Government officials in Guinea-Bissau have formally rejected a clinical trial that would be run by Danish researchers and funded by the US to look for alleged nonspecific harms from the newborn dose of the hepatitis B vaccine. The vaccine dose is currently recommended globally for all newborns and has been used safely and effectively for decades. In an email to Ars Technica, one of the trial’s lead researchers, Christine Stabell Benn of Bandim Health Project and the University of Southern Denmark, said that Guinea-Bissau’s Council of Ministers “formally reaffirmed its non-acceptance of the study on 16 October.” The revelation that the study was formally rejected last week comes after news on Monday that judicial police in Guinea-Bissau raided Bandim Health’s offices and seized vaccines. Stabell Benn highlighted that the police search came after the trial was rejected. Of the trial’s rejection, Stabell Benn wrote: “We regret the decision because we considered the study scientifically and ethically justified, but we respect the government’s authority to decide whether research may proceed in the country.” The World Health Organization and many outside experts have sharply disagreed with Stabell Benn and her colleagues on the trial’s justifications and design. It has been widely decried as “unethical” because it would withhold the newborn dose of hepatitis B vaccine from half of the participants. With the country’s high rates of chronic hepatitis B infections, this would put thousands of babies at risk of developing a lifelong infection that can lead to liver cirrhosis, cancer, and premature death. Experts have further said that there is no scientific justification for the study, given the vaccine’s long use and strong safety record. The WHO wrote in February that the trial “posits hypothetical safety outcomes without sufficient credible evidence” to justify putting newborns at risk. Outrage erupted over the trial after the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention announced in December that it had awarded an unsolicited grant of $1.6 million to Bandim Health, which Stabell Benn runs with her husband Peter Aaby. The researchers are known for pursuing studies on nonspecific harms from vaccines and have been lauded by anti-vaccine advocates, including US Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy. But Aaby and Stabell Benn’s scientific peers have been critical of the quality of their work; the researchers are currently under investigation by Danish authorities for alleged scientific misconduct in their previous studies. Their hepatitis B vaccine trial was reportedly halted in January by Guinea-Bissau authorities. But outcry erupted again after a report that the US CDC, under Kennedy, had once set another $1.6 million aside to fund the trial and intended to move forward with it. Stabell Benn and colleagues have publicly lamented their tie to Kennedy. They have also consistently argued that their hepatitis B study is not unethical because Guinea-Bissau does not currently offer routine hepatitis B vaccine doses to newborns. Therefore, newborns not given the vaccine in the trial wouldn’t have received it outside the trial anyway. The country—which experienced a coup in November 2025—plans to introduce a birth dose in 2028, but it lacks the resources and infrastructure to do so now. Outside experts, including the WHO, point to ethical guidelines, such as the Declaration of Helsinki, which require trials to provide the “best proven intervention” to participants, regardless of the current standard in the location where the trial is conducted. For hepatitis B prevention, the best proven prevention would be the newborn dose of the vaccine, which is globally recommended to be given within the first 24 hours of life. As the WHO wrote explicitly: “Resource constraints cannot be used to justify withholding proven care in a research study involving people.” Stabell Benn and colleagues appear impervious to this point. In a LinkedIn post Wednesday, Frederik Schaltz-Buchholzer, a Bandim Health researcher involved in the trial, blamed the backlash on the “Anglophone media” that “framed [the trial] as dangerous because there would be a control group that would have followed the current vaccine schedule” in Guinea-Bissau. Schaltz-Buchholzer’s post went on to claim that the media controversy has led to distrust of the hepatitis B vaccine in Guinea-Bissau and fears that the vaccine would pose a danger to newborns vaccinated in the trial, which he referred to as the intervention group, writing: In the Bissau-Guinean context, it was widely understood that this trial was dangerous for the intervention group. For instance, rumours spread on social media with an omnipresent desconfiança (mistrust) that this was actually a novel vaccine to be tested by the Americans. Based on the Anglophone media coverage framing the trial as dangerous, Bissau-Guinean influencers speculated that the reason to test the hepatitis B vaccine in Guinea-Bissau was that the vaccine is dangerous, and that Bissau-Guinean newborns are disposable. In her email to Ars Technica, Stabell Benn declined to say what reasons Guinea-Bissau officials gave for why the trial was rejected or why their office was searched and vaccine doses were seized. Ars Technica could not immediately reach Guinea-Bissau officials for comment. But local distrust of the researchers could have played a role in the rejection and police operation. Stabell Benn said police were directed to investigate the vaccine doses directly, saying: In its communiqué, the Council of Ministers instructed the competent authorities to investigate the origin, acquisition, transport, storage and traceability of vaccines intended for the proposed study. The Judicial Police subsequently searched the Bandim Health Project premises on 19 October and sealed the fridge containing a batch of hepatitis B vaccines. The vaccines were intended for the proposed study. The study had not begun, and none of the vaccines had been administered. We cooperated with the authorities. Stabell Benn said the trial will not proceed and that Bandim has no plans to move it to a different location. “The underlying scientific question remains important,” she said. “But any future proposal would have to arise from an appropriate local context and partnership and undergo all required scientific, ethical, and governmental reviews.”

This emulator reimagines Nintendo’s Virtual Boy with a full color display

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-22

Last year, we reported on Red Viper, a Virtual Boy emulator that added a splash of Super Game Boy-style color to Nintendo’s famously failed red-on-black “portable” 3D headset from the ’90s. Now, a new emulation experiment, VBoy Color, has taken the concept to a higher level, using a tile-based palette system that allows for many more distinct colors in each individual Virtual Boy frame. Rather than simply replacing the Virtual Boy’s four shades of red with four different colors, VBoy Color’s readme files explain how the emulator’s game-specific Color Packs can assign a distinct four-color palette to each 8×8 tile in a Virtual Boy scene. That means the emulator can differentiate between green pipes, grey platforms, and red brick wall in Mario Clash, for instance, even though all those in-game objects share the same overlapping red hues in the original game. The Game Boy Color-esque results feel like being transported into an alternate history where Nintendo invested in a full-color display rather than cheaper red LEDs for the Virtual Boy hardware. The increased contrast between differently colored elements is particularly welcome in games like Jack Bros. or Vertical Force, where the original red-on-red display often makes it hard to differentiate the player-character from busy red background elements. VBoy Color comes with built-in color packs for 15 different games, which actually represents a significant chunk of the Virtual Boy’s limited official library. Tinkerers can edit those packs manually using built-in video tools to color individual screens, teaching the emulator which colors to assign whenever that same tile is used elsewhere in the game. Unfortunately, the re-coloring process doesn’t work perfectly in the current beta release. For one, the coloring algorithm seems to occasionally miss individual tiles, leading to some flickering, uncolored squares that stand out distractingly from their surroundings. For another, this coloring process doesn’t work on games like 3-D Tetris or Red Alarm that eschew tile-based graphics for a more 3D wire frame effect. For these, VBoy Color also offers an option that lets you apply new palettes or color gradients based on the apparent depth of each line, making it easier to discern the 3D effect (especially on a flat monitor). Even in its early state, VBoy Color features some high-quality emulation (built off of previous emulators like VirtualBoyGo and Beetle VB) and useful features like full stereoscopic support on VR headsets or 3D monitors. All in all, it’s nice to have yet another emulator that goes beyond accurate gameplay to actually improve on the experience delivered by original hardware.

Amazon has warned that vulnerabilities in a programming language that underpins much of the Internet, discovered using AI tools, are being “broadly exploited” as governments and companies rush to secure their systems. The world’s largest cloud computing provider on Wednesday said it had identified an attempt by hackers to exploit the bug four days before the weakness was disclosed to the public. Amazon added the flaws were “now being broadly exploited in the wild.” The US National Security Agency and UK authorities earlier on Wednesday warned that the vulnerabilities had left websites around the world critically exposed to hacking, as some governments in Europe were reportedly taking sites offline while they patched the two security flaws. The vast impact of the bugs, uncovered with the help of OpenAI and Google models after being undetected for more than a decade, highlights the ability of AI to upend cybersecurity. The release of new models earlier this year from Anthropic and OpenAI with powerful cyber capabilities prompted warnings from politicians and financial regulators and led the US government to temporarily limit their rollout. The NSA on Wednesday warned all organizations using the programming language known as PHP to immediately update their systems or take them offline. The developers responsible for maintaining PHP released software updates to patch the vulnerabilities earlier on Wednesday. “These vulnerabilities often provide a direct path to the server’s file system or database,” the NSA said. “If an immediate update is not feasible, it is recommended that organizations take the system offline until it can be fully mitigated.” Researchers at two US cybersecurity groups, Calif and Wiz, discovered the vulnerabilities. Alphabet-owned Wiz estimated that the compromised programming language powered about 70 percent of all websites in the world. “Given the severity of these flaws and PHP’s widespread use, we consider this one of the most impactful security issues in recent memory,” OpenAI said on Wednesday. “To our knowledge, frontier AI found these vulnerabilities before attackers did.” “It highlights how AI can uncover security flaws with Internet-scale impact, but the same capabilities can also help attackers rapidly develop offensive tools and exploits,” the AI lab added. OpenAI worked with PHP, Calif, and Wiz to verify the vulnerabilities and develop the patches. The White House said federal agencies were assessing the potential impacts of the bugs on government systems and critical infrastructure. PHP’s wide presence on the Internet is largely because WordPress, the most widely used website-building platform, is written primarily in PHP and relies on it to function. Forget "Malaise 2.0," GM says it's still all-in on EVs and autonomy

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-22

Visit Original Website
It’s easy to criticize US automakers and their tepid approach to electric vehicles under the current administration and the expiration of the EV tax credit. It’s also easy to look at the rest of the world’s embrace of EVs and wonder if the US is entering a “Malaise 2.0” era. Which is why GM wants the world to know that it is still focused on its end goals of electrification, autonomy, and improving vehicle quality while reducing development time. At this year’s GM Forward event, company executives announced that its hands-off, eyes-off Super Cruise technology will expand across the lineup and be available on nearly every vehicle. This will include the pickup trucks and the crossovers, and not just the Escalade IQ that it was announced for last year. Sterling Anderson, GM’s head of product, said the company is testing this technology to the tune of 1 million miles (1.6 million km) a month in both the virtual and real world. The goal is that not only would the vehicle be able to take you places, but it could go and do things for you. That sounds rather dystopian. But Anderson told Ars in an interview that once you realize what your vehicle is capable of doing (beyond the paradigm of being a car to go places), it opens up a world of convenience that might be hard to envision right now. He’s not talking about an Optimus-style robot that’ll do your laundry, but a tool that you use every day that expands to do more things. GM’s newly announced OnStar AI will play a role. “Imagine you worked late, and you get in your car to go home,” he told us. “Your car might remember that you ordered takeout from a restaurant recently that you frequent and like. The car could prompt you and place a pick-up order for you, which will be ready when you arrive to pick it up.” Trust is the key here, and Anderson reiterated that several times, suggesting that a car might be a more trusted piece of technology than a mobile phone. “People have a relationship with their cars that they don’t have with other things,” he said. “If your phone randomly suggested your favorite restaurant, it might give you pause, but if your car did so within the appropriate context you might be more willing to accept that.” OnStar AI will do more than just learn your habits; it will be able to help you better understand and better use your vehicle, we’re told. For example: a driver hears the car beep but doesn’t know what the alert was for. In this case, they could ask OnStar and it would be able to tell them—for example, a speed limit warning. Being a specialized AI trained specifically on the details of the car, it can be integrated in a way that other models, like Google’s Gemini, cannot. Thanks to new compute on board, announced at last year’s Forward, the car will be able to handle much of that processing locally without requiring a connection to the cloud at all. But a cloud connection could improve the drive or make it safer. For some time now, automakers and suppliers have promised systems that crowdsource data to infer and predict road conditions—if you’re driving through a snowy area, and the cars in front of you have had traction control events, you could receive a warning to be extra careful as you approach. GM also wants to keep customers within its own ecosystem. Imagine a two-car, two-GM OnStar AI household, and you’re late picking up your child from practice. Here, OnStar AI could look and see your spouse is actually closer, swap roles, and redirect you to the takeout place to pick up dinner. OK, it’s a very specific use case. Trust continues to be the key. Anderson knows that must be earned, and trust is difficult to get back if it’s broken. His approach is to put that customer trust first in what he does. For example, GM’s new infotainment system will have the ability to record from the cameras as a dash cam and store that video. And it can upload that video to GM’s cloud to access anywhere. But it is end-to-end encrypted, meaning that when it hits GM’s servers, it’s in a format that GM can’t read. If law enforcement wanted to view the video, GM wouldn’t be able to help because it could not decrypt the video. That is the type of trust that is absolutely needed when dealing with this type of personal data, because there is a convenience to a lot of this. But the opportunity for that data to be misused is also there—it wasn’t too long ago that GM was found to be selling customers’ data, often without their knowledge, to insurance companies. We’ll want to experience GM’s new attitude in practice, of course, but hearing an automotive executive directly address that concern head-on was refreshing. This all leads into the fully autonomous functionality that GM is working on. Ars also spoke with Rashed Haq, VP of Autonomous Vehicles at GM, about the current state of the level 4 technology, and more importantly, when it’ll be safe enough for customers to use. “We look at two key factors, safety and roadcraft,” Haq told me. Safety obviously includes the vehicle’s occupants, but also the general public. And that requires millions of miles of testing in the real world but also digitally. Automakers like GM will never be able to prepare for every scenario, but it will combine training data with an open vocabulary to assess what information is there and make a driving decision based on that. Haq also reiterated the company’s expectation that when the car is driven by the computer, the responsibility falls on GM and the computer if something goes wrong due to a malfunction. The second key here is roadcraft. That means making sure that the vehicle isn’t creating a hazard or problem for other vehicles on the road. You’ve likely seen videos where Waymo vehicles are all crowded on a street blocking traffic: That’s not something GM wants its cars to do. Eventually, all of this leads back to the idea of a car working for you for tasks that aren’t just driving places. Your car could go pick up your dry cleaning, or at least drive there and wait while someone comes out and deposits the clothes inside it. You could ride to school with a child, both sitting in the back seat, reclaiming some time together because the car is doing the mundane task of driving. It’s an interesting idea and will only happen if consumers trust GM and their vehicle enough to do that, something we won’t know until closer to that technology maturing. GM also announced its Gen-4 EV architecture based on LMR battery technology. As we learned last year, the new lithium manganese-rich battery chemistry promises energy densities close to nickel-manganese-cobalt but without that expensive cobalt, making their cost closer to that of lithium iron phosphate (LFP) chemistry cells. A 30 percent cheaper battery should help GM offer a wide variety of EVs, hopefully including some affordable models, like the now-limited-run Bolt. If GM can also deliver affordable autonomy, built on a foundation of trust and safety, it’ll be a unique offering from a US automaker and will put the company in a better position to take on the challenges from global competition.

The optics of a mother who not only failed to defend her daughter from abuse, and not only sided with the abuser, but spent the next four decades calling her daughter a liar and trying to ruin her life—well, it’s hard to come up with a more nauseating maternal figure. Few moments this election cycle have made the stakes as clear as this hideous ad: As Americans cast their ballots in November, it’s these girls whose futures we’re voting for. With the midterm elections now just days away, it’s becoming clearer how the Trump administration plans to handle a potential blue wave that could destroy the president’s control of Congress for the remainder of his term. A key plank in this emerging strategy involves the most populous Democratic state in the country: California. The Department of Justice unveiled not one but two federal lawsuits over the past week that target the state’s elections, seeking the removal of countless voters from the rolls despite long-standing federal law that bans states from removing any voter within 90 days of a federal election. The trumped-up lawsuits make a showy attempt to go after a minuscule number of voters out of California’s whopping 23 million registered voters, but more importantly, the lawsuits conveniently lay the groundwork for a post-election legal fight—if things don’t go the president’s way. California being the Trump administration’s target should come as no surprise. Outgoing Gov. Gavin Newsom has been a constant critic of President Donald Trump. California is also the only Democrat-controlled state that successfully countered Trump’s mid-decade redistricting push, with voters there overwhelmingly passing a proposition to adopt new voting maps that could net up to five new Democrat-leaning House seats. The Trump administration unsuccessfully tried to convince the Supreme Court to ban California from using its new maps because they were “tainted by an unconstitutional racial gerrymander.” Clearly still salty about that matter, among others, the administration seems to have been cooking up a new way to attack California. Over the summer, Homeland Security sent California’s Secretary of State Shirley Weber a letter alleging that nearly 200,000 registered voters in California might be noncitizens (numerous secretaries of state received similar letters). But the letters included allegations alone, no evidence or data verifying DHS’s claims, which Weber said she flagged and asked to see, to no avail. Fast-forward to Oct. 13, when Weber, along with Los Angeles County Registrar-Recorder and County Clerk Dean Logan received another letter, this time from the DOJ—three weeks before Election Day—asking them to review a list of over 20,000 individuals suspected of being noncitizens who were sent a vote-by-mail ballot for the November election. DOJ wanted both Weber and Logan to direct election officials across the state to “segregate” submitted ballots from voters whose citizenship could not “immediately be verified,” refrain from sending ballots to any more people on its list of 20,000 suspected noncitizens “until we confirm their citizenship status,” and “if a ballot for any individual on the list has been returned, instruct the appropriate elections official to not verify the signature and/or tabulate the ballot until citizenship can be determined.” Weber and Logan were given approximately 48 hours to respond to the DOJ’s letter. And like clockwork, on Oct. 15 the DOJ filed a lawsuit asking a judge to issue a restraining order to prevent California election officials from processing ballots for any individual on its list of purported noncitizen voters. Never mind the fact that asking California to remove even a single name from its voter rolls at this point is a direct violation of the National Voter Registration Act, which established a 90-day “quiet period” before an election. This provision is meant to prevent states from doing exactly what the Trump administration is asking for: mass removals of voters too close to a federal election, which can cause confusion and prevent voters from fixing any registration issues with enough time left to cast their ballots. Weber called this out in responding to the DOJ’s Oct. 13 letter. She cited the NVRA and the unlawful ask being put on her and election officials across California. “With less than three weeks before the election, you are demanding unprecedented measures that threaten thousands of Californians’ right to vote, based on unverifiable information or methodology that you still fail to explain or provide,” she said. Weber also went after DOJ’s list of 20,000 purported noncitizens registered to vote in California, reiterating that she requested the underlying data and methodology months ago in order to attempt to verify the federal government’s claims. As it stands, the DOJ’s list effectively failed to “be of any investigative use.” On Tuesday, a Los Angeles County election official responded in court to the DOJ’s lawsuit. She called the DOJ’s demands potentially “catastrophic” and said they could end up halting all ballot counting, in person or by mail. She also revealed that election officials looked at a sample of 200 people on the federal government’s list and found that about 43 percent of them had a voter registration canceled, 71 percent had never actually cast a ballot, and nearly 40 percent indicated they were in fact U.S. citizens. This would not be the first time the Trump administration’s alleged noncitizen voting lists were found to be inaccurate. Last month, the Trump administration admitted that a list of nearly 16,000 purported noncitizen voters it flagged for Nevada election officials was wholly inaccurate. After Nevada Deputy Attorney General Greg D. Ott attempted to verify DHS’s claims, he found that every single person on DHS’s list was actually a lawful U.S. citizen. And since Trump retook the presidency, his DOJ has sued 30 states and D.C. to get access to their unredacted voter rolls. Twenty-six of those lawsuits were dismissed by federal judges, half of whom were appointed by Republican presidents, including by Trump himself. None of this matters to the Trump administration because its goal isn’t actually to weed out voter fraud, but to accomplish two things. First, these efforts seed doubt in America’s electoral process that could convince some voters to distrust the system to the point where they won’t bother casting a ballot. Second, these sham lawsuits give the administration a foothold in the courtroom in the event that a state’s election results are not satisfactory to Trump. Now, the DOJ and losing Republican candidates have a legal record to pull on to claim fraud and challenge state election results. Rick Hasen, law professor at UCLA and director of the Safeguarding Democracy Project, described this approach in a recent post on his Election Law Blog. Though Hasen expects the DOJ’s lawsuit against California “to go nowhere” in a court of law, he believes “this is a political document, potentially laying the groundwork for Trump to claim that the election results are illegitimate because California has not done enough to deal with noncitizen voting.” To put a finer point on this, the DOJ this week announced a second lawsuit against 28 Californians for election crimes. They’re each being accused of making false statements in their naturalization applications—those applications ask whether applicants previously registered to vote or claimed U.S. citizenship. A number of these folks, though, do not appear to have actually voted, and some registered to vote more than 20 years ago, likely by accident. “Twenty-eight out of 23 million registered voters in California is just … a drop in the bucket numerically,” California Attorney General Rob Bonta said. “It’s 0.0001 percent of all the registered voters, and it makes the point that there is no widespread voter fraud.” Research backs this up, with one study finding that when noncitizen voting and registration does happen, it’s usually due to bureaucratic error and not intentional. For instance, just this year New Jersey revealed that its Motor Vehicle Commission improperly registered about 6,600 noncitizens to vote back in 2023, but fewer than 400 of them actually cast ballots between 2023 and 2024. The Supreme Court has a hand in all of this, after it effectively emboldened the administration to pursue these types of lawsuits through an opinion issued just a month ago in DHS v. League of Women Voters. In a 6–3 decision, the justices have allowed the federal government to use an expanded federal database, known as the Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements, to identify noncitizen voting in states’ voter rolls. What can be done now? Well, Americans are the only ones who carry the power of the vote, and right now they can control when they cast their ballots. As Joyce Vance, former U.S. attorney for the Northern District of Alabama, emphasized, “this is the time to vote early.” It will be far more difficult for the Trump administration to challenge election results with ballots submitted weeks ahead of Election Day, especially when races have a large margin of victory. And that’s only possible with strong voter turnout. With less than two weeks to go before the midterms, the tale is the same as it has been all fall: It’s hard to find anything going Republicans’ way. Look around. While the GOP’s super PACs continue flooding Senate races in red states like Texas, Ohio, Kansas, and Iowa with ads, Senate Democrats’ super PAC is investing in Mississippi. In the House, Republican election groups are retreating from lost causes to defend core seats. But as Democrats put away House districts and Senate races in states like Georgia and North Carolina, one race is swimming curiously against the tide. On the same day that the election handicappers at the Cook Political Report shifted their rating of the Mississippi and South Carolina Senate races in Democrats’ favor, and fellow forecasters at Sabato’s Crystal Ball moved dozens of House races Democrats’ way, Cook made another change. It moved the Minnesota Senate race, a contest between Democratic Lt. Gov. Peggy Flanagan and Republican former sports reporter Michele Tafoya, from “Likely Democrat” to “Lean Democrat.” Crystal Ball had made a similar decision the previous week. In other words, forecasters believe Flanagan is still the favorite, but not by much. What’s going on here? Why, in a great environment for Democrats, and in a consistently blue state like Minnesota, is this race looking like a squeaker? It’s not the same as New Hampshire, Democrats’ other misbehaving light-blue state that should be a shoo-in this year but somehow isn’t. In the Granite State, Republicans are running a former senator from the state’s Republican royal family, and Republican outside groups invested substantially, and early, in that race. Senate Republicans’ main super PAC, meanwhile, hasn’t put any money behind Tafoya’s bid; each candidate’s campaign has spent a civilized couple-million dollars each. And it’s not that Minnesota is a runaway train trending redder demographically. It’s dominated by the Twin Cities, and has a high percentage of adults with college degrees, the dividing line on which so much of Trump-era political realignment hinges. Instead, Democrats’ inability to pull away in this race is a matter of candidate quality, and seismic news stories from Minnesota that garnered immense national attention. It’s not that Flanagan, an energetic progressive who harnessed anti-D.C. energy to win her primary over the more moderate Rep. Angie Craig, is a dunderhead. But Flanagan—like other progressive-wing nominees such as Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan and Troy Jackson in Maine—is clawing tooth and nail to get over the finish line even in this ideal Democratic environment while more moderate candidates like Roy Cooper in North Carolina and Jon Ossoff in Georgia have effectively put their races away. If the energy brought by progressive candidates is outweighing the softness they might have with the median voter, that’s not being picked up in polling. Tafoya, meanwhile, was mocked by many upon her entry to the race, as former Sunday Night Football sideline reporters don’t have substantial governing backgrounds. What former Sunday Night Football sideline reporters do have, though, is vast name recognition and an ability to speak well on TV. A lack of a substantial governing background has its advantages, too, as it allows her to draw up positions from scratch. She’s described herself as a “pro-choice Republican” (a posture that abortion rights advocates argue is not entirely on the level), has not had to take any votes on the Iran war, and has pledged to “stand up” to Trump when he’s wrong. What sets the race apart, aside from positions here or there, though, is that Tafoya, more than the average 2026 Republican in Trump’s America, has a lot of offensive material to work with. Flanagan is lieutenant to Gov. Tim Walz, who presided over a state welfare fraud scandal of such magnitude that it ended his bid for a third term. Tafoya has made this her central line of attack, arguing that Flanagan should be held accountable for the failures of the administration. “My opponent has been second in command in this state for eight years, drawing a taxpayer-funded paycheck,” Tafoya, in one of her many hits on Flanagan over the issue, said in a recent debate. “And what do we have to show for that? We have massive fraud, historic fraud. We have test scores down. We have crime spiking. We have a massive surplus turned into a deficit.” Flanagan’s defense, meanwhile, is that the social services agencies responsible for being defrauded were outside her portfolio, and that the blame rests with Walz. “The governor’s been very clear that the buck stops with him,” Flanagan said during the debate, positioning the governor under the bus. “He was the one who had oversight here.” Were that scandal to have been the last substantial national news story to explode out of Minnesota over the last year, Flanagan could be in even worse trouble. But it was the uncovering of those fraud scandals, along with its concentration of perpetrators within Minnesota’s Somali community, that egged the Trump administration into launching Operation Metro Surge, an assault on the Twin Cities from immigration enforcement authorities. That operation led to the deaths of two protesters under Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents’ heavy-handed tactics and turned the tide in both Minnesota and the nation against the administration’s mass deportation agenda. It’s the legacy of Operation Metro Surge that will make it so challenging for Tafoya to turn the race into a true toss-up before Election Day. Trump’s heavy-handedness is not just an abstraction for Minnesota Democrats. They’ve battled it in their streets. If Tafoya’s task of hammering the welfare scandal is simple, Flanagan’s task—bringing up Trump, and the need to stop him—is simpler. Earlier in the year, after she announced her candidacy, Tafoya said that she would be “honored” to have Trump’s endorsement, that she would “happily take” it. Trump has since become historically unpopular, and for a long time, she kept her distance. Just last week, though, Trump finally endorsed her, telling a crowd in Youngstown, Ohio (?), that “you have to vote for Michele Tafoya, who is a very respected person.” Trump said this with the briefest of pauses between “Michele” and “Tafoya,” as if he remembered in the moment that it might not be helpful to endorse her. Tafoya’s reaction to the endorsement was telling. “I heard about that,” she said when asked about it. “Look, this campaign is about bringing all Minnesotans together, whether they voted for President Trump or whether they voted for Kamala Harris.” She was similarly evasive when asked during the debate about whether she would appear with Trump should he campaign in Minnesota—something he has threatened to do—twice giving responses that weren’t even in the ballpark of answering the question. The RealClearPolitics polling average has Flanagan up 2 points over Tafoya at the moment, while an internal poll released by Senate Democrats’ super PAC, the Senate Majority PAC, at the beginning of the month showed her up by 8 points. Whatever it is, the same forecasters who are moving the race in Republicans’ direction “still believe the national environment will lift Flanagan to victory,” as the Cook Political Report’s Jessica Taylor put it. It’s possible that the polls are way off, that Minnesota will fall in line with a result closer to what you’d expect, and that Flanagan wins in a walk. It’s just strange that we don’t know, particularly when, in much swingier states, Democrats have already seemingly clinched victory. If the race is that tight, though, it’s a reminder that no “wave” election is ever uniform. Candidates matter, and state-specific issues matter. Weird things happen. In 2014, in an environment where Republicans won their largest House majority since the 1920s while picking up governor’s races in Maryland, Illinois, and Massachusetts, Democrats knocked off the incumbent GOP governor of Pennsylvania by 10 points. In 2018, when Democrats picked up 40 seats in the House, they lost a Florida Senate seat (and governor’s race) before the state had fully calcified into its present redness. There’s a not-unrealistic scenario in which Democrats win the Texas and Minnesota Senate seats by the same margin—or, if the polls are underestimating Republicans, lose them by the same margin. Maybe, like me, you’ve been aware that this layer of frozen soil is thawing, that it’s important in the global puzzle of climate change … and that’s about the extent of it. The great news for both of us is that my colleague James Temple just published two in-depth stories about permafrost. His reporting took him to the boreal forests of Alaska, where he helicoptered into the wilderness to learn more about how this material could affect our climate both now and in the future. I’d recommend you give both of James’s stories a read. There’s this feature about efforts to tackle the intensifying fires that are threatening permafrost, as well as this look at how researchers are trying to understand the complicated tangle of potential climate effects. Here are three things I took away. Permafrost is the frozen layer of soil that’s under about 15% of land around the Northern Hemisphere. While technically material needs to be frozen for only two consecutive years to count as permafrost, most of our current permafrost formed during the last ice age. That means there’s organic material that’s been frozen, and essentially locked away, for thousands of years. But as global temperatures tick up and this layer thaws, microbes are able to break down that organic matter, releasing carbon dioxide and methane—powerful greenhouse gases that drive climate change. Those emissions form a feedback loop, accelerating warming further. The steady rise in global temperatures has already shrunk the footprint of the Arctic permafrost by about 12% since 1850, and the pace has been picking up in the last 50 years. The wild thing is, only a minority of the models that have been used in UN climate reports actually take into account the gradual loss of permafrost. And none of them include the abrupt thaw and collapse that can happen after events like heat waves and fires (more on those fires in a moment). There are efforts to make sure future UN reports reflect the growing science, but for now, all this means that we’re probably underestimating how much and how fast the planet is going to warm. Fire is a natural part of boreal forests’ life cycle, but they’re getting more intense because of climate change. And crucially, fires can accelerate the thawing of permafrost. By the end of the century, this effect could release the equivalent of 80 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide. Today, the policy across remote parts of Canada and Alaska is generally to let wildfires burn as long as they’re not threatening human habitats. But some researchers say we should explore fighting these fires to save the carbon trapped in the forests and the permafrost beneath them. Researchers at the Woodwell Climate Research Center are developing a map of burn risk and carbon vulnerability across Alaska, essentially highlighting areas where fires are most likely to spread and which areas are home to certain types of permafrost that would release the most emissions. (Yes, apparently there are different types of permafrost. Yedoma is a particularly deep, carbon-dense form from the late Pleistocene era.) That’s all just the beginning—once we figure out potential targets, the question is what to do about it. Some suggest setting more prescribed fires, something Indigenous groups have done for thousands of years, partly to reduce the danger of massive blazes. Or resources could go to early detection and efforts to put out fires before they grow. There’s about 1.5 trillion tons of carbon locked away in permafrost, about twice the amount in the atmosphere. We know that permafrost thawing can release greenhouse-gas emissions and drive climate change. But there are complications. One particularly big wrinkle: Other effects from permafrost thawing and fire recovery might counteract the carbon lost from those events. For example, as temperatures warm, plants can gradually move north and take hold where they couldn’t have before. Those plants soak up carbon from the atmosphere as they grow, at least partly locking away what was emitted. Researchers are working to better understand this ecosystem. And the more we learn, the more complicated the picture looks. How much carbon is lost from permafrost, and how much a given area will recover its carbon, depend on a huge array of factors. How severe are the fires that have accelerated local thawing? What’s the local climate and environment? And what kind of permafrost is at stake? The higher we push the planet’s temperature, the more we mess with complicated natural phenomena that we’re only beginning to understand. AI’s hidden storage bill: What inference leaves behind

Ask most executives to picture the cost of AI and they’ll likely see GPUs—racks of accelerators with gigawatts of power—and a waitlist for silicon. But that picture leaves something out: Data storage costs. What happens to data after a model responds? Much of the back-end data that’s generated—from billions of daily prompts, images, and videos—needs to be stored. But data storage is one of the harder infrastructure costs to plan for, and it’s often overlooked in initial AI infrastructure planning. Traditionally, AI infrastructure conversations have centered on the compute-intensive process of training a model. Training is finite: a run starts, burns through compute, and ends. But inference runs continuously, every time a model answers a prompt. For infrastructure planning, that difference matters: A training run is a project you can budget for and retire. Inference doesn’t retire; every inference request leaves something behind. Take AI-generated video. In this workload, the back-end spend can be significant. From internal measurements, WD found that an 8-second clip (returned to a user as a 3.97MB 720p file) left more than 500MB of associated data behind on the back end, including logs, cached context, immediate renders, backups, and safety records. That’s a 130x increase over the output file size the user saw. Scale this to an enterprise generating longer, higher resolution video and the back-end footprint grows into the hundreds of gigabytes per clip. And that back-end footprint is growing fast. McKinsey projects AI inference demand will grow at a 35% CAGR through 2030, versus 22% for training, making up more than 40% of total data center demand by the end of the decade. A recent WD-sponsored IDC global white paper that surveyed 763 IT and business decision makers found that nearly 95% of respondents reported an increase in data volume due to generative AI over the past 12 months, with roughly 61% reporting growth of 25% or more. “You can think of having more storage as an optimization for keeping the GPUs busy,” said a senior product manager of a hyperscale cloud service provider quoted in report. “The biggest challenge is to ensure that the GPUs are not idle, waiting for data to show up—hours of GPU idle time becomes very expensive, very fast.” Put in dollar terms: if your storage spend is growing 25% year over year while your compute budget grows in the more predictable cyclical way, storage ends up outpacing the budget line that receives the most attention. Compute demand rises and falls with training cycles, product launches, and hardware refreshes. But the volume of retained data doesn't fall back down with it when compute demand slows. Model logs, synthetic outputs, inference results, and more keep accumulating, and organizations are holding onto more of it for longer. This changes the role of retained data. Information that once moved into an archive and rarely surfaced can now become an input to future AI workloads. As more data is retained, retrieved, and reused, storage must support not only growing capacity, but increasingly dynamic access to it. The same WD-IDC survey found 96% of respondents anticipate needing faster archive retrieval to support inference or retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). AI workloads require both performance and capacity, with different technologies serving different needs across the data lifecycle. And for the vast volumes of data that must be retained and revisited, HDDs provide a cost-effective, high-capacity foundation, with a significant cost-per-terabyte advantage For a CFO or CIO, this means “AI infrastructure spend” may be scoped too narrowly on compute. A model that idles while data loads costs about as much as one idling for lack of a GPU. But the storage bottleneck stays hidden until it’s already a problem, surfacing in operating costs and retrieval delays rather than a CapEx line next to the accelerators. Consider a retailer generating AI product videos for its catalog, or an insurer running claims documents through a RAG pipeline for faster underwriting. In both cases, the compute cost is a known, budgeted quantity: a per-generation or per-query rate that finance can forecast. The storage cost is different: It compounds with every video generated and every claim processed, because none of that back-end data disappears once the task is finished. A team that budgets AI as “compute plus a rounding error for storage” may find, in 12 to 18 months, that the rounding error has become a significant fraction of the program’s total cost—right around the time leadership is deciding whether to expand. Addressing this challenge starts with architecture. Storage strategy tends to be designed for expensive, hot-tier performance (for data accessed instantly and constantly), while most AI data by volume accumulates in the warm, cool, and cold tiers behind it. What enterprises need is economically scalable capacity: Infrastructure designed for the full AI data life cycle, balancing performance with capacity, power, reliability, and total cost of ownership as data persists, compounds, and is increasingly reused. That means treating archives that once sat untouched as active assets for RAG and inference, and setting retention policies for data’s growing strategic value rather than its original storage cost. It also means recognizing that decisions about storage play out across fleets, not individual drives. Such infrastructure requires storage tiers, where individualized power consumption, reliability, density, and operational efficiency all determine whether the system remains economically sustainable as capacity scales. There are three questions to ask about your organization’s AI infrastructure budget: Most organizations cannot answer all three with confidence.
