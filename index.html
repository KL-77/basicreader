With new open models, Meta pitches another reboot of its struggling AI strategy

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-08-10

Meta has announced its intention to focus on open-weight large language models. Additionally, the company announced the release of an open model called Muse Glimmer and a promise to open the weights for Muse Spark 1.2, its more powerful model, in the next few weeks. Alongside these announcements, Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg published a more than 6,000-word essay outlining the company’s philosophy about AI systems and governance moving forward. The essay aims to differentiate Meta from companies like OpenAI and Anthropic, which develop proprietary models and have lobbied the US government for help competing against large-scale distillation—which involves using an existing model to train a new one—or open-weight models by Chinese labs. Muse Glimmer is a 30 billion parameter model with a 128,000-token context window by default. It is distilled from Muse Spark, the larger and more capable model that Meta launched earlier this year. Glimmer is meant to run on users’ local machines, rather than via a cloud service or an API. Glimmer’s weights are open source under the Apache 2.0 license. Muse Spark was introduced in April as a closed, proprietary frontier-class model—Meta’s first major model release after a significant shake-up of the company’s AI teams last year and a departure from its focus on models that are, by some definition, open. When Meta released Muse Spark 1.1 in July, it introduced its first paid service—again, a departure from its previous strategy. Muse Spark 1.2 was released on August 5 and was accompanied by Muse Code, a terminal coding agent. Developers have generally found that Muse Code doesn’t quite match the frontier models from Anthropic or OpenAI in capability, but it competes well on cost—meaning it has similar positioning to many open-weight models from Chinese labs. As a smaller model designed to run on consumer GPUs, Muse Glimmer won’t compete on that level at all—but it reflects a growing movement to bring some inference to local devices to reduce reliance and spending on the models produced by the big labs, like Anthropic and OpenAI. Alongside the model releases, Mark Zuckerberg is credited as the author of a lengthy open letter that details Meta’s corporate strategy with AI and a broader argument for how AI should be developed, distributed, and regulated. It follows several statements by other Big Tech and AI company leaders debating the merits of open-weight models, proprietary labs, and the practice of distillation—something that some Chinese labs have reportedly done to build models that compete with the latest efforts from Anthropic and others. On July 24, several companies, including Nvidia, Hugging Face, Meta, Mistral, Mozilla, OpenAI, and others, co-signed an open letter titled “Open Weights and American AI Leadership.” The letter argued for the value of open-weight models as opposed—or at least in addition—to proprietary ones and defended distillation as a legitimate practice, even as it carved out a distinction for “unlawful efforts to extract value from closed models.” It advocated for “targeted legal and commercial frameworks rather than sweeping restrictions on techniques that play an important role in AI innovation.” On the topic of distillation, Meta and Zuckerberg wrote: The ability for models to learn from other models is an important principle of how the open source ecosystem works. All AI models are derived from human knowledge. Some have tried to frame distillation as harmful, but I think it is important to protect the principle that you can learn from anything you can observe. However, the majority of the essay focused on the notion of decentralized AI systems that are distributed widely. It also directly aims at arguments that tightly controlled proprietary AI models and systems are needed because of concerns about existential threats or catastrophic misalignment: It is surprising that the discourse from many developing AI is so filled with doom. I do not understand why anyone who believes that AI will eliminate most jobs and much of humanity’s relevance would rush to build that future. The notion that AI is so dangerous that the only safe path is an extreme concentration of power seems inherently problematic. Zuckerberg wrote that the view of alignment taken by companies like Anthropic—which claims that it is trying to train singular and broad foundation models with operational parameters and guardrails that will ensure they benefit humanity broadly—is “fundamentally flawed.” “People’s diverse values represent different tradeoffs they would make on important issues. There is no technological solution that can align with everyone’s opposing interests and values at once,” the essay says. “Any singular superintelligence would have to prioritize some values over others and in the process would be incapable of being benevolent to everyone.” Instead, Meta argues here that models should be personalized to the needs and values of individuals or groups of individuals. It also claims that decentralization will make everyone safer, because it will give the benefits and advantages of “superintelligence” to everyone equally, instead of privileging “a small number of individuals, businesses, governments, or AI itself.” Meta has been lagging behind other Big Tech companies and major frontier labs for foundation models. Its models haven’t seen the kind of adoption that those developed by OpenAI or Anthropic have. OpenAI and Anthropic have aggressively targeted enterprise customers, releasing powerful models and harnesses for knowledge-work tasks like software development, and they have made significant inroads and generated substantial revenue from this strategy. Meta has not seen the same level of success. Meta also saw a total overhaul of its AI division last year, when former Meta AI chief scientist Yann LeCun was replaced by former Scale AI CEO Alexandr Wang. The reset led to a change in focus. In recent months, the debate around open-weight models and distillation has increased in volume as recent Chinese models like Alibaba’s Qwen3.8-Max and Moonshot’s Kimi K3 have been shown to rival Anthropic and OpenAI at the frontier. Those models may perform slightly worse in coding benchmarks, for example, but they are generally cheaper to use. In a sense, Meta seems to be positioning itself as a US alternative to Alibaba, Moonshot, or DeepSeek—not quite as frontier-facing as Anthropic or OpenAI, but more open, customizable, and affordable. It is also orienting itself—at least with these public statements—more toward personal use as opposed to large-scale enterprise deployments, at least for now. That is a retreat from some of its earlier ambitions, in a way, as the company takes advantage of changing winds to try to plot a new course.

President Trump signed an executive order Monday that makes sweeping, unilateral changes to federal vaccine recommendations. The order aims to reduce the number of lifesaving vaccines all children are recommended to get—from 18 to 11—while also splitting them up and spacing them out over more time and more medical visits. The vaccine changes are not backed by evidence or spurred by new findings. Rather, they are based on false anti-vaccine fearmongering about harms, including the debunked claim that they cause autism, and misinformation that multiple vaccinations can “overwhelm” children’s immune systems. This false claim has also been repeatedly debunked and explained. During a signing event at the White House on Monday afternoon, Trump falsely claimed that pediatricians “have a vaccination that looks like the size of a bottle of soda,” that is “poured into a little child’s body, and bad things happen in too many cases. This is an explosion; this is an epidemic,” Trump said. He also tied the new vaccine recommendations to his goal of finding out “what’s going on with autism.” Dozens of high-quality studies encompassing data on millions of children have found no evidence linking the neurodevelopmental condition to immunizations. Nevertheless, Trump suggested that the changes outlined in the order would reduce autism rates in the US. “There’s a reason for such epidemic rates of autism,” Trump said. “It is essential to our research efforts that we have the very best vaccine recommendations in the entire world. So we’re reducing them.” Under the new recommendations, Trump said children should get fewer vaccines, suggesting that the US recommends an excessive number of vaccines compared with other high-income countries. In reality, by dropping down to only 11 recommended vaccinations, the US becomes an outlier in recommending so few, according to fact-checking by Stat News in January. The only other country that recommends so few vaccines is Denmark, a small, relatively homogenous country with universal healthcare. Trump’s order also calls for splitting up the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine into three separate shots. In the signing event, Trump falsely claimed that the current combination shot is “quite lethal,” and that they are “not at all lethal” when separated. Such individual immunizations are not currently available in the US and would require years of development and expensive trials to establish safety and efficacy. Those investments are particularly hefty given that the existing MMR vaccine has been safely used for decades, providing 97 percent efficacy with two doses. The MMR vaccine has never been linked to a death in a person with a competent immune system. (The vaccines are not recommended in people with compromised immune systems.) The order is in line with reports that Trump personally promotes the debunked claim that vaccines cause autism and that he wants part of his legacy tied to curing autism. Trump had reportedly put pressure on anti-vaccine Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. to do more to link vaccines and autism. In the signing event, Trump praised Kennedy, saying “he’s doing a fantastic job,” and telling the anti-vaccine advocate “I’m proud of you.” The order comes as vaccination rates in the US are already slipping. Only 10 states have vaccination coverage above the 95 percent threshold needed to thwart outbreaks. The US is on the verge of losing its measles elimination status—a status hard-won in 2000 after decades of persistent vaccination campaigns. This year, the US has already recorded 2,465 measles cases, the highest case count since 1991.

Amazon backs power plant that may become top source of US climate pollution

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-08-10

Visit Original Website
Amazon’s artificial intelligence ambitions will soon be partly fueled by a natural gas-burning power plant in Texas that “could become the largest single source of climate pollution in the United States,” The New York Times reported. These gas-burning plants are increasingly lumped into data center projects. They produce harmful pollutants, which can accelerate climate change impacts and harm public health—contributing to conditions like asthma, heart disease, lung cancer, and strokes. Some communities are demanding more stringent environmental reviews, but the Trump administration favors fast-tracking that can include skipping the permitting process entirely. Since Elon Musk’s xAI controversially began relying on gas turbines last spring to power its biggest AI data center, Colossus, there has been public backlash to off-the-grid data centers like the one Amazon is building. Notably, the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) in Mississippi sued xAI to get Musk’s gas turbines turned off in June. That spurred the Trump administration to join the fight, backing xAI and arguing that citizens have no power to enforce the Clean Air Act. However, as xAI and Trump pushed to dismiss the lawsuit, reports show that other AI giants have largely ignored mounting public criticism since it was filed. They’ve increasingly invested in various ways to avoid permitting discussions and supply their own power—including Anthropic, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, and Oracle. Together with Amazon’s investments, these alternative power plant projects swiftly securing approvals have become a harbinger that, unlike other data center fights that have successfully blocked $130 billion in projects so far this year, resistance against gas-burning plants may be futile. A potential loss in the xAI fight could devastate momentum in communities hoping to keep up the fight for more transparency. Dozens of firms have begun sourcing their own power supplies in the past year, but the Texas project is reportedly Amazon’s first big investment in an off-the-grid data center. After Amazon confirmed that it’s investing in the power plant to supply the energy needed for a massive planned data center project in Pecos County, the company boasted this ensured its project avoided straining the grid or raising electricity costs for neighbors. But the company did not respond to Ars’ questions about the power plant backlash, making it seem clear that the discussion it’s willing to have is over the cost of utilities, not the potentially mass-polluting impacts from its center’s reliance on gas turbines. In a statement provided to Ars, Amazon said that it “believes in paying the full costs of powering our operations,” and it plans to do so by relying on “new on-site generation that won’t raise electricity costs for Texas families and is designed to transition to grid-connected service as interconnection timelines allow.” Over time, Amazon expects its power plant will possibly add energy supply for local customers, which could eventually make the grid more reliable and affordable. That’s the vision for the future that Amazon and likely other AI firms want the public to focus on. However, it’s unclear if neighbors would ever see those future benefits. And any spike in emissions once the Texas power plant comes online seems to clash with Amazon’s voluntary commitments to reach net-zero carbon emissions by 2040, critics suggested. Amazon told Ars that it remains committed to its Climate Pledge. But under Amazon’s current plan, the Texas power plant would be permitted to release 33 million tons of carbon dioxide a year, which the NYT flagged as “more planet-warming gases than any other power plant in the country.” Amazon knows that its data center plans could obstruct its climate goals at a time when its emissions have already been steadily rising, the NYT reported. But Amazon’s bottom line might take precedence over that 2040 deadline. For companies like Amazon, gas-burning plants offer a fast lane to avoid years of delays associated with connecting to the grid and launching data centers quickly to meet ever-rising demand, just as AI becomes more mainstream. Any delay at all is viewed as money left on the table, and Amazon CEO Andy Jassy said on a July 30 earnings call that demand is particularly exploding for its AI and chips businesses amid the AI boom. Looking forward, Amazon expects supply may lag behind demand well into 2027. Margaret Callahan, an Amazon spokesperson, suggested that the 2040 deadline remains a priority but also noted that “the world looks different now than when we co-founded the climate pledge,” the NYT reported. Amazon’s Pecos County plan was first exposed by Cleanview, a company closely tracking environmental concerns with off-the-grid data center developments. At the start of August, Cleanview discovered that the tech giant had filed three construction permits in Texas near the power plant that were “beginning immediately” and wondered if the projects were linked. Thoroughly documenting the paper trail and taking satellite images, Cleanview got Amazon to confirm its suspicions, then released a report Friday warning that “Amazon is backing what could be the largest natural gas power plant ever built in the United States.” Specifically, one permit showed the power plant “would use 35 turbines to generate up to 7.65 gigawatts of power,” Cleanview reported. Although it’s rare for any facility to emit as much as its permits allow, Cleanview is concerned that Amazon’s rush to fulfill Amazon Web Services cloud orders will tempt the company to blast more and more emissions into more areas each year without adequately weighing public harms. On top of the Texas power plant, Amazon is also “in talks with the developers of a 4.5 GW gas power plant in Homer City, Pennsylvania,” Cleanview reported. That Pennsylvania power plant, Cleanview noted, was supposed to be the largest in the US, but clearly that bragging right wouldn’t have stuck for long. Spiraling demand for AI is now rapidly escalating the size of the power plants, Amazon’s plans suggest. And although one survey of data center developers earlier this year suggested that the majority think the “ideal energy mix” should predominantly rely on renewables and battery storage that are only complemented by natural gas, nuclear power, and other low-carbon sources, Donald Trump has predictably panned renewable energy while insisting that “rapidly building more fossil-fuel-burning power plants was essential to America’s AI race with China,” the NYT reported. At least one electric company, El Paso Electric, suggests Trump is right at least in the short-term, telling the NYT that “renewable generation alone cannot provide the continuous power required at this time” by the biggest data centers. Stoking additional backlash, the president also recently rolled back environmental protections to make it even easier for AI firms to build their own power plants at a time when his Environmental Protection Agency head, Lee Zeldin, has declined to set nationwide standards, Politico reported. Amazon told Ars that it’s exploring the use of solar energy and battery storage at the Texas data center but plans to rely on gas turbines in the meantime. In a recent report, Cleanview flagged 59 data centers “with a combined capacity of ~90 GW that plan to build their own power ‘behind-the-meter.’” That represents about 25 percent of all planned data center projects in the US and suggests that xAI’s Colossus strategy “has gone from niche to mainstream,” Cleanview said. “Speed to power is all that developers care about,” Cleanview reported, since “bringing a data center online even a few years early could result in tens of billions in revenue that would otherwise be forgone.” The communities most likely to be impacted by the rush to build off-the-grid data centers tend to be located near “major natural gas production regions,” in typically red states where regulatory frameworks “favor rapid development,” Cleanview reported. Currently, Texas is attracting the most projects, trailed by a large margin by Utah, Pennsylvania, Ohio, New Mexico, Wyoming, and Florida. On top of using “mobile gas generators strapped to semitrucks”—as xAI did in Memphis—firms are also scouring for other quick-fix solutions. Popular alternatives include “aeroderivative turbines originally designed for aircraft and warships,” “reciprocating engines that ramp fast, but are less efficient,” and “refurbished turbines acquired from industrial operations,” Cleanview found. Perhaps most problematic are some gas turbines with simpler designs, the NYT reported, which pollute more than standard turbines. For communities monitoring off-the-grid data centers, it can be hard to determine how big the pollution threat really is. The NAACP has argued that xAI’s reliance on gas turbines has only grown the longer the facility has operated, “growing from 18 to 27 to 33 to, now, 60 unpermitted gas turbines,” while “Memphis area communities were left to piece together the pollution threat for themselves.” So far, despite being the supposedly fast and easy solution, gas turbines aren’t widely used yet. According to Cleanview, xAI accounts for most of the 2 GW behind-the-meter generation capacity running as of mid-2026. Some of these projects can be approved in days, without any public notice, the NYT reported. One nonpartisan research group, the Environmental Integrity Project (EIP), recently estimated that “at least 82 gas-burning power plants are being built or have been proposed” to power US data centers—most within the last 18 months. But not every project gets a rubber stamp. Permitting delays have set back other attempts to move forward with off-the-grid data centers from OpenAI and Oracle, as well as Microsoft, Cleanview reported. And if nothing changes to end those delays by 2027, out of 13 GW in possible projects that could be online, only 5 GW of power may be added, the company forecasted. AI firms are doing their best to ensure there’s as much power as possible, avoiding delays by breaking up larger power projects into smaller phases that require less rigorous rules, the NYT reported. To stop that, the EIP is planning a lawsuit that could force US data center developers to adhere to stricter pollution rules. As Amazon’s data center plans expand, Jen Duggan, the EIP’s executive director, told the NYT that Texas remains a hotspot to watch for fast-tracked projects from the biggest tech companies. That’s troubling Texans, she said, who seem unlikely to simmer down since gas-burning plants “carry significant environmental footprints,” and “weak air pollution control permits that don’t protect public health are a systemic problem” already in that state.

Zuckerberg’s superyacht ignored emergency channel, failed to aid stranded boat

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-08-10

Visit Original Website
Social media scuttlebutt about Mark Zuckerberg’s $300 million superyacht failing to assist stranded boaters off the coast of Alaska was partly corroborated by ship-tracking data—and eventually prompted responses from both the US Coast Guard and a spokesperson for Zuckerberg. The incident was made public when Michael Love, a passenger aboard the replica steamship Wilderness Legacy during an Alaska cruise, posted about it on the social media platform Bluesky. The ship detoured to rescue a stranded 21-foot skiff with two women, a child and a dog, after the US Coast Guard had supposedly first radioed Zuckerberg’s superyacht, Launchpad, for help without success. “There was near unanimous booing when the captain announced this,” Love wrote in describing the Alaska cruise passengers’ response to the lack of help from Zuckerberg’s ship. The US Coast Guard described being hailed for assistance by the stranded boat located near Point Highland, Alaska, at 9:32 pm on August 3, in a statement shared by Halifax Shipping News. After the Coast Guard determined the vessel was not in distress, it issued a marine assistance request broadcast asking for voluntary help from nearby mariners. This is an important distinction because it means nearby vessels were not legally obligated to respond. If the Coast Guard had determined the stranded boat was in distress, then both international maritime law and US law would have legally required ship captains to render assistance. Although Launchpad and its $30 million support ship, Wingman, were not legally obligated to assist in this case, it may have represented a missed opportunity to do a little bit of good, according to Love, the passenger aboard the Wilderness Legacy. “Frankly, if I was Mark Zuckerberg I would be absolutely ecstatic at a chance to do something helpful for someone in need and maybe get some good PR out of it,” Love told the Alaska Beacon in a Bluesky message. Zuckerberg and his family were not aboard the superyacht at the time of the incident, according to a spokesperson for the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative in a statement shared with Halifax Shipping News and the Alaska Beacon. Ship-tracking data using AIS transponder data actively broadcast by each vessel shows that Zuckerberg’s 387-foot superyacht was initially moving ahead of the 192-foot Wilderness Legacy at a slightly faster speed. But then Launchpad slowed to a stop and allowed Wilderness Legacy to pass it. Wilderness Legacy appears to proceed with assisting the stranded boat, as described by the YouTube channel The Yacht Report in a video showing the AIS tracking paths. The Coast Guard confirmed that the ship towed the boat to Farragut Bay so that it could find safe anchorage, and the Alaska Beacon reported that Wilderness Legacy was able to refuel the skiff for the rest of its journey. However, the Coast Guard statement made no mention of Zuckerberg’s superyacht, Launchpad. Launchpad’s support ship, Wingman, is described by the publication YachtWorld as being equipped with deck space for carrying a helicopter, a Triton submarine that acts as a personal submersible for superyachts, water scooters, and multiple smaller boats. Ship-tracking data shows that Wingman moved past both Launchpad and the Wilderness Legacy without stopping. It also shows that Launchpad finally resumed moving again after the Wilderness Legacy appeared to return from having assisted the small skiff. The Zuckerberg spokesperson offered an explanation for why Launchpad did not initially respond to the Coast Guard’s request for assistance. “As the Coast Guard noted, the boat was not in distress, and by the time the crew reviewed the Coast Guard contact on a different radio channel from the one they were operating on, the assist was already underway,” the spokesperson said in the statement shared with news outlets. “We’re grateful all parties are safe.” But Democrat Senator Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island, himself a member of an elite sailing club, in a Facebook post criticized the lack of response from Zuckerberg’s superyacht. “Vessels are supposed to monitor channel 16 at all times, for this exact purpose,” Whitehouse wrote. “Billionaires don’t follow that rule either?” The US government’s radio watchkeeping regulations require any vessel equipped with a VHF marine radiotelephone to maintain watch on channel 16—reserved for emergency messages—whenever the radiotelephone is not being used for communication. Marine radiotelephones typically have dual-watch or even tri-watch features that specifically enable monitoring of multiple channels, with the transmissions on the emergency channel automatically taking priority. There are usually multiple VHF radios on the bridge of vessels such as Launchpad with at least one monitoring the emergency channel, according to eSysman, the host of The Yacht Report who goes by a pseudonym. “There is no situation where they would not have any VHF radios on the bridge of a vessel underway that were not on channel 16, so that excuse is kind of a poor one,” eSysman said in a video posted on August 10. That means any nearby ship monitoring the channel should have heard the stranded boaters asking the Coast Guard for help, along with hearing the Coast Guard’s follow-on request for assistance. It is also unclear why the crew of the support ship Wingman did not initially hear or heed the Coast Guard broadcast. Zuckerberg’s superyacht has previously courted controversy for turning off its AIS transponder and “going dark” to avoid public ship-tracking services, according to YachtWorld. That is a tactic only used by “shadow fleet” vessels carrying sanctioned oil or gas, illegal fishing boats, and the high-profile owners of luxury yachts, such as Zuckerberg or Jeff Bezos. But for safety-of-navigation reasons, the Coast Guard typically requires vessels to keep AIS activated in US waters. Such AIS tracking data helped shed a little more light on this latest controversy surrounding Launchpad. Society is running out of babies, and no one can agree on why. Most people around the world, including Americans, live in a country where overall fertility is below what demographers call the “replacement rate,” meaning that without migration, the population will shrink from one generation to the next. In some places, the birth rate is so low that each generation is set to be half the size of the previous one. A number of theories have been put forward to explain why this is happening. Some experts point to smartphones and social media, others to the cost of child care, still others to existential dread or overparenting. Claudia Goldin, the Nobel Prize–winning labor economist, favors another explanation: It’s the men. In a widely discussed new working paper, she argues that husbands still fail to do their fair share of housework and child care, forcing their wives to choose between procreation and career success. As a result, women are making the economically rational decision to have fewer kids. “The more that men can credibly signal they will be dependable ‘dads’ and not disappointing ‘duds,’ the higher the birth rate will be in the face of greater female agency,” Goldin writes in a companion essay. Goldin’s theory is attractive because it promises to resolve an uncomfortable tension in the fertility discussion. Historically, falling birth rates have accompanied increases in women’s education and autonomy. This was widely seen as a good thing when it meant going from seven kids on average to two or three. Now that birth rates have fallen below replacement, however, calls to reverse the decline can sound like attacks on feminism itself. But if Goldin is right, there’s no tension. Under her framework, rising equality for women helped bring birth rates down, and only more equality will get them back up. Unfortunately, the theory does not appear to be true. The data simply do not support it. For decades, men have been increasing their share of housework and child care. But the number of babies just keeps falling. When a country gets richer, its fertility tends to decline. But in the 1990s, scholars noticed that some high-income countries, such as France and Norway, had much higher fertility rates than others, such as Japan and Italy. What could explain this variation? Some hypothesized that traditions change more slowly than economies. In the Japans and Italys, they theorized, women were being told to be modern outside the home while still being asked to perform most of the labor inside it, leaving them burned-out and uninterested in having the usual two or three children. In the Frances and Norways, meanwhile, people were having more children because they were confident that things would be fairer in their households. “Women are able to see what happens to other women,” Peter McDonald, an Australian demographer who, in 2000, wrote one of the most famous papers proposing this theory, told me. Goldin’s new paper builds off the work of McDonald and others. Her theory is that countries with low fertility are experiencing a “mismatch” between women’s desire to be modern and men’s desire for them not to be. The paper proposes a simplified economic model to describe reproductive decisions. In the model, men are either “dads” who do sufficient child care and housework or “duds” who do not. If an educated woman marries a “dud” and has children, he won’t help out much, forcing her to limit her career and end up earning the same wage as if she had not gone to college. If she instead marries a “dad,” she can have kids and still realize her full economic potential. At a societal level, because these women don’t know which type of man they’ve married until after they have had a child, they’ll base their decision on whether to have children in proportion to the ratio of dads to duds in their country. The fewer the duds, the more comfortable they will be giving birth to the next generation without fear of a wage penalty. Marc Novicoff: The birth-rate crisis isn’t as bad as you’ve heard—it’s worse All economic models are simplifications, but some make more reasonable assumptions than others. Goldin’s model assumes that only college-educated women weigh whether to have children, but in some countries, including the United States, women who haven’t graduated from college are shifting toward childlessness faster than women who have. Another key assumption is that dad-or-dud status is randomly and blindly assigned. In fact, prospective couples are probably sorting more finely than ever. People are more likely to marry other people of similar education levels, the share of marriages that end in divorce is down, and the time spent living together before having children is up. We have never had more time to examine our partners before having children, and the chance of randomly drawing someone with sharply different beliefs about household gender roles is small and shrinking. An even bigger problem with the theory is the data ostensibly backing it up. As a proxy for the share of duds in a given country, Goldin uses survey data on how men and women spend their time in a set of OECD countries. In places where women report doing a lot more unpaid work (such as child care and household chores) than men, Goldin shows, the total fertility rate is lower. In places where the work is divvied up more evenly, fertility is higher. Her paper includes a graph that shows a strong correlation between the two factors. The trouble is that the relationship that the graph describes holds up only when you stick to Goldin’s somewhat idiosyncratic selection of data points. The fertility rates she uses for her y-axis, for example, are all from 2019, regardless of when the time-use survey was completed in a given country, and despite the fact that birth rates in many countries have fallen sharply since then. This creates distortions in various directions: Finland, for example, completed the survey she uses in 2010, when fertility stood at about 1.9 children per woman, but Goldin plots it at its 2019 fertility rate of 1.35. That happens to be close to Finland’s current rate, but the same isn’t true of Estonia, where fertility fell from about 1.7 in 2019 to 1.2 in 2025. Goldin restricts her sample to OECD countries with time-use surveys completed from 2009 to 2019, but leaves out two of them—Mexico and Turkey—in which women did a large share of the unpaid work but fertility was nonetheless high in 2019. She then adds Portugal and Denmark, whose time-use surveys were conducted in 1999 and 2001, and leaves out six other countries—Australia, Austria, Lithuania, Slovenia, Latvia, and Ireland—that conducted their surveys sometime from 1999 to 2009 and whose fertility rates mostly don’t fit the pattern of the ones included. In some cases, newer time-use data exist. Factoring in the latest surveys from Poland and the U.S., for example, would show a roughly 25-percent-smaller gap in unpaid work. If you include the countries that Goldin omits, and use the most recent time-use and fertility data, what you are left with is virtually no relationship at all. The slope flattens, and the amount of variation in fertility rates that is explained by the time-use differences (known in statistics as “the R-squared”) approaches 2 percent. When I spoke with Goldin about the paper, she was open to critiques about it. “All the comments that you’ve raised, I would raise about my own work,” she told me. She granted that “you can change the model in a hundred different ways.” As for the data points, she said she was trying to stay consistent with a previous version of the paper. “Sometimes, when you’re working on something, you begin with using one set of data and you build off that. And then you don’t ask, Well, maybe I should use something else.” Later, over email, she said that the graph was “not a major part of my paper or my research” and that “cross-country analysis is to be avoided.” Derek Thompson: When a promotion leads to divorce Versions of Goldin’s argument made by other academics have also been hampered by methodological issues. In a Slate article published last year, Darby Saxbe, a psychologist at the University of Southern California, argued that “dads can fix the birth rate” by doing more chores and child care. Her piece featured its own chart comparing men’s share of unpaid work to fertility rates in OECD countries. When I tried to re-create the chart, the results looked totally different. Over email, we figured out the likely culprit: The data for Saxbe’s chart, she explained, were sourced by AI, which seems to have hallucinated some of the numbers. Goldin told me that the original idea for her paper came from an influential 2008 study titled “Will the Stork Return to Europe and Japan? Understanding Fertility Within Developed Nations.” The authors of that paper showed a strong relationship between what young mothers said their male partners contributed to care and housework in a given country and that country’s fertility rate. According to the authors’ math, a 10-percentage-point increase in men’s unpaid-labor share was associated with 0.4 more births per woman—an enormous difference. So the correlation between fertility and men’s contributions at home was robust at one point. But, if you update the data, it disappears. The authors of “Will the Stork Return” used surveys and fertility rates from 2002. Using the 2022 version of the survey (the most recent available) and 2024 fertility rates (also the most recent available for most countries) produces an almost perfectly flat relationship, one in which variation in men’s share of housework explains essentially none of the variation in a country’s fertility. Birth rates still differ among rich countries, but the male share of housework apparently has nothing to do with it. “Will the Stork Return” included optimistic predictions for the future. Observing that “men in all high-income countries appear to be taking on a larger share of household duties,” the researchers forecast that “high-income countries with the lowest fertility rates are likely to see an increase in fertility in the coming decades.” That has not come true. Men’s share of housework has continued to rise, but fertility has only declined further. This set of facts applies to basically every country for which data exist. In Finland, the gap in unpaid work roughly halved from 2010 to 2021; the fertility rate went from from 1.9 to 1.5, and now sits at 1.3. In South Korea, the gap in household work shrank 42 percent from 1999 to 2024; over that time, South Korean fertility rates went from 1.4 births per woman to 0.75. “I would guess that the theory is right and there’s other things going on that have completely overwhelmed the theory,” Bruce Sacerdote, one of the economists who wrote the 2008 paper, told me. But if men’s increased participation in unpaid labor is pushing fertility up, the effect is undetectable. If men helping out more at home could reverse the birth-rate decline, that would be great news. It would ease the burden on women and simultaneously help save the world from aging populations, stagnant economies, and crumbling pension systems. Alas, the experiment has been run, and the results are in: Men are chipping in more, and the number of babies is still falling. That doesn’t mean they couldn’t do more, but it does mean that doing more is unlikely to fix the situation. And telling women that they’re surrounded by “duds” seems unlikelier still.

Right before the deadliest viral pandemic in a century, which made the problems of 24/7 digital everything even worse. But instead of lasting a couple of years, this politics illness became a chronic condition. Maybe that’s too metaphorical. But it is a terrible habit, and breaking any bad habit is hard. Such as overeating, which was the bad habit Americans adopted right after weaning themselves off cigarettes and before taking up all the unhealthy bad habits enabled by social media and AI. Next time I talk with Ash, I should ask if he’s proud of me for having a thought that could pass for leftish. But you forgot to include Big Pharma and the gun industry, he’ll say, and probably mention the guns I own, and we’d be at it again. You see? It’s a tough habit to break. Asher says he agrees and that he’s working on it too. He calls it an addiction, a rare figurative use of the word I don’t mind. Although “co-enabling and co-dependent rage addicts,” as he once said of us, was a bit much. But also dark and glamorous, like we’re Kurt Cobain and Courtney Love. Part of my rehab practice is reminding myself of arguments we had over the years that had no political content, the ones that became Things We Can All Laugh About Now. The respite from political talk is no doubt a reason I’ve stayed here in Tennessee instead of moving to San Francisco with Ash. If politicized thinking soured too much of our life, if it became an addiction, then I’m out here for the same reason recovering addicts remove themselves from easy access to drugs and booze and their intimates who use. I hate to admit it, but I’ve found that when I talk with a moving picture of my husband on a screen, I’m much better able to control my impulses to carp and roll my eyes and snort and make political jokes at his expense and get into dumb, unnecessary fights. Even though escaping technomania is a reason I’m here too. My biggest regret has been missing out on all these months of our daughter, Logan, who decided to live with her father. Of course, I’ve told myself, my day-to-day helicoptering utility to a 17-year-old is delimited and diminishing. Which is a lame rationalization. I know one of my main emotions from now on will be missing my children; it occurred to me that returning here may be a self-punishing head start on that with Logan, some sick parental version of breaking up with people before they dump you. Anyhow, what I said earlier about the half-life of the war’s traumas is true. But what to me feels even better is the fading of what preceded it—the compulsive politicization of everything, the fear and hatred of the Other Side as an animating, irresistible everyday emotion. The active contempt has become less salient, diminished, including in rural Middle Tennessee. “I don’t disagree about the vibe shift, honey,” says my local best friend, Victoria, who grew up around here and lives 15 minutes away. “But isn’t it also true for you right now because you don’t have Asher around to bicker with so much?” At first I found the term Disunion too clinical and wonky. But it is more correctly mutual than secession. And has some history, dignity, a touch of grandeur. I’m a fan of all of those. But I call it the breakup. It’s a little glib, but simple, factual, neutral, idiomatic, and broad enough to cover the whole era, beginning to end. It’s also a universally familiar concept, unlike Disunion. It’s relatable. Americans have been strange bedfellows in a mixed marriage for a long time. We grew apart. We stayed together, but finally came to find each other impossible. We fought. We had irreconcilable differences. We split. In a world where half the marriages unravel or rot or die of thirst or blow up. Or do whatever mine is doing. A clichéd question about America since I was a kid concerned our national political “fever,” if and when it would ever break. The fever broke. Along with our beautiful, uncomfortable, brittle, breakable antique national governing arrangements, now being rebuilt and refurbished. To a firefighter who is inside one, a fire creates as much darkness as heat. Flames start bright but throw off smoke that can swathe everything in pitch black. Lieutenant Kevin Dowdell did not fight fires so much as he groped through and problem-solved them, relying on half-blind judgment and expertise—and his fellow firefighters to keep flaming debris off his back. At the cindering end of a successful 24-hour shift with the New York City Fire Department, Kevin would hang up his helmet, so ash-rubbed that it looked salty, and drive home to his wife and two sons in Rockaway Beach, Queens. “Oh, we had a good job last night,” he’d say, elated as well as exhausted. By “good,” his family knew, he meant the fire had been big. Check out more from this issue and find your next story to read. In his innermost firefighter’s heart, Kevin liked it when the alarms went off. A good job broke the tedium of drills and doughnut-dunking in the firehouse kitchen. It called on Kevin’s whole mind and body; he had to calculate burn rates and airflow while wielding 40 pounds of gear. Fire needs four elements: oxygen, heat, fuel, and a chemical reaction. Deprive a fire of even one of those, and it goes out. But in New York—a steel-stacked, deep-tunneled metropolis with architecture ranging from tenements to glass towers—the interaction of the elements is complex. One of the questions on the test to become a lieutenant: What is the expansion factor of a 100-foot steel beam as it reaches the inherent heat level of 1,200 degrees Fahrenheit? A firefighter had to be part chemist, part engineer, and part daredevil. But the mission itself was simple. “You show up on someone’s worst day and try to make it better,” Kevin told his sons, Patrick and James. The FDNY had a saying: There were people who just worked for the department for a paycheck, and then there were firefighters. Kevin was a firefighter. “They love to go to a fire,” his widow, RoseEllen, told me, sitting at Patrick’s kitchen table in Rockaway Beach. “They’re waiting for a good fire.” But on September 11, 2001, Kevin met a fire that looked like hell’s own flames. It burned a quarter of a mile up in the sky, unreachable by any ladder, almost at the top of the World Trade Center’s Twin Towers. Kevin’s unit, Rescue 4 in Queens, arrived about 20 minutes after the second hijacked plane had hit. He would have read the color of the smoke and picked apart the elements of the fire: aviation fuel, combustible office materials, and an oxygen-rich chimney effect. They did not make for a good fire. They made for an uncontainable one. Nevertheless, Kevin led his men into the South Tower. Twenty-five years after the attacks, “Never Forget” is a T-shirt slogan. A third of the American public is too young to recall that day. Anniversaries have become rote rituals in which politicians describe scenes they did not witness and national unity that no longer exists. To get beyond the benumbed abstraction, it’s necessary to remember with specificity. September 11 was a lived event. One way to recall it meaningfully, then, is through a life. Kevin Dowdell was handsome in a regular way, with dark-brown hair in an old-fashioned side-comb and a lean triangle of a face that tapered to a good chin. But he had some gravel in him, and his forearms were as thick as the fat end of a baseball bat. He was the grandson of an Irish longshoreman who’d left a dying fishing port in Fingal and arrived at Ellis Island in 1904 with $3 in his pocket. Kevin’s father, a butcher, raised seven children on leftover baloney ends in an 1,100-square-foot rowhouse in Brooklyn. At the age of 18, Kevin went to work as a “sandhog,” the nickname for miners who dug the city’s maze of tunnels that carry cars, steam, sewers, water. He spent his early manhood descending hundreds of feet into compression chambers, which sometimes caused the “staggers” or “chokes,” as sandhogs called the bends. He dynamited through rock and dug out muck up to his knees—slow, grimy work so hazardous that it was said to kill a man for every mile. But a four-hour shift paid as if you’d worked eight. When he wasn’t sandhogging he was at Rockaway Beach, where he surfed and hung out in Jacob Riis Park with a crowd from his old Brooklyn neighborhood. Among them was RoseEllen Fedele, a tall, slim 18-year-old college student. At first he was too shy to make eye contact, but he eventually got her number. They dated for five years, which was how long it took him to get out of sandhogging—and she never doubted that he would. Between shifts he studied for and took every civil-service exam in the city. In 1980, Kevin qualified to be a police officer, and he married RoseEllen, who had become a schoolteacher. He spent just a year wearing a badge before he transferred to the fire department, intrigued by a brother-in-law’s stories about how satisfying the job was. RoseEllen had Patrick on Easter Sunday 1983. When they brought the baby home and relatives crowded to hold him, Kevin refused to let go. “Nobody’s touching him,” he joked. The Dowdells moved to the Rockaways, known as the “Irish Riviera” for its enclaves of firefighters and cops, where the neighbors might swap shifts so you could spend time with your kids. Kevin found a small bungalow that wasn’t winterized and renovated it with his own two hands. They were so poor that they couldn’t afford a stove at first; instead, they cooked on a hot plate. When James came 17 months after Patrick, they put a second crib in the tiny nursery. “You had to, like, slide between them” turned sideways, RoseEllen said. Kevin often worked seven days a week. When he was off duty, he laid hardwood floors and tile to make extra money. If he got home from work and found the boys in bed, sometimes he’d wake them up just to spend a little time with them. “Come on. Let’s take a walk.” They’d stroll down Rockaway Beach to look for old glass bottles, then get an ice cream. He taught them to surf and how to handle the large waves. “Dive to the bottom and grab a fistful of sand. By that time, the wave will have passed over. Trust me.” They did. On Sunday mornings, RoseEllen would simmer red sauce on the stove while she took the boys to Mass, then jar it for the rest of the week’s dinners. One of their few indulgences was Friday dinner in Greenwich Village, usually at a favorite Italian spot at the corner of Carmine and Bleecker Streets called Trattoria Spaghetto. “Put on something nice,” Kevin would tell the boys. “Dress like your father has a job.” He would order the veal parmigiana and a glass of red wine, look around the table at his family, and say, “This is what I work all week for.” The boys spent so much time at the firehouse that they learned the meaning of the crackling radio talk. A “10‑75” was a structure fire in progress, and “K” meant the end of a transmission. The boys would yell, “Rescue 2 to Queens 10‑75 K!” They did drills with Kevin and his team. The big men would strap 10-year-old James into a harness and hoist him over the second-floor firehouse roof. When a call came in, the boys would ride on the front bench seat of the truck, between the men in their bulky turnout coats. They’d get to press the foot pedal that sounded the horn. Kevin showed them how to release it in short blasts. “You have to tap it.” He’d pat James on the knee—the signal for another horn blare. At the site of the fire, the boys would watch through the windshield as the men dismounted in their cumbrous gear and walked through smoking doorways. “The bigger the fire,” James told me, “the bigger the smiles when they came out.” The city had five rescue units, one per borough, and their members were drawn from among the most capable firefighters in the FDNY. Kevin trained for the worst emergencies: tunnel fires, structural collapses, aviation crashes, train derailments. He carried specialized tools and was a skilled black-water diver, for when cars went into rivers. As Rescue 4’s lieutenant, he organized logistics and made swift, expert judgments about risk factors. A manhole cover weighed 300 pounds but could blow six stories into the air. Hidden fuel could be anywhere. An apartment lobby might have an adjacent dry cleaner; grocery stores had stock closets with flammable ingredients. Smoke and heat moved one way in hallways and another in vertical shafts. In a single day, Kevin and his men might pluck a stranded window washer off a building, deal with a transformer fire down a manhole, and fish passengers from the Hudson after their helicopter went down. In 2000, the unit responded to 2,524 calls. But primarily, Rescue 4 went to fires. Sometimes there were half a dozen in a shift. The bigger the fire, the calmer Kevin acted. “He could pull up to the building and tell you right away where the people are gonna be trapped and which way the fire’s gonna spread,” Liam Flaherty, a colleague in Rescue 4, told me. “I think his heart rate actually went down the worse the fire got.” Often Kevin’s men could feel the fire but not see it. “When you’re in this dark environment that’s hot, it’s like being locked in a room with a tiger,” Billy Murphy, another colleague, told me. “You’re like, ‘Where is it?’ ” Flaherty would peer through his breathing mask at an acrid black veil, trying to find his way around a burning apartment. Then he would hear Kevin’s cool voice. “Hey, Li, how we making out over there?” Over the years, Kevin worked so many “good” fires that he became something of a legend in the FDNY, according to Flaherty. In 1991, Kevin helped keep flames at bay on the 12th floor of a Seventh Avenue building to aid a stunning rope rescue of two men from window ledges. In 1993, he was among the first responders to the terrorist bombing of the World Trade Center, which left a crater several stories deep and required him to comb through hazardous rubble, still glowing orange, while gas tanks exploded in the underground parking garages. In 1994, a gas leak blew up a diner in Long Island City, dropping the ground floor into the basement and pinning a waitress under slabs of concrete. Kevin and his partners crawled into the wreckage and worked for three hours to free her as piles of debris quaked over them. He took turns holding her hand and shielding her body with his own, in case the slabs caved. They got her out alive, and won medals for that job. In 1995, he was part of an elite FEMA squad deployed in the aftermath of the Oklahoma City bombing to search for survivors and recover bodies. With his record, he could have risen higher in the department. But a captaincy would have required leaving Rescue 4 for another unit in the city, and he wanted to stay. “Better to be a happy lieutenant,” he told RoseEllen. The unit had a strong sense of brotherhood; many of its members played together in the Emerald Society Pipes and Drums, an Irish group within the department that marched in civic events. Flaherty, a tousle-haired raconteur, was their drum major. Murphy, a tall Coast Guard veteran, played the bagpipes. Kevin loved the Pipes and Drums so much that he coaxed his sons to take Irish-music lessons—Patrick, the pipes; James, the snare drum—so they could march in the St. Patrick’s Day parade, alongside Kevin himself on the bass drum. “I work with the best guys in the world,” he would tell his sons. As with every FDNY unit, the heart of Rescue 4 was the firehouse kitchen, where the men ate together, and drank coffee, and debriefed after every incident. It was also the scene of geyser-like laughter, juvenile and often at someone’s expense. If Kevin corrected the pronunciation of his name (it was Dowdell ), they’d call him “Diddle.” A shift was up to 24 hours, and between alarms they had time for pranks. Once, an officer made the mistake of complaining that he didn’t like hot dogs. For the next month, all the unit served for dinner was hot dogs. Hot dogs in barbecue sauce. Hot dogs in pasta. Hot dogs, grilled and boiled. The officer grimly waited it out. At the end of the month, a large meat loaf landed in the center of the table. Then they cut him a slice. The meat loaf was stuffed with hot dogs. Rescue 4 gave Kevin a sense of home, and a feeling that he was personally protecting his beloved Rockaways. He didn’t just respond to trouble in the area—he sought it out. After a boy in their neighborhood fell into a septic tank, Kevin installed a scanner on his dashboard; he believed he could have helped if he’d heard the call. He’d listen to emergency dispatches while driving home from Little League and say, “We’re close by. Let’s just go check it out.” Kevin would pull over if he saw a car accident and extricate someone from the wreck before EMS arrived. He’d tell his sons not to be bystanders. “Don’t sit on the sidelines. Make things happen. Do things.” Once, when Kevin saw smoke coming from a building in their neighborhood, he told the boys to call the fire department and then ran off, grabbed a garden hose, and climbed through a back window to start putting the blaze out himself. He was strict and exacting with his sons. For every minute they were late to something, Kevin docked them half an hour of free time. When they were old enough, he enlisted them in construction work, and didn’t pay them until they could lift heavy loads. “You work like a man, you get paid like a man.” He once called James home from a friend’s house because he hadn’t cleaned his room. He marched James into the doorway and said, “Look at that.” There was a pair of socks on the floor. Patrick was growing into a burly achiever who aspired to West Point. James was slighter, milder, but with a distance runner’s stamina. During long car rides in New York gridlock, Kevin would ask the boys what they intended to do with their lives. Their grandfather was a butcher. Their father was a fireman. “I want you to be the next step up from that,” Kevin told them. “I want you to do something better than me.” By late summer 2001, Kevin had earned 20 citations for outstanding performance. He was beginning to think about retirement, and he dreamed of traveling—to Iceland in particular, with its volcanic landscape forged in fire. And the boys were nearly men. Patrick was 18 and had just started at Iona University, in New Rochelle, having been wait-listed at West Point. James was turning 17 and starting his senior year at Xavier High School in Manhattan. He wanted to be a firefighter. “You’re going to college,” Kevin insisted, but he took James on a scuba-training run under the Throgs Neck Bridge. James donned an FDNY dry suit and plunged into the dark, roiling East River while his father supervised from the boat. After the dive, someone took a picture of the two: Kevin’s hand is clapped on his son’s shoulder, and James, shrugging off the suit, is grinning widely. On Sunday, September 9, the family celebrated James’s birthday. Kevin had the day off and Patrick came down from Iona, and they barbecued and ate cake. Kevin and Patrick cloistered themselves in the den to work on his reapplication to West Point. Then Kevin drove Patrick back to school and kissed him on the cheek. “Love you. See you soon. I’ll talk to you next week.” Kevin reported to Rescue 4 at 6 p.m. on the evening of September 10 for a 24-hour shift. Liam Flaherty went off duty and headed to band practice at the Elks Lodge. Billy Murphy was on vacation. The kitchen was quiet. At 8:46 the next morning, American Airlines Flight 11 made a terrifying yeering sound as it accelerated into the North Tower of the World Trade Center. Rescue 4’s summons came at 8:55. Seven men hopped on the rig with Kevin: Terrence Farrell, William Mahoney, Peter Nelson, Durrell Pearsall, Peter Brennan, Allan Tarasiewicz, and a potential recruit named Michael Cawley. The ride probably took about 20 minutes. As they barreled toward Lower Manhattan, an unnerving alert came over the dispatch radio: “Be advised we have jumpers.” From a distance, Rescue 4 would have seen the smoke surging from floors 93 to 99 of the North Tower, the tallest building in New York. About seven minutes into their drive, they might have seen the blooming explosion of the second jet, United Airlines Flight 175, as it tilted into the South Tower between floors 77 and 85. Rescue 4 arrived at a scene that was too much for the senses. A torrent of debris fell from the buildings—shredded metal and insulation, chunks of concrete, shivering glass shards—amid an earsplitting clamor of sirens and screaming. Torsos, luggage, and aluminum scraps of 767s were strewn on the sidewalks. What looked like splashes of red paint on the plazas were the remains of jumpers who had pinwheeled from the upper floors of the North Tower. Kevin ran into a stunned fellow lieutenant named Robert LaRocco, from Ladder 9, who had seen a severed forearm with a perfectly manicured hand. LaRocco managed to get a call through to his wife. He told her, “It’s raining people.” Kevin reported to a command post across the street from the North Tower, where the FDNY’s chief of special operations command, Ray Downey, was doling out orders. He assigned Rescue 4 to help with evacuations in the South Tower and, if possible, to get upstairs and beat back the fire. Kevin was seen lining up his men and talking to them intently. He then led them single file toward the South Tower, hugging the sides of buildings. A rescue squad would have had its hands full in the lobby alone. Elevators were broken, stairwells smoked, and radios were unreliable. People were streaming down, badly burned or bloody, and some had to be carried. One elevator had stopped just short of the ground floor with people trapped inside. Firefighters pried open the outer doors and could see their legs, but couldn’t find a way to lower the car enough to free them. Flaming jet fuel cascaded down the shaft as the crews worked. At 9:52 a.m., about half an hour after Kevin had arrived, a battalion chief named Orio Palmer reached the South Tower’s 78th floor, where the jet’s wing had inflicted mass casualties. “We’re going to need two engines up here,” Palmer radioed down. Wherever Kevin was—trying to force open elevators, or starting the slow ascent to 78—the elements of the fire were taking over. The second plane had struck the South Tower at more than 500 miles an hour, stripping the fireproofing from steel trusses and columns. The inferno fed on carpets, curtains, chairs, desks, computers. Plumes of soot trapped the heat. The temperature hit 1,800 degrees Fahrenheit, softening the bones of the building. At 9:59 a.m., the top of the South Tower sagged. Then it began to collapse. Beams and struts slammed into one another. As each floor failed, the falling mass increased, picking up velocity. About 500,000 tons of concrete and steel sped to the earth, reaching an estimated 124 miles an hour. Lieutenant LaRocco of Ladder 9 had just put his foot on a stalled escalator on the north end of the South Tower’s mezzanine when he felt something like a wave in the floor. He heard a mounting roar, like a space shuttle taking off. The light in the windows vanished, as if someone had yanked a giant shade down. Kevin would have had little time to think about the sound above him. It took just 10 seconds for the tower to fall. As the mass neared the ground, it generated a huge blast of compressed air and seismic waves equal to those produced by a 2.1-magnitude earthquake. Paperwork from the towers drifted miles away, littering the beaches of the Rockaways. For most of that day, RoseEllen had been at P.S. 312, preoccupied with getting her third graders back to their parents. Now, at home, she waited for Kevin to call. He always did, leaving a message when she didn’t answer. “Hey, it’s me. If you see anything on the news, don’t worry about it. I’m okay.” But 6 p.m. came and went without any word. At 12:30 a.m., Special Operations Command called and told RoseEllen that Kevin was listed among the missing; all of the guys on Rescue 4’s rig were unaccounted for, as was their captain, Brian Hickey, who had been working with Rescue 3 that day. RoseEllen stayed up all night, her eyes darting between the television news and the front door. Around sunrise, RoseEllen went onto the porch and stared down the street. “At that point, I knew,” she would say, years later. “In my head, I knew.” After a week, one of the crews digging through the pulverized wreckage at Ground Zero came across a Halligan bar, which might have been used to claw at an elevator door. It was marked with KD and R4. Kevin Dowdell, Rescue 4. The tool had been blown out of the lobby. But there was no other sign of Kevin. The remaining men of Rescue 4 set up a war room in the firehouse, manned round-the-clock shifts to dig through the wreckage, and assigned a firefighter to liaise with each family who was missing someone. “Did we find him today?” RoseEllen would ask. No, Billy Murphy would say, day after day. Whenever the door to the Dowdell house opened, three faces would swivel toward it with hope. “We were like, ‘If anyone got out of there or could get out of there, it’s him,’ ” Patrick recalled, but it was always a relative or neighbor. Two weeks after the attacks, RoseEllen decided that sitting around the house in their grief was not doing them any good. “Listen,” she said to the boys. “You’re going back to school, and I’m going back to work.” On the drive to Iona, she persuaded Patrick to finish his reapplication to West Point. “You will not—we will not—use anything as an excuse in your life,” she said. Xavier High School was steeped in mourning; 60 alumni and close relatives of students and staff were lost. Counselors and priests watched James for signs of depression, but when RoseEllen pushed him to see a therapist, he protested. “All I do is talk to people,” he told her. He would channel his emotions into running, sometimes 12 miles at a time. Back at Iona, Patrick couldn’t concentrate. He called James and suggested that they go to Ground Zero to search for their father’s remains. “If we find him, I want to be there,” Patrick told James. “I want to see him. I want to help carry him out.” James heard his father’s voice in his head. Don’t sit on the sidelines. Make things happen. Do things. “I’ll meet you there,” James told his brother. Rescue 4 held a meeting about whether to allow them to join the search. They took a vote, and the boys won. Murphy and Flaherty geared them up with fire boots, gloves, Carhartt suits, helmets, and tools. The guys worried about giving the boys nightmares. Murphy warned them: “If we come across somebody, I’ll ask you to step away until we find out who it is.” The pile, as it was called, was eight stories of contorted metal, sticking up like giant fork tines, spread over 16 smoldering acres. The burning electrical smell was sickening. As Patrick and James clambered through it, they could feel heat in their boot soles. Grapplers lifted steel from the smoking heaps, and the diggers showed the boys what to look for underneath: a boot, a piece of helmet, the reflective tape from a turnout coat. But the main sign of human remains was the odor of decay. As soon as the diggers smelled it, they would wave at the machines to stop. The first time the boys were present for a recovery, the crew cast sidelong looks at James and Patrick. The men and boys removed their helmets. A chaplain was called. They stood at attention as the remains were carried away on a flag-draped stretcher. For seven months, James and Patrick worked at Ground Zero as much as they could. “Being away from it became harder than being there,” Patrick recalled. When his classes at Iona were done for the day, he’d find a ride to the city and dig half the night, then return to campus at 3 or 4 a.m. slit-eyed from ash and lack of sleep, stowing his dusty gear in a garbage bag in his dorm. James kept his Carhartts and tools in his Xavier locker. He would shed his school uniform—blue oxford shirt, necktie, insignia-patched sweater—and tromp out the schoolhouse door, transformed, in coveralls and boots. The crew found parts of helmets, and parts of people. They found impaled bodies that had to be cut away from the steel with torches. They found intact bodies in strange voids; how long had some people survived under the wreckage? Sometimes the boys just put bone fragments in buckets. At the firehouse, Patrick and James sat around the kitchen table debriefing like members of the company. They listened to the men of Rescue 4 wrestle with survivor’s guilt. If they had been on duty, they’d have been in the South Tower at 9:59 a.m. Survival was a matter of lefts and rights, as Flaherty said. They posed terrible “What if?” questions. Should the fire commanders have foreseen the towers’ collapse? If so, should they have kept men from going up? “Well, what was the alternative?” Flaherty argued at the table. “There’s people trapped upstairs. We have to go get them.” Could you imagine, he said, if the FDNY had just given up on the towers? “That would have been the worst day in the history of the fire department the other way.” The kitchen conversations were a kind of counseling for the boys. “We had our people, and we just talked amongst ourselves, and that was enough,” Patrick remembered. So were the funerals for firefighters they played at with the Emerald Society Pipes and Drums. They stood by graves in kilts and red tunics that Flaherty had given them, and performed “Amazing Grace” and “Going Home.” Many children of the dead were preschoolers or grade-schoolers. RoseEllen saw that the firefighters were helping to steady her sons. “Is school good?” Flaherty and others would ask the boys while they dug through the pile. “If your grades aren’t good, you can’t be here.” As a picture of Kevin’s movements with Rescue 4 emerged from survivor accounts, the recovery concentrated on a spot near the South Tower elevator shafts. In late October, on a day the boys were not working, a crew found the remains of four men from Rescue 4. But not Kevin’s. A few feet away was a large, burned-out space where fires had raged below the tonnage for weeks. “You recover two guys that were kind of close by to each other in the rubble, and then you don’t get anybody else,” Patrick said. “It’s really hard. You don’t know how that happens, but that’s how it happens.” On the morning of November 12, 2001, when an American Airlines plane accidentally crashed into the Rockaways shortly after takeoff, RoseEllen could see the smoke from the porch. “You are not going over there,” she told James. When Rescue 4 pulled up to the crash site, James was already there in his gear, hosing down a burning house. “You’re one of us now,” a firefighter told him. James, though just 17, took the FDNY entrance exam and passed. By spring, the pile had become a pit, with almost 1.8 million tons of debris removed. The site was practically broom-clean. The recovery process was down to DNA testing of the smallest fragments. Even then, no Kevin. “There’s nothing there,” Murphy told RoseEllen. “It’s over.” They held Kevin’s memorial service on April 20, 2002. His casket was filled with dust from the spot where they believed he had died, and mementos of him: his extra turnout gear, a pair of fire boots, a copy of the Irish Voice newspaper, travel brochures for Iceland. Letters from his sons and siblings. Hundreds of people attended, including the mayor. Patrick played a bagpipe solo of “Amazing Grace” as the casket was carried into the church. “He was a teacher, mentor, tradesman, listener, and my best friend,” Patrick said during his eulogy, concluding with an Irish blessing that Kevin had recited to him: “May the best days of your past be the worst of your future.” The memorial service marked an abrupt end to months of mission. “It was so consuming from September to April that after it was all said and done, there was a sense of ‘Now what do we do?’ ” James recalled. Kevin was irrecoverable. But they had his voice. Don’t sit on the sidelines. Patrick had just been accepted to West Point, and in June he entered “Beast Barracks,” the academy’s rigorous six-week cadet-induction program. It made the news: 9/11 firefighter’s kid joins the War on Terror. Overnight, he went from friends consoling him to officers barking orders. He marched to breakfast, marched to lunch, and spent nights shining his shoes. What if he couldn’t cut it? He feared letting his father down. The Jamaican writer Marlon James won international fame with A Brief History of Seven Killings, which was awarded the Booker Prize in 2015. That title misled—the book describes dozens of killings, not just seven, and it is by no means brief—but its premise delivered. The 700-page novel, his third, revisits the Jamaican election of 1976 and the assassination attempt on Bob Marley that year; the gang truce of 1978; and the violence that preceded and followed, first in Kingston, then in New York City during the 1980s and early 1990s. Lots of Americans, myself included, learned some pivotal Jamaican history and some Jamaican English and patois from its many troubled narrators. The sole woman in the array—the only consistently sympathetic narrator—wants instead to disappear: “Shut everybody out. Shut everybody down. Just go.” She even pores over How to Disappear Completely and Never Be Found (it’s a real book). “Whether on a plane or in a box,” she muses, “I was going to leave this place.” James, who grew up in Kingston and now splits his time between New York City and Minneapolis, has said the same about himself: “Whether it was in a plane or in a coffin, I knew I had to get out of Jamaica.” The Disappearers, James’s new novel, shows why. Brief History studies men who want to rise, to dominate, to become stars; this one focuses on men who try not to be seen. It’s James’s most clearly autobiographical work. Of its five main narrators, two share his age, background, and interests, and one of those misses out on the worst of Jamaica’s violence and ends up, as James has, in a stable job with an advanced degree. It’s also James’s first realist novel since Brief History—he published two long works of complex, bloody, mythological fantasy in between. This time, he writes about Jamaican men who have sex with men (only some would call themselves gay). Eight of them assemble in a Kingston home in 1988 for rehearsals of the Canadian playwright John Herbert’s 1967 prison drama, Fortune and Men’s Eyes, under the direction of Havelock, a confident, queeny émigré who’s returned from England. Speaking in Jamaican English, as most of the characters do, he explains that it’s “a gay play about men loving and sexing and rule over other men.” None of the men who gather at the house on Lady Musgrave Road is a professional actor. All responded to flyers or heard about the play through word of mouth, and the five central narrators take turns admitting us into their minds. Eddy, a bookish university student and one of the James-like pair, combines his literary interests with a craving for porn; his passion for indie rock and X-Men comics alienates him further from his peers. Jordan, who lives in his car and hustles to survive, becomes almost a doppelgänger for Eddy: He likes comics and alternative rock too, and reads the same sort of literary books as Eddy. The group also includes a mysterious half-Chinese businessman whom Eddy can’t help finding hot; wealthy Omar, who funds the rehearsals; and three other actors. All discover, during and after rehearsals, some imaginative refuge—a space for people who, as Eddy puts it, “can’t leave but can’t stay.” They chat and flirt and grumble about what parts they’re assigned. One actor objects to playing a drag queen. Men feel they have to protect their manhood, even in this private space. Then the violence descends. A posse of men finds the house and beats almost everyone bloody or worse, using blackjacks, nunchucks, knives, fists, a crowbar, a cricket bat. James depicts the aggressors’ thoughts in a short Faulknerian fugue. At least one of the attackers is closeted: “Kill it in them, kill it in me.” Two are cops. Another’s a reggae star whose big hit “Batty Is Not Pussy” advocates killing gay men. (Batty is Jamaican slang for “rear end,” and battyman is an insulting term for gay men.) James’s made-up star recalls the real musician Buju Banton, whose breakout track, “Boom Bye Bye”—which he’s since repudiated—also extols gay-bashing and murder. The mayhem leaves one actor dead, another blinded, and the rest hoping to rebuild, or more likely flee, their prior lives. Parts of interlocking stories unfold in the narrators’ divergent voices. Havelock writes delightfully ornate letters to his former lover in England. Jordan and Eddy relate their own travails through recorded interviews with a journalist named Petra, but also describe, in the first person, their days and years. Jordan ends up with the most eventful character arc, ultimately tracking down perpetrators too: He wants them to pay, or at least not to disappear. Brief History looks at power in a place of violence and examines what secrets it hides. Now James has written a book about people whose whole lives feel like secrets. And he has a thesis: Those who grow up learning that they are bad and do not deserve to exist—people who might get raped, maimed, or murdered for trying to be themselves—almost inevitably organize their life around attempts to disappear, escape, or else retreat to the margins, becoming (like Eddy) figures who observe others, themselves unseen, or (like Jordan) agents of vengeance who stick to the shadows. “There is a Jamaica underneath this Jamaica,” Jordan tells Eddy, whose more sheltered and luckier life means he might not know. It’s a metaphor, except when it’s not; the drag queens, trans women, and unhoused youth who live in Kingston’s gullies, below street level, have made it literal, as Eddy and Jordan, in flight from another mob later in the story, show us. James makes the gully dwellers and their fragile world a compelling side story, intent on presenting a panorama of Kingston’s gay life, from those who occupy the economic heights (also literal hills) down to “all of them queens and freaks and fishboys and battymen” who “cannot and will not pass.” The survivors of Lady Musgrave Road flee to totally closeted respectability, “a safe place filled with disappointment,” where several remain for years; or else to opportunities off the island, or religious rebirth, or a near-invisible secluded life “at the top of a mountain.” We might see the whole novel as an attempt to create a stage, a space, where gay Jamaican men can honestly and fully appear, at least in retrospect. Like Brief History, The Disappearers offers a big, modernist assemblage of contrasting styles, personas, and points of view. It, too, is a work of classic realism, touring the social layers in a changing Kingston as its characters earn and lose money, rise and fall. And so it’s full of gay sex: ecstatic, conflicted, reluctant, a desperate secret or an open secret. James acknowledges occasions of sweet, mutual, that’s-how-it-should-be sex—but those happen largely offstage. Most of the on-page couplings instead mix, alarmingly, sex with violence. Some people seek humiliation; some want to inflict it. Some get hurt by their tricks. James manages not to kink-shame anyone, but he’s less interested in kink education than in displaying a sexual world where every hookup comes with scary risks: assault and battery, for example, or public exposure, or HIV in the age before retrovirals. Living in fear of “the AIDS” entails its own “long set of disappearing acts”—not just sickness and death, but shame and denial and an inability to mourn. He wants to bring his gay men’s sex lives into the light. The men, however, keep trying to “erase themselves,” learning how to “make yourself into the kind of person nobody wants to see.” Early on, Eddy claims, “I don’t think about it a lot,” and yet he’s never unaware of being “part of an us that people just want to beat to death.” Or bury alive. The novel opens up many pleasures for literary readers, in its pacing, in its observations, and especially in the ventriloquism of Havelock’s old-school prose (he even writes one letter in the obsolescent gay slang Polari). But it’s not, mostly, a book about pleasure: These lives are often—realistically—scary or sad. We should not label them tragedies, though, because that implies a controlling divinity, or at least a fixed fate. As Havelock insists, “We need to stop calling atrocities tragic.” The Disappearers is, among other things, one novel responding to many atrocities. When you grow up surrounded by people who hate you for what you are and whom you love, it’s tough to disentangle their hate from self-hate, and either one from a wish to die. And it’s not just the cast from Lady Musgrave Road that conflates self-destruction with self-fashioning, death with destiny. So does the music of Kurt Cobain, the most important among the cultural touchstones that give Jordan and Eddy alternatives to Kingston. They listen to Nirvana, and to the rock bands that Cobain recommended—“music that people who hate me don’t like,” Jordan says—more than to “Peter Metro and Admiral Bailey” and other hitmakers from the island. The weakest subplot turns Jordan into an obsessive, predictable avenger, less like one of the X-Men than like a Jamaican Joker, with a twist that’s no twist at all if you’ve seen horror films. Still, any reader will feel for Jordan when he exclaims, late in the novel, “My ghost suffering makes me want to scream.” He means both that, compared with people dying of AIDS, he isn’t genuinely suffering (though he is), and that he feels like a disembodied outcast, a kind of ghost. The Disappearers ends up as a violence-driven protest, a scream of rage—like Nirvana’s final album—against a world that keeps trying to kill people like him and then pretends that they asked for it, or that they never existed. The novel is a superbly fashioned instrument for showing what felt wrong, what went wrong, in Jamaica in the late 1980s and ’90s, and what’s still wrong in other places, too, where people who grow up marginalized get blamed for their own persecution, or else celebrated as brave exceptions when, against the odds, they succeed. The fantastic unfairness—especially if it’s as brutal and direct as what James depicts—might make you a vanisher or an avenger (like Jordan). Or it might instead make you a chronicler (like Eddy and Petra), a maker of memorials, even a rescuer for people who want to be rescued—people who get sick of trying to disappear. And some do: Most of the survivors from Lady Musgrave Road, however wounded, find a way to forge on. Some (like Havelock) even keep trying at “building something—I don’t know, a safe place, a room of one’s own and world so you can let your hair down so to speak?” In James’s wildly patriarchal Jamaica, the struggle to live as a gay man means a struggle to become and remain some kind of man, whatever you like to do in bed. Life is, or seems to be, all about men: Women appear in the book but never narrate (as one does in Brief History), and barely say how they feel. That’s part of the point of The Disappearers, in which the same men who persecute gay desire want women to disappear. As in any binary opposition, men learn to define themselves against their supposed other: “Nothing more shameful for a man than to be treated like a woman,” one character thinks, an idea all too familiar in the contemporary United States as well. Among the seven people who survive the Lady Musgrave Road assault, James traces some future for all but one: outside or inside Jamaica, in sham marriages that fail or desk jobs that allow them to subsist, and even in fragile, comforting, long-delayed reunions. As for that one actor who just drops out of the plot, maybe he died. After all, his “own family prefer him gone.” Or maybe that person was never a man in the first place and built a solid life elsewhere as an adult woman, her boyhood deleted for good—what trans people in earlier generations called “woodworking” (fading into the woodwork). Within The Disappearers, that’s one character’s speculation, dismissed as soon as it’s raised. But it’s also another hint of hope. And this novel, which ends in 2005, does offer hope: not just for gay Jamaican men but for trans teens, nonbinary adults, and anyone else since then who’s felt as though their society has kept asking them to hide, or lie, or die, or just go away. The Disappearers is a harrowing book, to be sure, but never nihilist, or defeatist, or cynical. Its joy in ultimate survival comes through. The Trump Immunity Deal Is Neither a Deal Nor Does It Provide Immunity

News and Politics - Slate Magazine | 2026-08-10

Visit Original Website

A single sheet of paper sits somewhere in the files of the Justice Department, announcing, in capital letters, that the United States is “FOREVER BARRED and PRECLUDED” from pursuing any claims against President Donald Trump, his two eldest sons, and the Trump Organization. One page. One signature. Last week, Trump appealed the 56-page ruling in which Judge Kathleen Williams of the Southern District of Florida dismantled the settlement that produced it and sanctioned the lawyers who engineered it. Whether that page has any legal force is now a question for the court of appeals; the Senate, for its part, gave its answer by confirming the man who signed it as attorney general. The plain answer is that it has none. In short, Trump’s tax immunity deal is unenforceable. Let me tell you why. The lawsuit was never real. Trump sued the IRS and the Treasury Department in January, demanding $10 billion for the first-term leak of his tax returns by an IRS contractor already prosecuted, convicted, and imprisoned. The Justice Department, whose lawyers ultimately answer to Trump, never mounted a real defense. By May the parties—who in every practical sense were the same side—announced a settlement under which, by the Justice Department’s own description, the plaintiffs received a formal apology and no money, while the government established a $1.776 billion “Anti-Weaponization Fund” for claimed victims of alleged Justice Department weaponization. One day later, Todd Blanche, then acting attorney general and Trump’s former personal criminal defense lawyer, signed an addendum barring any and all claims concerning any matters now or hereafter pending before the IRS, the Treasury Department, or “other agencies or departments.” By one estimate, the waiver could be worth more than $100 million. The plaintiff sued for money and settled for an apology plus something his complaint never requested and no court could have awarded: immunity, granted by his own subordinates. The defendant received nothing in return. And the document carries a single signature, that of the defendant’s lawyer, the plaintiff’s former lawyer. Litigation is supposed to be adversarial. This was a one-man show performed in two costumes. Judge Williams said as much. The parties “worked in tandem and were never actually adverse,” she wrote, calling the lawsuit a pretext for a settlement “that had no viable basis in law or fact.” She sanctioned Trump’s lawyers and barred the government from invoking the agreement in any official proceeding. Article 3 courts decide cases and controversies, and a proceeding in which both sides want the same outcome is neither. The Supreme Court has refused to lend its judgments to collusive litigation since Lord v. Veazie in 1850. A settlement between parties who were never adverse is not the resolution of a controversy; it is a press release with a case number. Asked by senators whether the immunity remained in effect anyway, Blanche said yes; the ruling, he said, was under “review.” Court orders are not suggestions under review. Blanche leans on Williams’ concession that she cannot void the private contract. He should be careful with that argument, because as a contract the immunity was unenforceable before she ever ruled. No one can bargain away federal tax liability in a tort settlement on any letterhead. Under Section 7121 of the Internal Revenue Code, the government may conclusively settle tax liability only through a closing agreement executed by the secretary of the Treasury or a delegate, under prescribed procedures, final only as to the periods and matters specified. The Supreme Court held in Botany Worsted Mills v. United States in 1929 that where Congress prescribes the exclusive method for compromising tax liability, informal settlements by officials, however senior, do not bind the United States. If any sufficiently senior official could sign away tax liability with a memo, the revenue laws would bind only taxpayers without friends in the government. The Blanche addendum fails every element. It is not a closing agreement. It was not executed under Section 7121 procedures. It specifies no tax periods or matters, purporting instead to resolve “any and all claims,” forever, before agencies it does not even name. And it emerged from a damages suit about leaked records that placed no tax liability in controversy. The document waives nothing. An IRS commissioner who opened an audit of the Trump Organization tomorrow would breach no judgment, statute, or agreement. The only thing standing between the Trump family and an examination is the current management of the IRS. The immunity is not a legal status; it is a personnel decision. Even indulging the fiction that the addendum is a contract, contracts require parties, consideration, and terms that hold still. This one fails on all three. Blanche signed alone. The government received nothing. And the terms are being rewritten in real time by the sole signatory. He assured Sen. John Cornyn that “other agencies or departments” somehow binds only the IRS and Treasury, a reading Cornyn told him the text will not bear. Then, days before the committee vote, he posted two orders on X, one rescinding the fund and one “clarifying” the immunity’s scope. No plaintiff signed them, and the settlement itself provides that it may be modified only by written agreement of the parties. The arrangement now violates itself. A document one side signs alone and revises by social media post is not an agreement; it is a memorandum of intentions. And intentions do not survive elections. Nothing here binds a future administration, and courts almost never stop the government from disavowing promises its officials had no authority to make. “Forever,” in a document one side wrote for itself, means January 2029. Which returns us to that single sheet of paper. The administration did not defy the tax laws. It counterfeited them. The document has the typography of law and none of its machinery: no adverseness, no authority, no statutory vehicle, no second signature. A counterfeit works only until someone examines it, and Judge Williams already has. The Senate spent its summer examining it, then confirmed the man who signed it. Confirmation can elevate the official; it cannot ratify the paper. The court of appeals examines it next; it will reach the same conclusion as Judge Williams. The immunity exists on a single page, signed by a single official, construed to mean whatever the current week requires. That is not immunity. That is stationery. For most Firefox and Thunderbird users, the key swap shouldn't require any action. Anyone manually verifying Mozilla's GPG signatures, however, will need to import the new signing key and the revocation for the old one. The change is a little more involved for users who installed Firefox through Mozilla's RPM repository. On Fedora 43 and later, DNF should download the updated key during the next Firefox update, although users will be asked to approve its import. Mozilla says users running Fedora 42 or earlier, RHEL, Rocky Linux, AlmaLinux, openSUSE, or SUSE will need to remove the old key and manually import its replacement. Thunderbird users don't have to worry about RPM-specific shenanigans, as Mozilla doesn't provide official RPM packages for the email client. www.theregister.com - Articles | 2026-08-11

Researchers find standards-compliant functionality can be abused to hijack modems, downgrade connections, and even execute code Researchers have found that a malicious SIM card can tell some phones and cellular-connected devices to leak data, drop to 2G, shut themselves down, or even execute code, all thanks to functionality that's supposed to be there. The research [PDF], presented at the USENIX WOOT conference in Baltimore this week, examines proactive SIM functionality, which allows a SIM to issue commands to the device hosting it. One of those is RUN AT, which allows the SIM to request the execution of AT commands, the instruction set that's been bossing modems around since the 1980s. Give that ability to a hostile SIM, and things get rather more interesting. Tomasz Piotr Lisowski and Marius Muench of the University of Birmingham, working with Fuzzware's Kristian Covic, built a toolkit called CATANA to see what a malicious SIM could get away with. They tested 26 devices – 18 smartphones and eight IoT modems – and found that nine exposed an AT command interface to the SIM. The IoT kit was particularly accommodating, with seven of the eight modems exposing it. The researchers uncovered four vulnerabilities and demonstrated attacks including code execution, arbitrary file reads, denial of service, and downgrading connections to 2G. "The fascinating part here is that the proactive capabilities of a SIM and the resulting attack surface is explicitly defined in the technical specifications for cellular communication," said Muench, making the attacks "specification-compliant." He added that hostile SIMs are still missing from many threat models despite previous research and leaked intelligence documents demonstrating the risks. The researchers put that access to work on an Autel EV charger fitted with a Quectel EC25-AFX cellular module. By sending commands from the SIM, they were able to exploit a command injection bug in the modem's Linux-based application processor and achieve code execution. On an Oppo Reno14 F 5G, meanwhile, they found 198 AT commands and variants available through the SIM interface. Among them were commands that could power down the handset, kill its modem, or shove it back onto 2G. The last trick was particularly stubborn: toggling airplane mode, disabling the SIM, and changing the phone's network settings all failed to reverse the downgrade. The team also demonstrated file theft against a Quectel EG25-G modem, combining a malicious symbolic link with SIM-originating commands to email a targeted file to an attacker-controlled server. Before you start eyeing your SIM tray suspiciously, there is a catch: the attacks require control of the SIM itself. That could come through compromised SIM software, physical tampering, abuse of remote administration by a malicious or breached operator, or supply chain shenanigans. The researchers also found that vulnerable versions of Android allowed a hostile SIM to invoke the standardized LAUNCH BROWSER command and open an attacker-controlled website without user interaction, even while the phone was locked. Google tracked the flaw as CVE-2025-48618 and patched Android 13 through 16 in December 2025. The researchers disclosed their findings to Google, Oppo, Quectel, Semtech, and Qualcomm in March, followed by the GSMA in May. Qualcomm has since produced a hardened configuration that disables the SIM AT interface by default, while the GSMA is tracking the wider issue as CVD-2026-0122. The researchers reckon the best long-term answer is to retire RUN AT and other risky proactive SIM functionality. Modern smartphones appear to have largely got the memo. Why hybrid clouds break and what to do about it

SPONSORED EXPLAINER: There's nothing wrong with hybrid cloud. It's the one-size-fits-all strategy on top of it that's the problem. Hybrid cloud breaks down when every environment has its own provisioning process, security model, monitoring stack, and cost structure. In practice, many hybrid strategies were built around where infrastructure happened to live, not around each workload’s latency, data governance, resilience, performance, and cost requirements. Most enterprises didn't choose hybrid so much as stumble into it. A cloud-first push might have handled greenfield developments, while regulated workloads stayed put. Acquisitions arrived with their own datacenters. Edge deployments came in through operational technology teams, and each layer picked up its own tools, its own security model, and its own bill. The result is a hybrid cloud environment that behaves like three people in a trenchcoat. It looks unified on the surface if you squint hard enough, but still operates as separate environments for provisioning, security, observability, troubleshooting, and cost management. Without those, the "hybrid" part describes what you own, not how you run it. When hybrid cloud gets treated as a collection of disconnected environments instead of an operating model, it starts to feel broken. The fix is not another isolated management console. It is a common operating model that lets teams place workloads based on technical and business requirements, then manage provisioning, policy, observability, and cost consistently across environments. Begin by examining the workload's characteristics. What performance does it need? What data does it touch, and where is that data governed? What does downtime cost, and which regulator has an opinion about it? The answers point to a likely home for that workload. Public cloud infrastructure might work for elastic front ends, while private cloud might be best for sensitive workloads. Edge computing environments will likely hold anything that can't afford the round trip back to home base. Proper workload analysis might flag a high-throughput data platform as a candidate for colocation, or on-premises for entrenched legacy systems that haven't yet proven financially viable to move. Placement gets shaped by a range of parameters, including: Deliberate workload placement. HPE helps customers assess hybrid estates, align workloads to the right environments, and apply a more consistent cloud operating model across distributed infrastructure. A consistent operating model. One set of tools handles provisioning, security, observability, and cost wherever the workload lives. Consistency turns a mixed estate into a working hybrid model. AI without the data-shipping bill. AI workloads often need to stay close to large, governed datasets. A workload-first approach helps teams avoid unnecessary data movement while still giving developers and data teams access to cloud-like agility. The payoff isn't just a cheaper cloud bill, though few will turn away that opportunity. It's the ability to move workloads where they belong without navigating another isolated management console every time. The next phase of hybrid cloud success will come from an intentional approach, in which organizations acknowledge each workload's differences while still managing the entire estate uniformly. Last week, I headed 30 miles south of San Francisco to a hotel in Mountain View, California, to join some of the most accomplished, and some of the most promising, AI researchers in the world. I was hosting roundtable interviews and speaking at a media training for a convening of the Schmidt Sciences AI2050 program, an initiative funded by Eric and Wendy Schmidt that supports academics whose work involves AI. The fellows list is a who’s who of AI luminaries, and though not all of them made it out to the Bay, every time I turned a corner I saw a scientist whom I’d interviewed previously or whose research I admired. (Full disclosure: I received a science communication award funded by Schmidt Sciences in 2024.) It’s a weird time for university AI researchers, who make up most of the AI2050 group. In the past four years, AI research has reoriented around large language models, and its cutting edge has moved from academic institutions to private companies. Universities simply can’t afford the GPUs required to train and run frontier models, and even if they could, Anthropic and OpenAI aren’t letting anyone else see the inner details of Claude or ChatGPT. In a conversation over lunch, Nika Haghtalab, a computer science professor at UC Berkeley, said that being an AI academic these days was like being a biologist in a world in which private companies had exclusive control over the gene-editing tool CRISPR. Experts outside the frontier labs can study how ChatGPT and Claude behave, but they can’t do any detailed research on the design and training of those tools, nor can they steer that design or training themselves. The AI2050 program does offer fellows some funding that they can use to buy GPUs, which some researchers I spoke with said was a major benefit of participating in the program. But money remains a pressing concern, especially given the reduction of federal scientific funding in the United States. AI for science needs reasoning, not just data

Every few decades, someone announces that science has reached its end. In 1903, the revered physicist Albert Michelson wrote that the “facts of physical science have all been discovered.” In the 1980s, Stephen Hawking predicted that theoretical physics might be finished by the end of the century. With the explosive arrival of artificial intelligence, the feeling is in the air again—this time accompanied by a Nobel Prize. In 2024, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper of Google DeepMind were awarded part of the Nobel in chemistry for their neural network AlphaFold, which predicts the three-dimensional structures of proteins by learning from thousands of experimentally measured shapes. This devilish problem had resisted systematic attacks for half a century; AlphaFold seemed to have solved it once and for all, and the world became fixated on the promise of its approach. Hassabis and his team called AlphaFold “the template for how AI can accelerate all of science to digital speed.” A wave of startups building foundation models for biology, chemistry, and materials discovery raised billions of dollars, buoyed by DeepMind’s success. AlphaFold had shown that the combination of AI and sufficient data could make groundbreaking discoveries (even if we did not understand the underlying mechanisms involved), and it seemed, once again, that a path through the rest of science was laid out before us. To be sure, AI will bring extraordinary changes to science, but it has become increasingly clear that AlphaFold, and things like it, may not be the best template for that metamorphosis. Though it is a profound achievement, the conditions that produced the likes of AlphaFold are rare, and the time it will take to meet those conditions in other fields will be measured in decades, not years. Instead, the acceleration of science will come about thanks to another approach: AI agents. The primary condition for AlphaFold’s success was the existence of the Protein Data Bank, a data set of roughly 170,000 experimentally validated protein structures on which DeepMind’s team could train its model. The creation of the Protein Data Bank was not simple: It took 53 years of international scientific cooperation and, by a recent estimate, roughly $21 billion worth of experimental work to assemble. Efforts of that scale are infamously difficult to fund, next to impossible to coordinate, and hugely time-consuming to execute; they have often been unsuccessful as a result. But even in fields with the requisite cohesion and resources, and where the relevant data are not rendered inaccessible by commercial ownership, another barrier is too little discussed: the scientific impossibility of generating comparable data. In the case of protein structures, the key experimental technique—protein crystallography—is an unusually replicable and dependable tool, so much so that over 25 Nobel Prizes have relied on it. But in most of experimental science, results vary more often than not. Cell lines drift. Chemicals have trace contaminants. Lab humidity changes. The creation of measured datasets that will be consistent enough, accurate enough, precise enough, and scalable enough to train a modern neural network in biology or most of chemistry would require new kinds of measurement and new standardized approaches—none of which will be ready anytime soon. Of course, there are a handful of fields where these requirements are met: weather forecasting, much of genomics, very limited areas of chemistry. These may see AlphaFold-style breakthroughs soon, if they haven’t already. Government support for the production and coordination of those datasets will be critical, as the US National Security Commission on Emerging Biotechnology has argued. But for most open questions in science, we will need a different plan, at least in the short term. Luckily, something quieter and more modest has begun to show promise. Scientists have always reasoned under uncertainty. Biologists working to identify new drug targets have never had perfect datasets. Instead, they combine docking calculations and known structures, factor in molecular dynamics, run a handful of binding assays, and use their judgment to weigh each method according to its particular strengths and points of failure. The skill of science is not in any single tool; it is synthesizing what many tools produce, and revising the results as the evidence comes in. This is how most working research actually proceeds. But until very recently, no software could do it. Agents now can. Simply put, an agent is an AI reasoning engine that has been given access to tools—digital or physical—and the capabilities to use them. Over the last few years, a fundamental architectural shift in AI has enabled the rapid proliferation of these programs, which are powered by large language models, dramatically reducing the need for scientifically specialized datasets. For science, this technological advancement represents a foundational change: it has allowed us to create digital tools that can mimic the iterative, highly contingent process of actual research. While tools like AlphaFold apply a powerful approach to a limited question, agents are inherently generalists. They do not represent a new way to do science—instead, they digitally model the human process of discovery. Consider Google’s AI Co-Scientist, announced in May. Researchers gave it a one-page brief and a goal: Figure out how antibiotic resistance spreads between bacterial species, a key driver of drug-resistant infections. The system spun up sub-agents. One drafted hypotheses from the literature. Another picked them apart like a peer reviewer. A third ran tournaments to rank the strongest candidates. A fourth refined the winning hypothesis. The agent concluded that resistance genes were hitching rides on bacterial viruses, borrowing whichever virus could ferry them into a new host. The hypothesis was correct. Researchers at Imperial College London had spent a decade reaching the same conclusion through painstaking wet-lab work; their paper, previously unseen by Co-Scientist, was still in peer review. Agents like Co-Scientist are still novel tools, and there are real challenges to overcome before they become a ubiquitous part of the scientific process: They are still liable to hallucinate, their judgment is not consistent, and they have memory and input constraints that limit the time they can run autonomously. But these technical barriers will fall away, and as they do we will begin to notice the compounding effects of scientific agents on the reliability, consistency, and velocity with which science is done. Perhaps most notably, agents offer a structural fix for science’s “reproducibility crisis,” the widespread problem of researchers’ inability to replicate each other’s results. For decades, the scientific community has begged researchers to share their raw data and exact code in an effort to standardize experimental processes. But researchers have long resisted this tedious administrative work, which happens after the interesting science is already done. Agents, in contrast, automatically log every move they make, creating an exact record of the method that led to their results and allowing for precise replication. A second consequence will be an amplification of scientific memory. The transfer of knowledge between researchers is a famously murky process; if it isn’t done over years of training and observation, graduate students are left to pore through the messy lab notebooks kept by decades of predecessors, looking for the details that will make or break their protocol. As agents become an increasingly large part of the scientific process, though, a lab’s entire scientific history will be recorded in a central, standardized repository of institutional knowledge. But the most important impact of agents will be speed. In any field, when testing an idea takes less time than arguing about it in a meeting, people stop debating and just run the test. An agent that can read a thousand papers in an hour, design 500 molecules, and learn from its failed tests by morning will bring down the cost of experimentation and fundamentally change the pace at which science gets done. It will also give researchers the freedom to chase bold, strange questions they never would have risked their time on before, opening scientific doors we have yet to imagine. While the AlphaFold template will certainly be key to incredible discoveries, it alone will not bring us to the end of science. Instead, the shift toward agentic AI represents a much rarer tier of breakthrough: a tool that envelops every field of science at once. Historically, tools of such scope have arrived just a handful of times: calculus, statistical inference, spectroscopy, the computer. Each revealed a world of problems no one had thought to formulate, and those problems, in turn, defined their fields anew. With agents, another such transformation is upon us. Way back in the summer of 2017, AI researchers at Google put out a paper called “Attention Is All You Need,” in which they described a new type of neural network called a transformer. It proved to be very good at processing long sequences of data, especially text. Nine years on, transformers are the engines inside every major large language model on the market. “The entire AI industry is built on transformers,” says Justin Dangel, cofounder and CEO of the AI startup Subquadratic. “They are one of the most important innovations in the history of computer science, and they’ve changed the world.” But transformers are starting to show their age. Many of the recent advances in LLMs, such as the development of so-called reasoning models and their ability to handle large amounts of input at once, are not neat extensions of that core technology but workarounds that patch over some of its fundamental flaws. A growing number of scientists and engineers are now asking what’s coming next. LLMs are not going anywhere, but the way they get built is up for grabs. (MIT Technology Review dubbed this future generation of models LLMs+ in this year’s list of the 10 things that matter in AI.) Enter a wave of startups hoping to push the boundaries of this boomtown technology. Some will no doubt fail—but they have everything to play for and far less to lose than the companies at the front of the pack today. But first, the problem. The key strength of transformers lies in a mechanism called dense attention, which encodes the meaning of a block of text in a series of numbers. The process involves comparing every word (or part of a word, known as a token) in that text with every other word via a form of multiplication. Dense attention can capture the meaning of text with remarkable accuracy. But as the length of that text grows, the number of computations needed to process it adds up fast. A document 10,000 words long might require a transformer to perform 50 million multiplications. That’s the main reason LLMs suck up so much power. The costs are huge. OpenAI is set to spend $50 billion on computing this year, according to the company’s president, Greg Brockman. And the International Energy Agency predicts that the total amount of electricity consumed by data centers will double by 2030. What’s more, transformers struggle with what many of the latest models are designed to do. Because of the way they process text word by word, transformers are not great at keeping track of a lot of information at once (in other words, what's known as their context window cannot get too large). And yet if LLMs are to carry out harder tasks, they will need to take in larger amounts of data: a whole library of documents, an entire code base, or in the case of agents, output from other LLMs. As for reasoning models, they work by writing notes to themselves (in a kind of scratch pad known as a chain of thought) and then reading them back, which again adds to the amount of data to stay on top of. As LLMs get bigger and better, transformers have become a bottleneck. The technology’s key strength is now a limitation. Here are four new ideas for how to solve the transformer problem—innovations that could change LLMs for good, making them faster, far more efficient, and (maybe) even smarter. An obvious way to make LLMs faster and cheaper is to tackle the problem head on and change the way attention works. Swapping out dense attention for a mechanism called sparse attention, which runs calculations on only some pairings of words in a block of text instead of all of them, can radically reduce the amount of computation LLMs need to do. Researchers have come up with plenty of sparse attention mechanisms over the years. The problem is that none of them were as good as dense attention at capturing meaning. That might have changed. Subquadratic, a startup based in Miami, claims it has invented the first sparse attention mechanism that rivals top mainstream LLMs on a handful of tasks, including search and coding. It’s a huge claim (and some people in the industry remain skeptical). Subquadratic says its model, SubQ, works by figuring out on the fly—for each piece of text it is given—which words matter and which don’t. The company also claims that thousands have signed up to its waitlist and plans to make the model widely available soon. Meanwhile, Manifest AI, a startup based in San Francisco, is coming at the problem from a different angle. Instead of changing how attention works, it is replacing it with something else. It has developed a mechanism it calls power retention, which stores only the most relevant information for a given task and ensures that the amount of data an LLM has to keep track of doesn’t blow up. Attention mechanisms force LLMs to keep track of everything in their context window. A sparse attention model (such as SubQ) throws out a lot of the individual words, but it still retains a rough picture of everything it has seen. In contrast, power retention works by providing the model with a rolling summary of its context window. As new information is added, less relevant information is dropped. The basic principle of retention has been around for a decade. Manifest AI claims it has updated those techniques to build models that can stand up to transformer-based LLMs for the first time. The company says it is possible to adapt a transformer model into a power retention model with minimal retraining. To demonstrate this, it has turned an existing open-source coding LLM called StarCoder into a version that uses power retention, called PowerCoder. It has also released a model called Brumby, which it claims rivals some versions of Alibaba’s popular open-source model Qwen. Manifest AI wants its power retention tech to become the go-to solution when LLMs need to carry out tasks that involve processing huge amounts of data. There are many useful applications, Manifest AI’s cofounder and CTO, Carles Gelada, claimed in a video announcing his company’s technology last year—from analyzing videos that are hours long to building agents that can stay on task for weeks at a time. Liquid AI, an MIT spinout based in Cambridge, Massachusetts, hasn’t changed or ditched transformers fully but pairs them with its own tech, liquid neural networks, to build what cofounder and CEO Ramin Hasani calls LFMs (liquid foundation models). Liquid AI’s models are far smaller and use less energy than most LLMs. The firm builds models for car makers, including Mercedes, which run on the small chips inside vehicles. Its latest models can run on a Raspberry Pi, a low-powered hobbyist computer that costs $50. Its models are available for free to any organization with an annual revenue less than $10 million. And they have proved popular: The company has racked up almost 34 million downloads, says Hasani. Liquid neural networks were inspired by worm brains. They are an extension of another type of neural network that predates transformers, called convolutional networks. The key innovation is a mechanism that lets a model adapt its behavior to new information, so it can learn as it goes. That’s not possible with transformers: Once a model is trained, its behavior is fixed. Liquid AI’s first models were pretty basic but could fly drones or drive vehicles. With LFMs, the company is trying to scale up its technology to compete with mainstream LLMs. Its new models match the performance of rivals four times bigger, including versions of Alibaba’s Qwen and Google’s open-source LLM Gemma. A typical LLM is built from a stack of transformers wired together. Liquid AI’s recent LFMs are hybrid models made up of 20% transformers and 80% liquid neural networks. That ratio was hit upon by another AI system that Liquid AI has built, which it uses to help design all its models. “It’s the core technology of our company right now,” says Hasani. This designer AI sifts through many different combinations of neural networks—liquid, convolutional, and more, as well as transformers—and comes up with designs that bolt different ones together to hit a sweet spot of performance and efficiency. Hasani thinks transformers were just the beginning: “Your brain is an AGI system, you know, and it operates with 20 watts of power. How is it possible? We can get a lot more innovative.” Almost all LLMs produce their output one word at a time. It makes sense, because that is how people speak and write. But for computers, it’s very inefficient. It is faster and cheaper for LLMs to generate text all at once—spitting out whole sentences or paragraphs in one shot. That’s the approach taken by Inception, a startup based in Palo Alto, California, which is building LLMs using a technique called diffusion. Diffusion is better known as the technology that drives most image and video generation models. Diffusion models are trained to take a random grid of pixels—like the static on an old TV set—and turn it into an image. They do this by working on all the pixels at the same time, figuring out which need changing to make the static look more like a high-definition photo. It turns out this process works on text too. Inception has trained its LLMs to take a random string of words and turn it into sentences that make sense. Diffusion LLMs still use transformers to encode meaning, but by producing whole blocks of text at once, they make transformers do more for less. “You’re still using a big transformer model, but you can predict many tokens at the same time,” says Inception’s cofounder and CEO, Stefano Ermon. “That’s why these models are so much faster and cost-efficient compared to what most other people are building today.” The challenge was to take a technology designed for image generation and apply it to text. With images, if you need to change a blue pixel to a red one you can step through intermediate colors, says Ermon. That doesn’t work with text: “When you have ‘cat’ and ‘dog,’ there is not really something in between.” Ermon is also a researcher at Stanford University. In 2024, he and a pair of his Stanford colleagues figured out the math to make diffusion models work with text. They trained a diffusion model that matched the performance of GPT-2—an LLM that OpenAI built in 2019—but was 10 times faster. It was enough for Ermon to spin out a company. Today he has his sights on the big league. Inception claims its latest model, Mercury 2, performs as well as some of OpenAI’s GPT-4 models, released in 2023, but again 10 times faster. “We’re bullish about this approach because it’s the one that is going to scale up,” says Ermon. The only things that matter are speed and cost, he adds: “Ultimately, the currency is going to be intelligence per dollar.” Inception is not the only company betting on diffusion. Google is also experimenting with this approach and has built a prototype LLM called Diffusion Gemma. But Ermon is not worried about the competition. “I think it's validating,” he says. “This is the future.” Pathway, another startup based in Palo Alto, is perhaps the most extreme of this new bunch. It wants to free LLMs from the constraints of language. The firm has built a type of LLM called Dragon Hatchling (named after the dragons in Terry Pratchett’s novel Color of Magic, which materialize if you think about them hard enough). Its standout result so far is a high score on a benchmark that pits LLMs against more than 250,000 very hard sudoku puzzles. Dragon Hatchling beat more than 97% of the puzzles; several leading LLMs from the top labs failed to solve any. The point Pathway wants to make is that despite their remarkable success at many different tasks, there are still crucial classes of problems where LLMs fail. Sudoku is just one example. If we want LLMs to come up with genuine, novel solutions to real problems, we need to move beyond transformers, says Pathway’s cofounder and CEO, Zuzanna Stamirowska. That’s because transformers force LLMs to do everything with text. But language is not the best tool for certain kinds of reasoning. “It’s very difficult to represent a sudoku board word by word,” says Stamirowska. Pathway’s solution is to change the math behind the transformer, replacing the attention mechanism with a mathematical structure called a state space. Instead of encoding information word by word, state spaces compress it into a more abstract representation. Using this technique, Dragon Hatchling can still process and produce text, but it can also mimic forms of reasoning that do not involve sequences of words. This not only makes Pathway’s model more efficient, but (in theory) it lets it take on tasks that other LLMs cannot do. Think of chess or mathematics—those kinds of puzzles are not held in your head as a long sentence, says Stamirowska: “The eureka moment that pops up in your brain isn’t necessarily in language. We would argue that if you have to reason in language, you’re somehow constrained.” Stamirowska admits that a mainstream LLM could read a book about how to solve sudoku and then write code to do it. But we want to build models with more than book smarts, she says: “The hope for AI is not to solve sudoku; it’s to cure cancer. There’s not a book for that.” “Transformers are an engineering convenience that we fell on,” she adds. “It started a religion, but it’s silly to think that a breakthrough won’t happen again.” Subquadratic has now shared more details about its new model. But some are still skeptical. It makes it easy to trick them into doing things they shouldn’t, such as telling you how to sabotage an aircraft’s navigation system.
