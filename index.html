Mythos attack on 3rd-round PQC algorithm candidate puts it out of commission

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-07-29

Visit Original Website
A quantum-resistant cryptography algorithm that was under consideration as an official US standard has been taken out of the running after an Anthropic security model helped find a flaw that rendered it broken. The algorithm is known as HAWK. It’s a digital signature scheme designed to withstand future attacks from quantum computers. HAWK had survived two rounds of testing by NIST (the National Institute of Standards and Technology) for evaluating the security of PQC (post-quantum cryptographic) algorithms through widespread testing. HAWK was in a third round of testing designed to catch precisely the kinds of flaws Mythos helped uncover. Following Anthropic’s Monday announcement of the results, the developer of HAWK said Tuesday he was withdrawing it. Even before the development, Anthropic was hailing the results of the two cryptographic problems it threw at its Mythos AI security model. The model found weaknesses in the mathematical problems underpinning HAWK and, separately, the widely used AES cipher. Despite the withdrawing of HAWK, it’s hard to know how much of the company’s reporting is marketing hype, but the findings are still worth paying attention to because they could signal important advances in breaking cryptography that’s crucial to privacy and security. Before digging into the results, a few caveats. First, the outcomes are incremental. They don’t break any of the cryptosystems anyone relies on today. Instead, they reveal methods for moderately reducing the work that would be required to defeat the systems. Second, the cryptosystems tested were weakened versions of the ones defined in their formal specifications. Such “challenge instances” are provided by the specification authors for use in adversarial peer review. It’s standard to use the weakened versions in testing, but the real ones are considerably more robust in production settings. Third, even with the improvement, the underlying “primitives”—meaning the underlying mathematical problems that form the basic building blocks of cryptosystems—remain safe, at least for now. Lastly, both of the attacks use methods that would likely be infeasible outside of testing environments. Anthropic said its Mythos model—which currently remains available only to a select group of trusted users—was able to advance attacks against two cryptosystems. The first system is HAWK, a digital signature scheme designed to withstand future attacks from quantum computers. With about 60 hours of work and about $100,000 of compute cost, an Anthropic researcher with no expertise in cryptography prompted Mythos to improve the best-known existing attack on the algorithm that effectively cut its key strength in half. The math HAWK’s security relies on is the hardness of the Lattice Isomorphism Problem, which, unlike today’s most used digital signature schemes, is believed to be safe from quantum computing attacks. The best-known classical computing attack to solve this problem works by finding what are known as automorphism symmetries. Mythos outputted a previously unknown method for finding such symmetries by definition broke the algorithm. The weakness can be mitigated by doubling the key size, but the added computation makes HAWK less desirable than available PQC signing algorithms. In academics cryptographic algorithms are considered broken when weaknesses allow an adversary to derive a key faster than is possible using a brute-force attack. Matthew Green, a Johns Hopkins professor and expert in cryptography, said the impressive thing about the discovery was its reliance on several existing methods that no one previously thought to put together. “What’s particularly concerning (and so especially ripe for AI) is that the attack does not invent fundamentally new mathematics,” Green wrote. “It simply extends a bunch of tools that were lying around and well-known, and gets a good result.” To find the attack, Claude Mythos Preview worked semi-autonomously in an agentic harness, with occasional human guidance and nontechnical direction. Mythos found the attack after an extensive literature review to understand the state of the art, and substantial mathematical reasoning and computational experiments. After finding the attack, Mythos implemented an end-to-end verification pipeline to convince itself—and the human operator—of the attack’s correctness. To find the improved method, Mythos deployed two separate agents that worked largely independently. One initially rejected the method as unworkable. The second found a way to make it work. The agents eventually worked in unison until they produced an agreement that the improved attack was effective. (As with the description of the attack against AES later in this article, the HAWK attack methodology has been simplified. For the full details, see the Anthropic post or two longer papers on the HAWK and AES attacks, respectively.) Sophie Schmieg, an expert in PQC at Google, said HAWK was already suspected to have weaknesses that would eventually be found. Still, the method for halving the key strength found through Mythos made the candidate algorithm less competitive than existing PQC digital signature schemes such as ML-DSA and FN-DSA. “Basically with this paper, HAWK is dead,” she wrote. The attack against AES produced less dramatic results. It’s based on an improvement found through Mythos for performing a “meet-in-the-middle” attack, which is used to derive a key under a chosen plaintext threat model, the best-known existing attack against AES. The technique inputs large numbers of known plaintext into the crypto system and analyzes the encrypted output for clues that, with enough inputs, eventually reveal an unknown key. Previously, the best-known meet-in-the-middle attack against AES required roughly 2105 plaintext inputs, a number large enough to make the method infeasible. Mythos helped to find a new meet-in-the-middle technique that relies on a Möbius Bridge, a more sophisticated fingerprinting algorithm used in meet-in-the-middle attacks. Using it, Green said, the code Mythos produced was able to reduce the number of required inputs to 289. Anthropic said that savings can reduce the time required for such attacks by 200- to 800-fold. The ability to produce that many inputs makes the attack beyond reach outside of the laboratory. Further, the actual speed-up is unknown, since the weakened AES algorithm tested used only 7 rounds. Specification-compliant AES, Green said, uses 10, 12, or 14 rounds, depending on key size. Anthropic is careful to explicitly spell out most of these caveats. The Monday blog post goes on to argue, however, that the results are nonetheless meaningful and could ultimately fundamentally disrupt the process of cryptanalysis, or the adversarial testing of cryptosystems. “The cybersecurity community is now grappling with the fact that language models are able to discover so many bugs that the standard human processes (like vulnerability triage, verification, and remediation) struggle to keep up,” Anthropic wrote. “We predict that the same will soon be true in academic cryptography research. As language models increasingly produce novel research outputs autonomously, human researchers may become bottlenecked on studying and validating these results for technical validity, novelty, and utility.” Not mentioned in Anthropic’s report is whether its researchers used Mythos to attack more tested cryptosystems, such as elliptic curve cryptography and RSA. Attack improvements against these systems would be more impressive. By achieving the most impressive result against an algorithm still in its infancy, it’s not clear how much of an advantage Mythos truly provided. There’s no way of knowing if researchers using conventional cryptanalysis techniques were already close to discovering the same attack. Ultimately, the lesson from the research is simple. AI-assisted cryptanalysis remains untested, and providers of these platforms have a vested interest in exaggerating their benefits. At the same time, there’s growing evidence that LLMs may provide significant advantages in finding cryptographic weaknesses. It would be a mistake to conclude that LLMs won’t one day play an important role in the race between securing and compromising our most vital assets. Elon Musk has settled his lawsuit with advertisers that he once claimed should be criminally prosecuted for refusing to advertise on his social media platform, X. In a joint statement that reveals little about the deal, the World Federation of Advertisers (WFA) and X said that they were “putting the litigation involving the Global Alliance for Responsible Media (GARM) behind them.” X sued the WFA in 2024, shortly after a series of brands boycotting X caused the platform’s revenue to drop by $1.5 billion by the end of 2023. Musk became upset, declaring “it is war” on X about eight months after he told advertisers who refused to buy X ads to “go fuck yourself.” According to Musk, he had “no choice but to file suit against the perpetrators and collaborators in the advertising boycott racket,” who he tried to argue were violating antitrust laws by illegally conspiring to tank X revenue. A lot has changed since Musk filed the lawsuit. Most relevant to the lawsuit, GARM was quickly disbanded after Musk called out the small not-for-profit initiative for setting brand standards that X said led advertisers to steer clear of X. The point of the initiative was “to help the industry address the challenge of illegal or harmful content on digital media platforms and its monetization via advertising,” a now-shuttered website said. But Musk claimed GARM was instead trying to monopolize which content gets monetized online, and the effort’s dissolution will remain his biggest win in the case. The joint statement confirmed that GARM will remain inactive as a result of the WFA’s settlement with X. No other concessions were discussed. The rest of the statement claims that the settlement “resets the relationship between the two organizations,” which are now supposedly “fully aligned in the view that brands, platforms, and consumers will all benefit from brand-safety innovation.” Additionally, the WFA said it is aligned with X on “its commitment to freedom of speech,” which seems to be a nod to the controversial reports on X content that Musk said triggered his “war” with advertisers. Those reports found that as hateful content was increasing on the platform, X ad controls weren’t working to stop big brands’ ads from appearing next to posts touting Hitler and the Nazi Party. It’s unclear why advertisers settled, since a court ruled in March that the ad boycott was perfectly legal and X’s antitrust claims fell apart without proof of consumer harm. The next month, X appealed, but it appeared to be dragging its feet in filing a brief as an appellant, asking the court for an extension until August. Perhaps settlement negotiations were already underway. For Musk, the settlement comes shortly after the launch of X Money, a payments product offered through his social media platform that he hopes will help X be less reliant on advertisers for revenue. Whether X Money can succeed at the scale required to meaningfully rival X’s advertising business will likely depend on X removing barriers to adoption. Those include the possibility that X Money transactions could be declined if an automated support error mistakenly suspends a user’s X account without warning. There’s also the simpler problem that X Money isn’t available in all 50 states.

Who wins and who loses after US bans foreign robots?

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-07-29

Visit Original Website
Humanoid robots and four-legged robots made in China fall squarely under a new ban imposed by the US Federal Communications Commission on foreign-made robots. But the sweeping prohibition covers a wide variety of robots manufactured anywhere abroad, including the newest robot vacuum cleaners. The Trump administration’s FCC justified the ban on foreign-made robots by referencing cybersecurity vulnerabilities previously discovered in robots specifically made by Chinese companies. On July 28, the federal agency added “foreign-produced advanced robotic devices” to its Covered List of technologies “deemed to pose an unacceptable risk to the national security of the US or the safety and security of US persons.” However, the FCC cannot update the Covered List on its own initiative and is required by law to follow the direction of a “qualifying national security authority.” The agency describes the White House as having “convened an executive branch interagency body” that “included appropriate national security agencies,” which determined foreign-made robots as presenting a national security risk. So what foreign-made robots are prohibited? The ban defines “advanced robotic devices” as being “a mechanical mobile device, including autonomous mobile robots, humanoid robots, and quadrupeds” that can move around on the ground and operate either autonomously on their own or remotely under the control of a human. The definition also describes covered robots as having sensors capable of perceiving the robot’s environment, network connectivity such as Bluetooth or satellite connections with speeds surpassing 200 kbps in either direction, and software or AI models running onboard the robot or remotely from cloud computing servers. Such an import ban would apply to some of the most affordable robots primarily produced by Chinese companies, including Unitree’s humanoid robots that are used by robotics labs and researchers for tasks such as experimental robot surgeries. US consumers would also likely lose access to the newest robot vacuum cleaners that are mainly manufactured by Chinese companies such as Roborock. But the ban also broadly applies to foreign-made robots produced by countries nominally allied to the United States, including Japan, South Korea, and Germany. There are a number of exemptions. For example, the new ban defines covered robots as being any mobile device—potentially in combination with a docking station—weighing more than 4.4 pounds. That would permit US consumers to continue buying small robots, such as robotic toys, although most robot vacuums weigh more. The FCC ban also only covers new models of foreign-made robots. That means US consumers and businesses can continue purchasing existing robot models previously authorized by the FCC and continue to use previously purchased robots, but are blocked from purchasing newer, unauthorized models that come out from this point onward. The agency made clear that its foreign-made robot ban does not apply to trains, drones, underwater vehicles, and “connected vehicles” that drive on roads, such as robotaxis. The FCC already enacted an import ban on foreign-made drone models in December 2025. Similarly, it does not cover anything classified as devices by the Food and Drug Administration, including medical or surgical robots, external limb prostheses, and mobility assistive devices, such as wheelchairs. The FCC ban also specifically exempts fixed or stationary robots. That means US businesses can continue purchasing and using foreign-made industrial robot arms and other specialized, non-mobile robots. Notably, the US Department of Defense can determine that a foreign-made robot or class of such robots does not pose security risks—thereby exempting such robots from the FCC’s Covered List. The Department of Defense has been rebranded the Department of War under the Trump administration and is referenced as such in the FCC documents. The ban on foreign-made robots could theoretically encourage more US and foreign companies to set up manufacturing facilities in the United States. There are already multiple companies racing to scale up production of humanoid robots in US factories, including Agility Robotics, 1X Technologies, and Figure AI. Tesla has been attempting to shift production away from older electric vehicle models and toward its Optimus humanoid robot. Boston Dynamics has already been making its Atlas humanoid robot, along with its four-legged Spot robot and wheeled Stretch robot, at its main facility in Waltham, Massachusetts. The US robotics company is also planning to massively scale up manufacturing of the Atlas robot under South Korea’s Hyundai Motor Company, which gained full ownership of Boston Dynamics in July 2026. “This is one of the strongest technology-security actions in modern US history,” wrote Evan Beard, CEO of Standard Bots, in a LinkedIn post. “The message is unambiguous: robotics is a technology America must lead and own—and foreign-subsidized robots will not be allowed to unfairly dominate US robotics as they did solar.” Similar praise came from Rush Doshi, director of the Initiative on China Strategy at the Council on Foreign Relations, who, in a social media post, described the FCC decision as “one of the most significant actions taken so far in support of the US robotics ecosystem.” However, several robotics researchers and analysts interviewed by The Robot Report expressed skepticism about any potential boost to US competitiveness in robotics. Some even warned that the ban could prove counterproductive for US robotics efforts to develop humanoid robots. “In the near term, the measure could slow US physical AI innovation by cutting startups and researchers off from future low-cost Chinese platforms before comparable Western alternatives exist,” said Georg Stieler, a global robotics advisor and managing director for Asia at Stieler Technology & Market Advisory, in an interview with The Robot Report. US domestic production of robots lags behind China in terms of mass manufacturing at lower cost, said Rueben Scriven, a senior analyst at Interact Analysis. “This announcement is more likely to inhibit the US humanoid robotics industry, as the presence of low-cost Chinese humanoid robots has been helping educate the US market through promotional and entertainment use cases—an effect this policy risks undermining,” Scriven told The Robot Report. The outcome of previous FCC bans is also not necessarily encouraging. US government restrictions on drones made by Chinese companies such as DJI have failed to create a “competitive mass-market consumer-drone producer” based in the United States, Stieler pointed out. The Verge has also reported that newly created companies have popped up to sidestep the US ban on foreign drones by selling “barely disguised versions of DJI technology.”

The lawsuit claims the store manager took videos of the assaults and ordered employees to participate in the assaults and record their own videos. A Comcast store in Plainville, Connecticut, “had a policy that the highest-ranked Retail Sales Consultant for the prior month was instructed by his or her supervisor—Ms. Peterson, the Comcast Store manager—to tie the lowest-ranked sales consultant for the prior month to a chair in the back office and thereafter assault that person by violently smashing a cream pie in their face,” the complaint alleged. Plaintiff David Figueroa’s lawsuit said he was hired as a retail sales consultant on February 2, 2026, and was supervised by store manager Sully Fuentes Peterson. Figueroa alleges that Peterson “designed and implemented” the pie-in-face ritual to meet goals related to sales and positive responses in customer surveys. “Defendant did not inform the Plaintiff prior to his acceptance of Defendant’s offer of employment that the Comcast Store has a policy of subjecting Retail Sales Consultants to public assaults by co-workers—at the direction of Ms. Peterson, the store manager—for the purpose of increasing Defendant’s sales and profitability,” the lawsuit said. Figueroa resigned on February 27, and he alleges it was a constructive discharge. The lawsuit says the defendant, Comcast, was negligent because it “reasonably should have known” about the store management’s policies and that the policies could harm employees. Comcast “failed to properly supervise the Comcast Store’s management team,” allowing store management to humiliate employees “for the purpose of promoting the Defendant’s revenues and profits,” the lawsuit alleged. Comcast is likely to dispute the negligence claim and may dispute at least some of the factual allegations. Comcast provided a statement to Ars today in which it said it disagrees with the complaint, but did not provide any details. Comcast also did not say whether it still employs the manager. “The Company has zero tolerance for harassment, humiliation, or any behavior that compromises a respectful and safe workplace,” Comcast said in the statement provided to Ars. “This matter is in litigation so we will not comment on the specific allegations, other than to say that we disagree with the claims in the complaint and its characterization of the alleged events, and intend to fully respond through the legal process.” Figueroa is seeking compensatory damages for “past and future economic losses” and for emotional distress. The complaint was filed on July 9 in a Connecticut superior court and was described in articles published yesterday by Law360 and The Desk. Peterson maintained a chart in the back office that ranked store sales staff and “identified the employees who either had been recently assaulted or were scheduled to be assaulted in this way,” the lawsuit said. The complaint includes a photo of what is said to be the chart, which includes people’s first names followed by a month and a “pie face” emoji. “An objective, reasonable person in Plaintiff’s position would have felt compelled to resign his employment as a result of this work atmosphere that Defendant—through its employees, servants, agents—created,” the lawsuit said. “As a result of the pervasive, repeated, and ongoing threats of violence—and actual violence inflicted on co-workers not meeting sales goals—Plaintiff resigned his position with Defendant, resulting in his constructive discharge from Defendant’s employment.” As the case moves forward, the court could be shown video evidence of alleged pie assaults. Cody instructed Figueroa to send her a text message memorializing his concerns and reasons for wanting to resign, the lawsuit said. Figueroa said he did not receive a response to the text message or his complaints, and went ahead with his resignation. Comcast’s cable division has been trying to reverse customer losses over the past year by offering a five-year price guarantee and plans without data caps. The changes came after Comcast President Mike Cavanagh said in April 2025 that “we are not winning in the marketplace.”

Governments around the world are suddenly very keen on online age verification requirements. In some places, this takes the form of ID requirements to access adult content, but several US states have also focused on app stores. With these laws now going into effect, Google has announced an expansion of its beta Play Age Signals API, which it claims will allow developers to confirm user ages without impinging on privacy. Google’s system doesn’t require an ID or selfie verification—it’s connected to the company’s existing Family Link app. The idea is that parents will set age ranges for their kids’ managed accounts. For example, you might set a range like 16–17, allowing apps to tailor experiences and content for teenagers. Google notes that the ranges are dynamic and may change as young users get older. So a kid who’s 13 may be in the same app content bracket for a few years before hitting the next level. Parents have the option to set and share these ranges if they want that additional level of control over what their children can access in Google Play. However, it’s not mandatory for child accounts—it’s fully opt-in and can be turned off at any time. Google began testing the Play Age Signals API late last year as it became apparent that multiple states were going to put the onus of age verification on app stores. The Computer & Communications Industry Association (CCIA), which lobbies on behalf of tech firms, has opposed these laws. However, the US Supreme Court recently paused a lower court injunction, allowing the Texas law to take effect. Louisiana and Utah also have similar laws going into effect. The Play Store API was initially only available as a test in Brazil, but Google says it’s now beginning its global rollout. Australia and Canada will be the next regions to get the new API, and Google says developers in all countries, including the US, will have access to the Age Signals API by the end of 2026. This system won’t keep determined minors away from apps deemed too adult for them. Users must voluntarily share age data, and not all parents will bother setting this up. Kids can also just create alternative accounts and lie about their ages. Still, this should take care of Google’s compliance in any jurisdiction that requires store-level age verification for apps. However, it’s up to developers to use the API. The Texas law does require that developers actively use age-gating features in app stores. The Play Store already has content ratings for apps, so it should be relatively simple for developers to translate that to reported age ranges. Google and other companies will probably continue fighting laws that require them to be app gatekeepers, but that may be a lost cause if courts don’t accept the First Amendment argument against them.

They might see more clearly the way that people can become desperate for a savior, and realize what a fantasy it is to imagine that such people exist.

Toward the end of her speech in Detroit, Ocasio-Cortez said that El-Sayed’s campaign “represents the politics of the future.” In the present, though, can those politics win Democrats a Senate seat they can’t afford to lose? On the same Saturday of Abdul El-Sayed’s rally in Detroit, I met Haley Stevens at a campaign event in a small, Black-owned flower shop. I joined Stevens, the shop owner, and one other national reporter at a round table and we interviewed the congresswoman for about 20 minutes. We touched on everything from her no vote on a recent amendment to cut off military aid to Israel (“that one was way too far”) to why she considers herself more electable than El-Sayed (she’s won close races before; his only political experience is in a failed run for governor in 2018). Stevens speaks energetically in a thick regional accent. She throws every tangentially relevant piece of information she can—talking points, asides about old campaigns, recommendations for where to spend time in Detroit, references to bills she’s worked on—into each response. Occasionally, these responses address the question. Stevens had just spent a week enduring more hostility than she usually receives from her detractors in the online left. A pep talk of hers, in which she said, “You better believe I’m going to bring it with a little bit of enthusiasm, a little bit of energy, and a little bit of stick-it-to-’em,” like an old-timey football coach, went viral and was roundly mocked online. It earned enough derision that El-Sayed put out a statement to his followers about how such comments were “unkind and unhelpful” and that if you supported him, “please stop.” (Bernie Sanders used to put out these statements when he ran against Hillary Clinton, too.) In another familiar move, the Stevens campaign was in the process of leaning into it, with Stevens staffers showing me the box of freshly printed “STICK IT TO ‘EM” signs her team had just produced. A friend had even made it her ringtone, she told me. “But hey, whatever’s resonating, whatever brings a little bit of joy. Whatever brings a little bit of that stick it to ’em,” she said, doing the thing. Something felt disorienting about the event. On this valuable Saturday-morning time slot a couple of weeks before the primary, we were in a small flower shop in a pedestrian-unfriendly area with few, if any, walk-in customers to meet and greet. There were several campaign aides, though, as well as various campaign camerapeople taking photos behind us. Hours later, when I looked at Stevens’ X feed, I was surprised to see a photo of our interview, appearing as though we reporters were part of a bustling roundtable Stevens had held with voters.Absent the voters, and for the purposes of the photo op, we would have to do. Prior to this cycle, Stevens may not have had trouble getting through a primary. Michigan Democrats haven’t had a genuinely competitive Senate primary since 1994. (That was also the last year Democrats lost a Senate race in Michigan—which, move along, nothing to see here.) She’s a loyal House Democrat, a pragmatist more focused on legislation than spectacle. That’s usually enough to clear the primary field. Democratic voters in competitive states, always on edge about electability, have tended to follow the cues from Washington. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, who supports Stevens, doesn’t dictate each race, and some of his preferences are made more overt than others. But for all the grief Schumer gets about this top-down approach (“treating politicians like trading cards,” as El-Sayed put it in a rally in Grand Rapids), it’s worked pretty well for Democrats. In three separate cycles since I’ve been covering elections—2010, 2012, and 2022—Republicans have blown good chances at the Senate majority by nominating fatally flawed candidates. Democrats, who have less margin for error given their structural disadvantage in the chamber, haven’t. What Democrats have blown, though, is two out of three races against Donald Trump. That has created a trust gap between the party establishment and many Democratic voters. And it’s opened up the market to new types of candidates—candidates who will fight, and candidates who will champion progressive causes that the Democratic Party as a whole has never jumped on board with, like Medicare for all. At Stevens’ next event that Saturday, a summer picnic put on by Democrats in her Oakland County congressional district, I asked her why she thinks a progressive has been able to give someone like her a run for her money. “I’ll just say this: I signed up for this election knowing it was going to be a primary, right?” she said. “I knew it was going to be a race, and I decided to run because it was not the time to be comfortable.” This was the most I would get, but it was enough. She was telling me that she wasn’t so naive as to believe that after 2024, with withering rage directed at the Democratic establishment, and a large Arab American community in Michigan that had protest-voted against the Biden administration’s Gaza policy, that she—a card-carrying member of the Democratic establishment and close ally of AIPAC—would get a free primary ride. Maybe she didn’t get the best crowds. Maybe she was suffering an inordinate amount of vitriol online about her accent, her looks, her mannerisms, and her vast financial support from corporate PACs, wealthy donors, and the Israel lobby. But the immense national pressure of carrying a Democratic establishment to victory in a must-win state did not in any outward way appear to be breaking her spirits. Even though she was, by all accounts, struggling to do so. Before she gave a small speech at the picnic, Stevens took a few questions from reporters. As her team worked to assess which direction she should face for the best shot, she stood next to an unlit public charcoal grill and joked, “I’m hot, I’m hot!” Then, turning to me, she muttered, “Not according to the internet this week. I’m joking!” “She has events?” Abdul El-Sayed asked sarcastically the following day, in Grand Rapids, when I said I had attended some of Haley Stevens’ stops on the trail. I offered that they were certainly of a different nature from the rallies he was hosting. “She can’t have rallies of her own,” he said. “You have to have supporters to have rallies.” Stevens has supporters, though their idea of Saturday night may not involve going to campaign rallies. El-Sayed argues that the way she’s earning those votes, though, will never bring about real change. That’s because, stripped to their essence, modern Senate campaigns tend to be large cash transfers from wealthy donors to the owners of local television affiliates. The events on the ground are helpful for forming certain narratives in the press and earning coverage where you can. But campaigns rely on the tens of millions of dollars in advertisements to achieve the actual work of shifting poll numbers. As Stevens and I had been speaking at the flower shop, or the picnic, the real effort to get Haley Stevens into the general election was taking place on the small screen, through a constellation of super PACs. Chief among them is the United Democracy Project, AIPAC’s super PAC. Per the most recent Federal Election Commission filings, UDP has spent about $25 million in advertisements and direct mail. The second-largest super PAC, A Stronger Michigan, came into existence on June 1 and has plunked down nearly $17 million already. Per its mid-July filing, its funding had come almost entirely from a single nonprofit—making it “dark money” that doesn’t need to disclose its donors. A third, Unite to Win, has spent nearly $3 million. There are some smaller super PACs supporting El-Sayed. But all told, the pro-Stevens PACs were outspending their competition 12 to 1 as the race ramped up in mid-July. UDP’s ads don’t talk about Israel. One of its most-discussed spots features former President Barack Obama praising Stevens’ work on the auto industry rescue, but the clip is from a 2018 rally—Obama has not endorsed in the current race. The Stevens campaign, though, is banking heavily on turning out Black voters, among whom she enjoys a significant advantage, in metro Detroit. El-Sayed told me that before this race, he didn’t “understand just how craven big money could get.” He knew that there would be big money at play in the race, particularly AIPAC money, as he’s a vocal critic of Israel’s war in Gaza. But since seeing that Obama ad? “You know, when you have AIPAC running ads pretending like President Obama endorsed their candidate, even though they spent the end of his presidency opposing his principal foreign policy goal”—the Iran nuclear deal—“It’s just craven. It’s cynical. I think a lot of the establishment is just showing just how cynical they are about democracy itself.” This isn’t the first time AIPAC has come to Stevens’ rescue. In 2022, the year in which AIPAC formed UDP and began intervening in primaries, the super PAC helped carry her across the line in a member-on-member primary against Rep. Andy Levin. But its massive intervention in this race is as much about stopping El-Sayed as it is supporting Stevens. A swing-state Senate seat would be a groundbreaking development for those Democrats hoping to upend American policy toward Israel. And so, as El-Sayed put it to me, “they spend all this money to put my head on a pike.” But given Democrats’ turn against Israel over the past few years and AIPAC itself becoming such a lightning rod, El-Sayed has worked to use their heavy-handed involvement as his principal argument against Stevens. It’s hardly a new technique for a less funded candidate to complain that their opponent is bought and paid for by special interests. Usually the complainer loses, and life goes on. This time, given the bright spotlight on the race, AIPAC’s new villain status among many in the Democratic base, and El-Sayed’s gifts as a communicator, progressives hope this race can chart a new course for defeating big money in campaigns by weaponizing it in return. “For many years,” Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez told reporters after the Detroit rally, “we’ve been chipping away at how to figure out how we actually break up the system of big money in politics.” She listed some recent races where the progressive candidates had been outspent but won their primaries. “People are starting, and communities are starting, I think, to associate a lot of this large spending with the big money that requires it to be.” In 2020, four years after losing to Hillary Clinton, the presidential nomination was Bernie Sanders’ to lose. But South Carolina primary voters fled to the safety of Joe Biden, someone they, then the rest of the field and the party establishment, decided was “electable.” It was effectively over by Super Tuesday the following week. “Electability,” interpreted as risk aversion, has governed Democratic voting strategy in the high-stakes era of Donald Trump, standing as a bulwark against the inroads Sanders and his descendants have made into the party. Polls show Haley Stevens and Abdul El-Sayed performing about equally in hypothetical matchups against Mike Rogers, a non-crazy former House Republican who’s running again after he lost by only 19,000 votes to Sen. Elissa Slotkin two years ago. But Stevens more aptly fits the conventional understanding of electable, a point that her campaign is hammering home as its closing message. She’s not particularly ideological, and stays on message about affordability and stopping Trump’s corruption. “All I can speak to is my record that I have won tough primaries, and I’ve won tough general elections, and I have done so based on my passion, my commitment, my Michigan way of campaigning, fighting, and delivering for people,” she told me when I asked what makes her more electable. The subtext of her argument, though, is that El-Sayed specifically isn’t electable. El-Sayed has a history, which UDP has been eager to point out, of calling to defund the police, for example. He also supports abolishing Immigration and Customs Enforcement, a position that still gives many Democrats the willies even with all of ICE’s recent abuses. Republicans agree with the Stevens camp’s view that El-Sayed is the easier opponent. That’s why Senate Republicans ran a web ad in late June warning that El-Sayed was “too radical for Michigan”—a meddling tactic designed to boost his name recognition and make him seem especially cool to Democratic primary voters. And as CNN reported in late July, Rogers told supporters in a June phone call about El-Sayed that “if he wins, I think we’re in good shape.” El-Sayed isn’t really having it. “It’s funny watching folks make an argument about electability when electable candidates don’t need $50 million crutches,” he told me. “Just think about what this race would be if there was no outside spending at all. We wouldn’t be talking about any opposition. This would be such a lopsided race. Y’all wouldn’t be covering it.” But there’s another dimension around the “electability” conversation. El-Sayed is a Muslim and Arab American, and no such person has ever been elected to the U.S. Senate. El-Sayed and his surrogates insinuated that ultimately, this is what the “electability” talk is all about—a coded way to worry that his race and religion will prevent him from winning. “I want to tell you what they’re whispering about Michiganders out in Washington, because I’ve heard it myself,” Ocasio-Cortez told the crowd at the Detroit rally. “They’re saying a state like yours, and people like you, can’t elect an American like Abdul.” “They’ve taken to calling me ‘dangerous,’ ” El-Sayed said in his speech, before laying into some sarcasm. “Now, I don’t know why you would call somebody who looks like me, with a name like mine, ‘dangerous.’ It’s an interesting adjective to use. Nobody’s ever called somebody who looks like me ‘dangerous.’ ” I asked El-Sayed if, when people argue he’s unelectable, he felt they were talking about his ethnicity or his politics. He was clear that it’s the former. “When you call somebody dangerous, and then your whole ad campaign is about how you’re disrespectful to women, let’s ask what they’re playing to,” he said. “Part of me is just like, I’d respect you more if you just came right out and said it.” El-Sayed had just finished his two biggest rallies of his campaign and was about to start his third. He seemed boggled at how a candidate with thousands of people screaming for him could be deemed the less electable one. “I just think it’s really important to be asking, like, how do you win when you haven’t built a movement?” He said of Stevens. “You know, people are talking about electability as if it’s this thing on paper. Movements are electability.” But senators win without movements all the time. Just about all of them do, really. Elissa Slotkin won the same state, against the same Republican, in a worse environment, without a “movement” just two years ago. For every Democratic voter who might attend an El-Sayed mega-rally, there are many who vote regularly but don’t insist upon, or want, radical change. They don’t need electoral politics to thrill them. They just need to win. The warm-up band at Abdul El-Sayed’s Grand Rapids rally with Bernie Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez was electric. It was a Latin-infused seven-piece ensemble that could (and did) range from “Oye Como Va” to “Don’t Stop Believin’.” It was enough to get El-Sayed himself to emerge and sing a few bars of “Bohemian Rhapsody” hours before he was set to speak. Shortly after this, I was led backstage to his greenroom for my interview. I expected to see him in similarly jovial spirits. Instead, he was hunched over in a single foldout plastic chair in an otherwise empty room, looking spent. The primary has been exhausting, especially given the pressure being applied by national political factions. (Both candidates said they didn’t feel the pressure. I didn’t believe either of them.) But if the candidates feel exhausted now, just wait until the day after the primary. Not only will they have just three months to pivot to the general election and Mike Rogers, they’ll have to work just as hard, and just as quickly, to repair the wreckage from this primary. Just as it would be a triumph to win the primary, so too would it be an embarrassment to go on to lose a Michigan Senate seat, in a good Democratic year, for the first time since the Clinton administration. The credibility of whichever Democratic faction wins this proxy primary is on the line. If Haley Stevens wins, a lot of progressive Democrats will, rightly or wrongly, feel disgusted, as if big money and AIPAC stole the election from the candidate who’d run the better race. The El-Sayed campaign’s rhetoric against Stevens would not make a post-primary reconciliation any easier, either. I asked attendees at El-Sayed’s Detroit rally if they would vote for Stevens in a general election if it came to that. One, a 19-year-old student at Michigan State, told me he might, “but it’s just really hard because I feel like when I talk about Stevens, I’m talking about another Republican.” If El-Sayed wins, moderate Democrats in Michigan—who’ve never been in the position of having to hold their nose and vote for a progressive Senate nominee—will have to find some way to get comfortable with him. A lot of the reconciliation would be up to the candidates themselves. The last time I saw Stevens on the trail was at a Black church in Ann Arbor, where she spoke to a welcoming congregation about the “complex challenges before us that aren’t going to be met with simple solutions or simple slogans.” Addressing her target audience, she emphasized her role working with the Obama administration on the auto rescue and her support from the Congressional Black Caucus. She hung around the service for a bit after giving remarks, keeping her hand raised in praise—as best I could tell, she was the only congregant doing this—with her head bowed as the gospel choir got going. I asked her afterward if she would endorse and campaign for El-Sayed if she lost the primary. “Absolutely, I am on the team, I am so proud to be on the team,” she said. “You know, Mike Rogers wants to rubber-stamp Donald Trump. I’m going to do everything I can, that’s why I’ve been a part of this process right now. I’d be excited and honored to earn the nomination, but we’re going to finish this election and we’re going to win.” When Bernie Sanders lost the Democratic nomination in 2016, he got a lot of grief for taking his sweet time to endorse Hillary Clinton. At least part of Sanders’ thinking, though, was that his campaign had portrayed Clinton as a model of corrupt, corporate, and establishment nefariousness, and it would take his supporters some time to come back for air. So he negotiated with the Clinton campaign on the party platform to show his supporters that he at least got something. Still, on the first day of the Democratic National Convention in Philadelphia that year, when Sanders convened his delegates to break the news that we “have got to elect Hillary Clinton,” he was roundly booed. So I asked El-Sayed the same question later that night: Would he endorse and campaign for Stevens if she lost? “I’m committed to beating Mike Rogers, I’ve said that so many times,” he said. I noted that that wasn’t precisely a yes. “As I’ve said, I am going to do everything I need to do to defeat Mike Rogers.” We may never learn what he meant by sticking to such a precise response. The polling has largely shown El-Sayed ahead of Stevens. That’s in line, frankly, with the developments in the race. Top state Democrats like Sen. Gary Peters and Gov. Gretchen Whitmer, who had no interest in getting involved in this primary, have come off the sidelines in the final few weeks to endorse Stevens. The super PACs are not easing their spending. The Stevens camp, and the Democratic establishment generally, are unloading their arsenal. If Stevens loses, then El-Sayed will have done something unprecedented for progressives since Sanders offered the first draft a decade ago. He’ll have a chance to test-drive the progressive theory of electability in a prime swing election. We’ll finally get an answer. The only risk for Democrats is that the answer is no. Peptides are a rising favorite of wellness medical spas and longevity clinics, despite being largely unstudied and sold in the U.S. with research-use-only labels. Supposedly, the compounds are a way to sleep better, heal faster, fight aging, combat certain medical conditions, and in general boost health and vitality. In addition to the influencers who are often paid to promote them, peptides have a champion in Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who uses them personally. Last week, a Food and Drug Administration panel narrowly voted—with Kennedy’s support—to recommend the loosening of regulations around six peptides. This was another remarkable moment for Kennedy’s war against mainstream medical science, given that the FDA’s own staff scientists, in a report, had urged the panel against such a decision. But for Kennedy’s MAHA movement, the ability to explore peptides without having to circumvent our federal regulatory system is a win for individual medical freedom. It’s worth thinking through why peptides are accepted when so many mainstream medical treatments are rejected by Kennedy and his followers. Self-proclaimed MAHA moms advocate for the freedom to let their children’s bodies “naturally” fight illnesses that vaccines could have easily prevented. MAHA activists promote raw milk for drinking and beef tallow for cooking. They talk of the importance of exercise and unprocessed food to defeat chronic disease. And yet, they have been injecting themselves with pharmaceuticals they ordered from unregulated warehouses in China. Why? “Olympic-level mental gymnastics are required to square this circle,” said Timothy Caulfield, a professor of law and public health at the University of Alberta. But as Caulfield noted, the wellness movement has undergone a major cultural change in the past half-decade that has made it almost unrecognizable from the movement that birthed MAHA. And if you understand this turn, the experimental peptides start to make more sense. To understand peptide mania, he said, you need to understand that wellness has become profoundly obsessed with masculinity. Wellness as practiced and performed by Kennedy is certainly curated to project manliness. He  follows a carnivore diet, picks up snakes with his bare hands, and pushes videos of himself doing pushups shirtless and in jeans. His FDA is promoting testosterone therapy for men while cozying up to men’s health influencers such as Joe Rogan and Andrew Huberman. These days, wellness products that used to be branded in spiritual and soft, earthy, non-Western ways during the Goop era are now promoted by men in tight black T-shirts talking about optimization and hard science. Peptides, with their biochemical-sounding names, fit perfectly into this masculine version of wellness. But more significantly, the peptide trend literally emerged from a hypermasculine community in the first place: bodybuilder forums. There, users discussed different “stacks,” or combinations of peptides, to reduce body fat and achieve muscle growth. After that, peptides escaped the bodybuilder forums to be discovered by “biohackers”—the Silicon Valley tech workers who are fixated on “optimization” and believe people can hack their way to an ideal state of physical and cognitive performance. They next gained traction with longevity bros, the men who try to use their financial and technological resources to age as slowly and live as long as possible. And they have been promoted to the larger public by conservative male podcasters, Rogan and Huberman chief among them. If there was any question whether MAHA had lost its natural, body-purity-focused origins, Kennedy’s peptide campaign has answered it definitively: It has. Looksmaxxing is the internet community in which boys and men, convinced that good looks are the most valuable asset a person can have, share tips to try to become as physically attractive as possible. The most famous looksmaxxer, the 20-year-old influencer known as Clavicular, made a career out of selling destructive ideas about the human body to followers— advising young men to, for example, smash their faces with a hammer to get sharper bone structures. Clavicular has said he started taking testosterone supplements at age 14 and more recently admitted to using crystal meth to suppress his appetite. How an overlooked geothermal plant got a second chance

In June 2024, a small company called Zanskar purchased a geothermal power plant in New Mexico that was failing fast. The water coming from the underground reservoir was getting colder by the day, making the plant uneconomical to run. Now, two years later, that plant is running at full capacity again, thanks to a new well. With the help of advanced modeling and modern drilling technology, the company was able to identify a better potential well site, drill down thousands of feet, and revive the entire operation. As the world looks for more sources of emissions-free electricity that are available 24-7, Lightning Dock shows there’s still hidden potential deep beneath our feet. Conventional geothermal power plants rely on having the right underground conditions. Water flows through fractured hot rocks to harvest heat and then through a power plant to generate electricity. If the water isn’t hot enough or not enough flows, the power plant can’t work efficiently. Lightning Dock came online in 2013, and the site historically had two production wells that were used to feed the power plant. It’s common to see temperatures drop at a well site over time, typically at a rate of 1 to 2 °F per year. In the period before Zanskar took over the site, Lightning Dock saw temperatures drop by 50 °F over five years, a rate of 10 °F per year. When the company purchased the facility, the water going into the power plant was just 250 °F, while the plant was designed to operate at temperatures of at least 310 °F. When Zanskar mapped the underground conditions at Lightning Dock using advanced modeling techniques, they discovered that the wells were only hitting the very top of the reservoir there. Those production wells were quite shallow (at just 2,500 feet deep) and not in the best location, says Joel Edwards, Zanskar’s cofounder and CEO. The company’s modeling predicted that if the company were to drill another, deeper well in a new spot, the plant would be able to run efficiently. The team drilled a new well that reaches a depth of 8,000 feet and started operation in May 2025. After a full year, the well is still flowing at more than 4,000 gallons per minute. The plant has “completely turned around,” Edwards says. “It looks really exciting.” The data so far shows that the plant should be successful for years to come. “Ultimately you need to run these things for long time frames to get confidence in their performance over long time frames,” Edwards says. The progress at Lightning Dock could be good news for other geothermal sites too. The conventional wisdom in geothermal energy is that the deeper you go, the hotter it gets. But there’s usually a trade-off: With those depths come rocks that are packed tighter together. So drilling deeper could mean the hot water can’t flow as effectively, a problem for anyone trying to use it to generate electricity in a power plant. What the team found, however, was that the flow actually increased in the area where they drilled the new well. “That fundamentally changes how you think about not just Lightning Dock but all hydrothermal assets in America and what the potential can be for all of them,” says Ben Brenner, director of federal affairs at Zanskar. Over the past year of operation, Lightning Dock generated over twice the electricity it would have with the old wells. This is a relatively small power plant, with a capacity of 15 megawatts going to the local grid (about enough to power 11,000 US homes). Oil and gas developers have chased resources deeper underground over the past few decades. Operations started relatively close to the surface, but oil and gas production can stretch down 20,000 feet or more today. “I think that arc is going to play out in geothermal,” Edwards says. While typical geothermal well fields range from 3,000 to 5,000 feet deep, it could become more common to go deeper in the future, he adds. The company plans to do further development at the Lightning Dock site: with a few years of development and an upgrade to the power plant, it could get even more electricity out of this area, Edwards says. There’s been a lot of buzz and investment in enhanced geothermal systems—projects that aim to expand where geothermal resources can be used. Fervo Energy, for example, uses fracking techniques to open up rock that would otherwise be too closed off for traditional geothermal energy. But there’s still plenty of “low-hanging fruit” in the geothermal world, Edwards says. Many of these conventional resources have plenty of potential—they just need a second look. To see what they could do, the researchers removed most of their cybersecurity guardrails. Then they ran the models inside a sandbox that was cut off from the internet except for one link to a third-party piece of software that acted as a proxy to the outside world, so that the models could install code they needed to beat ExploitGym. On July 9, according to reporting by Reuters, OpenAI’s models started trying to break through the proxy. They found an unknown bug in the proxy’s software and used it to access the internet. From there, they broke into Hugging Face’s computer systems on July 11, apparently looking for data sets and solutions that would help them complete the tasks they were being tested on. Hugging Face announced the hack on July 16. OpenAI did not realize (or at least did not reveal) that its models were involved until July 21, around 10 days after they broke containment and a week after Hugging Face had shut down the attack and alerted the FBI. In a statement given to MIT Technology Review, OpenAI says: “We are conducting a thorough review along with external advisors and with oversight from our Safety and Security Committee. Once the review is complete, we will publish a technical report of our learnings for everyone.” The firm also confirmed that its researchers were properly using existing safety guidelines and procedures at the time. OpenAI has said the event was unprecedented—and in many ways it was. This was the first time outside of a simulation that LLMs escaped what was thought to be a secure sandbox, accessed the open internet, and attacked another organization. It’s a wake-up call that shows just how good the latest LLMs are at finding and exploiting vulnerabilities in real-world software with little or no human guidance.
