Software squeezes more data out of Webb Telescope for imaging exoplanets

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-05

Visit Original Website
With a huge and still-growing catalog of exoplanets, researchers are eager to take the next steps: trying to infer what conditions are like on them, either by imaging them directly or by capturing the light that has passed through their atmosphere. Both are substantial challenges, given the presence of stars that are far, far brighter than light that has come to us via the planet. While telescopes can be fitted with devices called coronagraphs that block out much of a star’s light, there are limits to their effectiveness, caused by noise from subtle variations in the telescope’s configuration and pointing between observations. Now, researchers have developed a new software package that optimizes a physical model of the telescope in a way that allows us to better model these differences, producing low-noise imaging of exoplanets. Central to this work is the concept of a point spread function. If you use a telescope to image a single point of light, it will not appear that way in the actual image. Instead, it will be slightly blurry and spread out, due to imperfections in the telescope hardware and the limits of our ability to keep it pointed accurately through the time it takes to image something. The mathematical description of how a telescope distorts a point of light is called its point spread function. There are two ways to figure out what the point spread function is. One is to use the telescope to image a known target and use those observations to derive a mathematical description of the point spread function. This is effective at reducing noise but has its limits, as the behavior of the telescope hardware can change over time, and the point spread function may vary with the wavelengths being imaged. The alternative is to try to model the hardware’s behavior and use that model to derive a point spread function. But modern telescopes are incredibly complicated instruments, with many, many factors feeding into their performance in ways that can be difficult to ascertain, even with the full engineering schematics. As a result, these models simply contain too many parameters that need to be tuned to make this route computationally tractable. But anyone paying attention to the world of AI developments will recognize that we’ve become quite successful at tuning billions of parameters in neural networks, in part through a mathematical technique called gradient descent. And, because this is central to training large neural networks, people have put a lot of work into optimizing the performance of gradient descent on GPUs. So a team of researchers has developed a process they’re calling FLIP (a non-acronym for Physics-based Learning for Imaging Planets) that uses a physical model of the hardware (in this case, the NIRCam on the James Webb Space Telescope) with 106 possible parameters, and then uses software to optimize all its parameters. The optimization starts with a simulated point spread function, then uses gradient descent to optimize its performance on data from an actual star. Run on a current GPU, this process only takes a bit more than half an hour. The researchers then fine-tune the results on the actual scientific image they are analyzing. This last part of the process is critical, since some variables will differ between observations. To test how this works, the researchers used observations of two stars with known exoplanets (HIP 65426 and 51 Eridani), along with a third (TYC 5899) that doesn’t have any known exoplanets but is known to vary a lot between observations. These are young stars, and two of them have massive planets that are still warm from their gravitational collapse, allowing them to have been imaged previously. Using Webb data, the team used FLIP to boost the signal-to-noise ratio by a factor of 1.5 to 2, depending on the observations. At a wavelength that can be used to detect carbon dioxide, they were able to spot one of the planets in the Webb data for the first time. For TYC 5899, the approach boosted the sensitivity of the observations by a factor of 15. The researchers point out that the applications go beyond getting a better view of known planets. By lowering the noise, it should also be possible to spot dimmer (and hence older or lower-mass) ones that had previously eluded detection. They also think they can modify FLIP to highlight where it’s making the most dramatic changes to the model, which can potentially tell us useful things about what’s going on with the telescope’s hardware. Kalshi court losses pile up as judges refuse to block state gambling laws

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-05

Visit Original Website
Kalshi is having a bad week in the federal court system. Two circuit appeals courts rejected the prediction market’s latest attempts to block state gambling laws in Connecticut, New York, and Nevada. Kalshi is separately facing a new lawsuit filed by the Arkansas attorney general. In all cases involving Kalshi and state governments around the US, the main issue is whether state gambling laws apply to prediction markets despite Kalshi’s claim that its platform offers “swaps” that can only be regulated by the US Commodity Futures Exchange (CFTC). The Trump administration is on Kalshi’s side, with Donald Trump Jr. serving as an advisor to Kalshi in exchange for a generous equity deal. “Kalshi insists that its products are not gambling,” but “Arkansas law is concerned with substance, not semantics,” Arkansas Attorney General Tim Griffin said yesterday when announcing his lawsuit against Kalshi. Kalshi’s prediction market lets customers “wager money on the outcome of just about anything” despite the fact that “gambling on an unregulated platform is squarely prohibited by Arkansas law,” he said. Griffin’s lawsuit was filed in an Arkansas state court. Kalshi violated state law by offering gambling to people as young as 18 and by failing to pay taxes or comply with other restrictions, Griffin said. Arkansas law permits gambling within certain limits, such as an age minimum of 21 years old for electronic wagering accounts. “Instead of obtaining the licenses, submitting to the oversight, complying with the restrictions, and paying the taxes required of lawful gambling operators in Arkansas, Kalshi opened its platforms to Arkansas consumers, including individuals as young as 18 years old, encouraged them to wager real money on sporting and entertainment events, and profited from those wagers,” Griffin said. Arkansas filed its lawsuit about a week after Utah Attorney General Derek Brown lodged criminal charges against Kalshi and its founders for violating Utah’s ban on gambling. A federal judge in Utah previously ruled that Utah can enforce its anti-gambling laws against Kalshi. Many other states are trying to enforce their gambling laws against Kalshi, and the Supreme Court may ultimately decide whether they are able to do so. We contacted Kalshi today and will update this article if it provides a response. Kalshi has won some rulings, but Griffin may feel emboldened by the company’s recent court losses. For example, the US Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit ruled in August that Nevada can stop the prediction market from allowing sports bets. A panel of three judges unanimously found that sports bets on Kalshi are not swaps and can be regulated under state gambling laws. Kalshi sought to overturn the judges’ panel ruling by seeking a rehearing in front of the entire Ninth Circuit appeals court, which has 29 judges. None of the judges supported a rehearing. “The full court has been advised of the petition for rehearing en banc and no judge has requested a vote on whether to rehear the matter en banc,” the court said in a ruling yesterday. “The petition for panel rehearing and the petition for rehearing en banc are denied.” Kalshi also lost on Tuesday in the Second Circuit, where it was trying to overturn earlier losses to New York and Connecticut. Kalshi sought preliminary injunctions to prevent New York and Connecticut from enforcing their gambling laws, but both requests were rejected by US district court judges. A plaintiff seeking a preliminary injunction must show that it is likely to succeed on the merits of its case, and judges decided that Kalshi had not done so. The Second Circuit appeals court considered Kalshi’s losses to New York and Connecticut in a single case and upheld the lower-court decisions. “Appellants have failed to make the required showing that an injunction pending appeal is warranted,” a three-judge panel ruled on Tuesday. The judges also refused to overturn Coinbase’s loss against Connecticut in a case that presented the same legal issues. Kalshi lost another significant case in September when an appeals court determined that Ohio and Tennessee can enforce their gambling laws against the prediction market. A three-judge panel in the US Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit ruled unanimously against Kalshi in that one. The Trump administration is simultaneously pursuing litigation against states trying to enforce gambling laws against prediction markets. The CFTC, claiming sole authority to regulate platforms like Kalshi and Polymarket, has sued Arizona, Connecticut, Illinois, Kentucky, Minnesota, New Mexico, New York, Rhode Island, Utah, and Wisconsin. Kalshi’s biggest win came in April in the Third Circuit, where the appeals court ruled that New Jersey cannot regulate sports bets on prediction markets. Judges there decided that wagers on Kalshi meet the federal definition of swaps, defined under US law as contracts in which payment “is dependent on the occurrence, nonoccurrence, or the extent of the occurrence of an event or contingency associated with a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence.” “Kalshi’s sports-related event contracts are swaps traded on a CFTC-licensed DCM [designated contract market], so the CFTC has exclusive jurisdiction,” Third Circuit judges wrote. But other courts that ruled against Kalshi found that at least some kinds of wagers on the prediction market are not swaps and that US law allows regulation of prediction markets by both state and federal governments. New Jersey asked the Supreme Court to reverse its loss in the Third Circuit, and 39 other states and the District of Columbia filed a brief supporting New Jersey’s petition.

As Democrats blew away their Republican rivals in an unprecedented midterm sweep Tuesday, the Make America Health Again movement also got bowled over, raising questions about the fate of the motley movement. MAHA—which has centered around anti-vaccine activist and current Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.—saw many of its favored candidates and allies lose, while vocal adversaries of Kennedy won. And the coalition was splintering under stress even before the voting started; in recent months, supporters have accused Kennedy and the Trump administration of inaction and betrayals in their two years in power. Now hobbled in Washington, shedding supporters, and showing little to no influence in voting booths, is MAHA on its deathbed? Or will it be reborn—possibly into a new group? Could it be assimilated into a blue platform—after sloughing off the more repulsive, conspiratorial aspects of Kennedy’s ideology? These are the questions circling in the aftermath of Tuesday’s bloodbath. In a social media poll Wednesday afternoon, Mary Talley Bowden—a dejected MAHA activist, anti-vaccine advocate, and doctor reprimanded for prescribing ivermectin to treat COVID-19—polled her followers about what should come next. She offered three options: Start a third party, join the Democrats, or overthrow the GOP. As of publication time, joining Democrats trailed at just 3 percent of the votes. The remaining votes were roughly split between overthrowing the GOP—whatever that means—and forming a third party, which had a small lead at 49 percent. That MAHA could fade out entirely or fracture into negligible shards seems like a very real possibility. Its collection of disparate groups and varied agenda items has always made its cohesiveness seem tenuous. A Politico poll in June revealed that people who identified as MAHA had different ideas of what the movement’s priorities are (or should be), as well as different political leanings, and sometimes opposing ideologies. Some within the movement embrace (or at least accept) Kennedy’s fringe beliefs, such as his zealous anti-vaccine views, his rejection of germ theory, and his false claim that 5G is damaging our brains and causing cancer. Some also believe, like Kennedy, in chemtrails and want all mRNA vaccines banned. There are members who support his plan to ban water fluoridation and also seem fine with his false claim that Lyme disease was created by the US military as a military weapon. Others, meanwhile, have been attracted to MAHA by the more reasonable, non-absurd issues Kennedy touts. Those include improving the quality of Americans’ food and diets, reducing their exposure to environmental contaminants, and limiting the influence of industry and corporate interests on Americans’ health, particularly limiting the sway of pharmaceutical and chemical companies. With various priorities, Kennedy and MAHA leaders have spent the past two years finding ways to disappoint their members. Some like Bowdon are angered that Kennedy has yet to ban mRNA vaccines. In February, Kennedy sparked particularly intense backlash among supporters for embracing Trump’s decision to increase domestic production of the pesticide glyphosate—a pesticide many in the MAHA movement wanted banned. Kennedy further fueled feelings of betrayal in September by appearing at a MAHA-branded summit that had hefty corporate sponsorships, including from behemoths such as Walmart, Anthropic, OpenAI, Grail, and UnitedHealth. “MAHA was supposed to expose the revolving door between money, industry and government, not build its own,” Robert Malone, a fringe scientist and previous Kennedy ally, wrote in a scathing response to the summit. Going into the midterms on Tuesday, the number of disenchanted MAHA members had mounted. In October, Tiffany Cianci, a former Kennedy supporter, published a MAHA scorecard, which lists 42 promises that Kennedy and leaders made to the MAHA members and then largely broke. The scorecard reports 37 promises broken, five partially fulfilled, and zero kept. One of the “partially kept” promises was removing synthetic dyes from foods, particularly children’s foods. Kennedy announced an effort to do that—with much ballyhoo—but later admitted that the FDA did not ban or even have an agreement with food companies to remove the dyes. Instead, Kennedy said he has an “understanding” with food companies. The dyes remain in foods. In a press release, Cianci wrote that the scorecard revealed “an abysmal string of broken commitments and betrayals.” It also contained a direct quote from her, saying: “I believed deeply in the promise that MAHA represented, particularly the possibility of confronting chronic disease and building a healthier future for America’s children. But belief was never supposed to mean blind loyalty.” The press release included quotes from several other prominent MAHA leaders, all expressing disappointment with Kennedy and the Trump administration. Later in October, Calley Means, a top ally and senior advisor to Kennedy, resigned from his position, reportedly “disillusioned” and frustrated by delays in food policy changes sought by MAHA. Another prominent figure to fall out with Kennedy was Vani Hari, an online influencer who goes by “food babe” and has previously identified as a “MAHA Mom.” As voting results were streaming in Tuesday night, Hari posted a comment on social media that she had given Politico months earlier, saying: “Women don’t come out to vote for glyphosate and war,” referring to the pesticide Kennedy had embraced and the Iran war. The document outlines plans to restore regulations that protect clean water and air damaged by the Trump administration, advance policies to keep toxic substances out of food, and further Food is Medicine policies, which provide produce prescriptions to American families to improve nutrition and health. MAHA leaders tied to Kennedy and the anti-vaccine movement, like Bowden and Honeycutt, are unlikely to get behind such a shift. While MAHA does have significant support among voters—41 percent of US adults say they support MAHA—that support is primarily tied to the health, environmental, and food safety issues, which are popular among non-MAHA supporters, too. Further, just 4 percent of MAHA supporters cited vaccines and medical choice—Kennedy’s key issues—as their reason for supporting the movement, the KFF poll revealed. Beyond MAHA’s primary issues, both voters overall and MAHA supporters specifically said MAHA issues weren’t top of mind this election; instead, both groups named the cost of healthcare as a bigger issue than those prioritized by MAHA (the MAHA movement is not associated with the issue of health care reform). A separate KFF poll on election day found that 50 percent of voters thought that the Democratic Party was better able to handle healthcare, while only 30 percent of voters said the same of the Republican Party. For now, it’s unclear exactly how—or if—MAHA will influence the political landscape going forward. It could break apart, live on through revamped Democratic policies, or try to form a new political party entirely. And, apparently, there’s also the possibility it will try to overthrow the GOP.

While the upcoming Grand Theft Auto VI won’t be available on PC this month, some gamers are making ridiculous preparations to use their PC graphics cards to apply Nvidia’s DLSS 5 technology to the console exclusive. On an explicitly unmoderated GTAVI subreddit, user RevolutionaryBus8208 lays out a proposed setup, which runs a PlayStation 5 through a 4K capture card to a PC. From there, the video signal is run through Veyra, an open source tool that can apply DLSS 5’s AI-powered “neural rendering” to video content. The result—which also uses DLSS frame generation to increase the 30 fps source to a 90 fps output—is then piped back to a 4K TV, theoretically adding some of the benefits of a beefy PC graphics card to a console-exclusive game. To demonstrate the potential of this setup, RevolutionaryBus8208 used Veyra to upscale the Grand Theft Auto VI Extended Look trailer posted in August. The resulting DLSS-ified version looks a bit better in places, with sharper background elements, subtler shadows, and more believable light scattering on some surfaces. But it also seems a bit washed out compared to the original video’s deeper blacks and arguably suffers from an unnaturally smooth “soap opera effect” in some scenes. While the upscaled version causes some occasional graphical artifacts and glitches from the frame generation process, it’s still an interesting proof of concept that shows just how far DLSS 5 has come from some of the early horror-show memes that circulated on the Internet earlier this year. Of course, running this kind of remote frame generation on a linear video is very different from applying it to a real-time interactive game. While a pure “passthrough” capture card wouldn’t necessarily add any latency itself, any software processing done to get the video feed ready for upscaling—and to do the upscaling itself—could add significant delays between player inputs and on-screen results. RevolutionaryBus8208 claims that Veyra itself adds a relatively small 11 ms of additional latency to the video source but admits he doesn’t have a capture card to test it with a signal coming from an actual PS5. Other commenters who have tried similar setups with other PS5 games said the input lag was “noticeable but tolerable” or claimed an impossible-to-believe latency addition of “around 1-2 milliseconds.” For players who can already use Sony’s “PlayStation Spectral Super Resolution” on a PS5 Pro, going through this convoluted process might cause a “hat on a hat” effect. But for stock PS5 owners who happen to have a beefy PC GPU, the hassles and trade-offs might be worth it to get some additional horsepower on console exclusives like GTA VI. Or, you know, you could just wait for the game’s actual PC release, which is reportedly slated for early 2027. Or for the likely AI-assisted unofficial decompilation port that should be along any day now.

Before Tuesday’s election, Republicans held more governorships and state legislative seats than Democrats, along with an advantage in state trifectas—unified control of the governor’s office and both chambers of the legislature. That advantage has disappeared, clearing the path for more climate and clean energy legislation, according to analysts and advocates. Democrats swept the vast majority of competitive races at the state level, flipping control of governors’ offices from Republicans in Georgia, Iowa, Ohio, Nevada, and Texas. Democrats have a 29-19 edge in governorships, with Kansas too close to call and Alaska likely to take a while to declare a winner. The party is on track for a net gain of hundreds of legislative seats, potentially giving it control of more nationwide than Republicans for the first time in more than a decade. Going into the election, Republicans had 4,072 seats and Democrats held 3,257, according to MultiState, a research and lobbying firm. Democrats had a net gain of more than 350 seats on Tuesday and are likely to add several hundred more as results continue to be tabulated, according to MultiState. Democrats won five new trifectas: Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin, with Arizona likely to be the sixth once officials finish counting. Those additions would give the party 22 trifectas, with Republicans holding unified control in 18 states and nine with split control. Kansas is yet to be determined. Republicans lost trifectas in Iowa, Georgia, Texas, New Hampshire, and Ohio. Before the election, Republicans had trifectas in 23 states, Democrats had 16, and 11 were under split control. The implications for climate and energy policy are likely to be profound, said Caroline Spears, executive director of Climate Cabinet, an advocacy group. “In the states, we had a generational political window shift to make people’s lives better, to lower costs, and to lower climate pollution at the same time,” she said in a conference call. This was the “affordability election” with concerns about inflation and high energy prices, she said. Newly elected lawmakers will enter office with energy policy proposals to address voter concerns, she said. She noted that renewable energy is less expensive than fossil fuels and can be developed quickly, promising rapid benefits for consumers. The growth of electric transportation reduces greenhouse gas emissions and shields consumers from the volatility of gasoline and diesel, she said. Jay Inslee, former Democratic governor of Washington and a prominent advocate for climate and clean energy policies, urged lawmakers to seize this opportunity. Inslee took a jab at President Donald Trump, calling him the election’s clear loser, even though he wasn’t on the ballot. “The chief climate denier got his head handed to him,” Inslee said. John Farrell, co-director of the Institute for Local Self-Reliance, a research and advocacy nonprofit, said in an interview that he is excited to see what comes next with the new trifectas. “There is this growing sense that more accountability measures are needed in order to rein in [utility] rates, and that utility companies are central to that problem,” he said. Farrell sees an opportunity for Wisconsin to catch up with neighboring states that have already established climate and clean energy agendas. He’s pleased that Democrats’ legislative majorities in Minnesota are large enough to give the party leeway to propose more ambitious policies. In Michigan, he is encouraged by residents calling for publicly owned utilities and pushing back against the influence of large utilities. In the past, Farrell might have worried that utilities would stave off consumer-friendly policies by showering newly elected Democrats with campaign donations. But he thinks scenarios like this are less likely now because the public is wary of candidates they view as puppets for large companies. And some candidates have shown it’s possible to win without accepting money from utilities. “We’re in a moment right now where there is enough scrutiny to make it harder for that sort of check-writing strategy to be successful,” he said. Motorola is patching a hole in its US lineup with a non-folding flagship phone, but the pricing puts this device dangerously close to the company’s expensive tablet-style foldable. The long-rumored Motorola Signature 27 is real, featuring top-of-the-line specs, a super-bright OLED screen, and a ton of cameras. But all this high-end hardware won’t come cheap in the age of AI. Motorola’s new flagship smartphone will run a whopping $1,600 when it arrives later this month. The Signature 27 is a new take on flagship smartphones from Motorola, leaving behind the middling legacy of the Edge series. However, you’ll still immediately clock this as a Motorola device due to its distinctive use of materials and Pantone colors. The phone is metal, ceramic, and glass, but the backs are textured polycarbonate. The device comes in Coal Smoke (black) or Capulet Olive (green) colors. The former has a “tactical weave” finish inspired by automotive design, and the latter is “textile-inspired.” This device will include the Snapdragon 8 Elite Extreme Gen 6, the most powerful mobile chip Qualcomm offers right now. While some smartphones have flirted with 16GB of memory, that’s less common these days. The Signature 27 will have 12GB like Samsung’s base model flagships. However, there’s not going to be an upgrade option for more. You will, however, get a generous 512GB of storage. The phone is fairly thin in most places, but it tapers outward near the camera module. It’s easy to see why Moto needed a little more space up there with four image sensors. Note: That’s not four cameras. There are three standard cameras: a 50MP primary, a 50MP ultrawide, and a 200MP 3x optical zoom (100x digital zoom). The results of the study are available on a new website by Visa that shows the outcome of 15 separate shopping expeditions by each of the 22 agents. Unsurprisingly, the agents powered by more cutting edge AI models fared best, with the Aragon 4 version of Google’s Gemini and the Wonder version of Meta’s Making notching the highest scores. Both agents made a successful purchase 82% of the time. A Claude agent came in third with a 76% success rate, while an OpenAI agent finished 6th with a 64% success rate. The agents’ struggle to make a successful purchase is rooted in part in a simple reality: The motivations behind any given purchase are often complicated and, in many cases, consumers lack information and are not certain what they want. Visa’s experiment sought to capture this reality by, in the case of TV shoppers, assigning a list of attributes they needed in a television—for instance, compatibility with a child’s gaming console, a 30-day return window, and a model with Dolby Vision. As is the case with real world consumers, the purchaser (who in this case was also an agent) would volunteer some information, but not all of it. The upshot is that, for an AI agent to succeed in Visa’s experiment, it had to interact with the shopper in a way that would elicit the missing information in a reasonable time. Success amounted to buying a TV that matched the shopper’s needs but, in many cases, the agents bought the wrong product, or simply failed to suggest something suitable at all. “We’re not there yet from a trust or technology perspective. But we’ll soon get into a world where consumers can trust and delegate,” Visa’s Global Head of Growth, Rubail Birwadker, told Fortune. Birwadker noted that agent-driven shopping is still in its early days, but that it is advancing rapidly. He added that the focus of Visa’s experiment—product discovery—is just one element of this new type of commerce that retailers and AI companies are trying to figure out. For AI agents to enter the shopping mainstream, companies will also have to sort out smooth and secure payments—a challenge that Visa and its rival Mastercard are already seeking to address by educating merchants and building tools to facilitate so-called agentic commerce. There are also new and unresolved legal issues when it comes to AI agents going shopping. Most notably, it’s still unclear who should bear the liability when agents make rogue purchases or commit fraud. According to Birwadker, these questions will get resolved over time in the same way that an earlier generation of consumers and merchants learned to use ecommerce—he added, though, that agent-based shopping is going to be a very different experience than how we shop online today. Decade-old vulnerability in OpenSSH is now fixed, so get a-patchin' On certain Linux systems, an attacker could gain root access by entering a password longer than 256 bytes. The vulnerability, dubbed *sshole by Wakefield researcher Özgün Kültekin, who discovered it, can only be exploited in very specific conditions. The flaw affects certain Linux systems using musl libc instead of the traditional GNU C library, and even then only those running OpenSSH's secure shell daemon without Pluggable Authentication Modules (PAM) and with password authentication enabled. If those conditions are met, and at least one other account has a valid password hash, accounts that shouldn't accept passwords, such as root, key-only deployment users, and LDAP users, may be accessed by supplying a password between 257 and 1,024 characters. The targeted accounts must have an asterisk (*) in their password field, and direct root login remains subject to OpenSSH's configuration. That might seem niche, but sorry: It’s not. Musl libc is common in embedded Linux systems due to its low footprint. More critically than that, it's also the standard C library for Alpine Linux, which provides official cloud images for both AWS and Azure. Some Alpine systems running in those environments may therefore be vulnerable, depending on their configuration. The vulnerability in OpenSSH on musl-based Linux systems dates back to 2016, but Kültekin explains that its roots go back much further – to the BSD Unix era, actually. In 1980, Sun Microsystems cofounder and BSD Unix heavyweight Bill Joy was involved in the development of BSD Unix, whose password file contained a single asterisk in the /etc/passwd entry for the daemon user. The asterisk wasn't a password hash and effectively disabled password authentication for that account. Nowadays, password hashes typically live in /etc/shadow and contain hashed information that the system uses to verify a plaintext password entered by a user. Asterisks are still present for accounts that aren't supposed to accept password logins, but as Kültekin explains in his writeup, that isn't exactly a foolproof way to keep people out. The crypt() function, which generates password hashes for comparison with stored values, can return NULL if it can't compute a hash. Applications that fail to check that result may crash. Musl libc's developers instead chose to return a single character for certain failures, and they picked the asterisk. Musl also added SHA-512-based password hashing around the same time, which Kültekin notes gets slower the longer a password is, potentially leading to denial of service if someone floods a server with a super-long password. Musl capped passwords at 256 bytes, and you can likely see where this is going. In 2016, OpenSSH introduced a workaround to prevent attackers from enumerating valid account names through SSH. Before the change, sshd could reject authentication attempts for nonexistent users more quickly than those for valid accounts. Attempts against valid accounts took longer because the server had to hash the supplied password before rejecting it. The solution was to have sshd perform dummy password hashing for invalid users, using an appropriate hashing algorithm and salt to reduce differences in response times. On affected musl-based Linux systems, accounts marked with an asterisk instead of a password hash could be busted open by supplying a password longer than 256 bytes – a flaw dating back to 2016. Any musl-based Linux system running a vulnerable OpenSSH configuration without PAM, with password authentication enabled, at least one account marked with an asterisk, and another account with a valid password hash could be vulnerable to a *sshole attack.
