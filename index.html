IBM has rolled out the newest models in its family of open-weight large language models designed to be downloaded and self-hosted. The newly launched Granite 4.2 comes in 3B, 8B, and 30B parameter variants. Like previous versions, IBM is taking a decoder-only approach here. These new releases offer a 128,000-token context window natively. The 8B and 30B variants (not the 3B one) also go through an agentic reinforcement-learning block; they were trained for expanded capabilities like using the terminal, searching the web, or using external tools. The 3B model supports tools too, but without the same level of specialized training. Beyond those tweaks, this release is particularly notable because, as IBM itself writes, “Granite 4.2 is the reasoning-focused release of the Granite language-model family.” When researchers or developers in the field say a model is capable of reasoning, they do not mean it in the same sense as we often assume when talking about human reasoning; the models are not consciously understanding the problem. Instead, they’re talking about functional reasoning, in particular via “chain-of-thought” and carrying intermediate results forward through multiple steps. For the user, this means more rigorous and accurate responses in some cases, but often slower response times and higher compute demands. IBM’s Granite family of models rarely grabs headlines for being the fastest or most aggressively innovative. Relative to even other competitors in the local enterprise space, like Nvidia’s Nemotron, the pitch seems to be predictable deployments—which is the priority you might expect from IBM these days. There has been an enormous amount of discourse about the cost and compute crunch around frontier cloud models from companies like Anthropic or OpenAI lately. Across many domains, both individual developers and enterprise organizations have been exploring local models as cheaper alternatives. That has also led to increased interest in model routers—AI tools whose main job is to interpret user prompts, tasks, or projects and route them to appropriately scoped models to balance performance, speed, and cost. Models like this are also popular with hobbyists, AI researchers, and individual developers because they can be tinkered with on local hardware without per-token API fees.

Xtracycle's Swoop ASM e-bike brings an automatic transmission to cargo bikes

The Velotric Discover M e-bike we reviewed earlier this year had a feature I found somewhat annoying: It would sporadically block much of the display with an alert telling me I should be in a different gear. While the implementation bugged me, the concept made a certain degree of sense; most e-bikes now know your cadence, how much torque you’re generating, and how that power is being translated into speed. That’s everything you need to know to determine an optimal gear. Somewhere in the back of my mind, I thought, “If you’re so smart, why don’t you just shift for me?” I was completely unaware that Shimano had already developed a system that did exactly that. And for the past few weeks, I’ve been using it on a $4,500 cargo bike from Xtracycle called the Swoop ASM that let me see what it’s like to hand control of my gears to an electronic brain. The short answer is that the electronics on the Swoop really are smart enough to figure out when it should be shifting. But the system is less adept at the actual shifting process, making for rides that are less smooth than they could be when it’s active. But you can shut that feature off, and the Swoop will shine as a fantastic cargo bike. Xtracycle got its start providing cargo trailers for mountain bikes and then branched into dedicated cargo bikes. The Swoop ASM is a long-tail cargo bike with what’s called a mullet setup: a larger tire up front and a smaller one in back. The small wheel is designed to help keep the weight held by the large rear rack a bit closer to the road. It also has a front suspension to help cushion the ride. Notably, the bike is built with steel tubes. Most manufacturers’ lineups use a mix of either carbon fiber and aluminum because those two materials can be made into the exact same shape, simplifying manufacturing. But other materials still have their fans, as they can offer distinct combinations of weight, stiffness, and vibration dissipation. I haven’t owned a steel tube bike since the 1990s, but the Swoop was a good reminder of why it still has its fans. It’s relatively lightweight (just under 70 lbs/31 kg) for its size, its ride is very comfortable, and it was surprisingly responsive when I stood in the pedals to accelerate away from stops. Despite its highly specialized geometry, the Swoop felt like a fundamentally good bicycle. There are limits to how well a single frame size can adapt to different body sizes, but Xtracycle does its best to widen the comfort window. It includes a double-telescoping seat post to maximize the seat-pedal distance flexibility. There’s also a quick release on the front handlebar clamp, making it easy to tweak the angle where the grips meet your hands. I found settings that were relatively comfortable but still a bit short of ideal for my 6-plus-foot (188 cm) frame—even with the seat post at its recommended maximum, I could touch the toes of both of my feet down while seated. Beyond that, plenty of nice touches make this a solid cargo bike. These include protective covers for the rear wheel and chain to keep clothing and limbs from accidentally getting entangled. A robust U-shaped kickstand keeps the bike stable even if you park it when fully loaded, and there’s a front cargo rack that can hold some baggage. The rear rack can be outfitted with a large range of accessories; mine came with the default setup meant for carrying passengers. This means footrests extending out at the level of the rear hub and two handgrip bars running parallel to the bench seating. But remove a soft cover, and the metal beneath reveals a series of slots that allow a wide variety of attachments to be fitted into place. You can also use one of Xtracycle’s bag attachments in this setup; I was provided one that ran the full length of the rear attachment and held a fair amount of cargo. Adding a second would have handled all of my typical shopping needs. (While having only one bag might seem awkward, the bike performed very well with everything I was carrying loaded on one side.) The only problem is that this hardware makes the rear wheel extremely difficult to reach. I struggled to lock the back tire to anything, and I considered myself fortunate to not have to figure out how to deal with a flat. All those basics make the Swoop a solid entry into the cargo bike market. What sets it apart is its use of a Shimano Cues Di2, one of the component maker’s low-end electronic shifting systems. Its mere existence surprised me, since I thought Shimano was reserving electronic shifting for higher-end component lines. But the company not only took it lower in the range; it added a couple of surprise features to its electronic shifting. First, the basics: the drivetrain uses a single chainring on the cranks and a 10-speed rear cassette. The rear derailleur requires only a power source and control signals—no cable required. Once properly aligned with the cassette gears, it executes a perfect shift every time it’s told to. This shift includes a brief period when the derailleur over-travels to ensure the chain engages the next ring along, then drops back into the optimal location for continued cycling. Everything in the system—the derailleur, the controllers, and the motor—takes power from a single removable battery. If the battery’s charge is good, you’re set to ride. The mid-motor offers three levels of assist, adjusted through a controller on the left side that also includes the power button and a simple display. Shifting is controlled by the one on the right, which lacks a display. Separating the two allows each to be fairly minimalist, making it easy to find the control you need by feel. On its own, the system worked beautifully. Shifting was fuss-free, and each change was blissfully silent. The assist worked well, and its three levels seemed to cover any needs short of going up steep hills with lots of cargo. This is not a bike that lets you skimp on pedaling in hot conditions; there’s no throttle, and even the highest setting of assist still requires you to exert some force on the pedals. But the motor definitely cuts down on the effort you have to make. The battery, while looking fairly compact, easily handled dozens of miles without needing a recharge, which means several days of typical errands. While the battery lacks a carrying handle, it’s easy to take it out to charge it, as it pivots sideways out of the frame. When in place, it acts as a protector that keeps anything on the left side of the bike from sliding through the frame and getting tangled in the chain. This is the first Shimano e-bike system I’ve tested, and it was just as good as you’d expect from a company that has come to dominate cycling drivetrains. Shimano Cues may be its lower-end system, but there’s no apparent skimping on the quality. And that’s before the bonus surprises Shimano has included. Somewhere in one of the controllers, Shimano’s software gathers all the relevant information about your ride: how fast you’re going, how much force your feet are exerting on the cranks, what gear you’re in, and how fast your legs are churning (and possibly more?). And it uses that data to do a couple of interesting things. One is what it calls Free Shift. Normally, a bike can’t shift to a different gear unless you’re turning the pedals to move the chain, which can potentially create problems. For example, you could move the derailleur down a gear but not complete the shift before stopping, meaning that the shift would be made when you start pedaling again, and the chain is under a lot of tension as you crank away from a full stop. Or you could move the derailleur several gears over without the chain following. But the whole point of having a motor is to move the chain. So if you shift without pedaling and the bike isn’t stationary, the Cues system will use the motor to move the chain just enough to complete the shift. It does so without speeding up the bike at all. Practically, this means that as you coast to a stop, you can simply hit the downshift button a few times, and the system will go into a low gear to make it easy to start up again without requiring you to turn the cranks. It’s … weird. Decades of cycling experience have programmed me to instinctually move my legs when I shift. Those instincts are suddenly superfluous. It gets even weirder if you enable the automatic transmission. Shimano has included two settings, one for general riding and one for hauling cargo or passengers. When the system recognizes that you can get more out of your pedaling in a different gear, it will simply move you into that gear. That includes doing all the downshifting as you’re coasting to a stop and rolling back up through the gears again as you accelerate away from it. Let me make that clear: You can coast to a stop without turning the cranks or touching the shifter, and you’ll still be in a different gear when you start up again. The system is remarkably good at matching when I feel a shift is needed—Shimano’s programmers have done a fine job. Because of that, it’s pretty easy to induce a shift by altering your pedaling, as well. You can tell when it’s close, and just a bit more force will cause the system to send you up a gear. It’s kind of pointless to do so, since the buttons that shift up and down still work when it’s in auto mode, but I found myself doing it regardless. The downside is that these shifts are not situations where instinctual actions are superfluous. To execute a smooth shift, you have to ease tension off the drivetrain by pedaling with less force, allowing the chain to slide over onto a neighboring gear with a bit less stress. Failing to do so can make for a clunky, inefficient shift and wear down components over time. My problem with the automatic transmission mode was that I could tell when a shift was likely to come, but I couldn’t predict its precise moment. So it typically came while I was pedaling away happily, with full tension on the chain, making for a loud and awkward shift. This was especially unpleasant when accelerating away from a stop, when the bike would make three or four clunky shifts in rapid succession, each emitting a noise that told me I’d just done something I’d spent years learning to avoid. Living in a neighborhood with lots of four-way stops meant I sometimes experienced that every single block. Shimano is not stupid, and these systems use parts that are specifically hardened to handle the added strain and wear. But it still felt bad every time I heard and felt that clunk. The Swoop is not an inexpensive bike; at $4,500, it sits somewhere between the Trek Fetch+ 2 and the Urban Arrow Family I’ve reviewed previously. While it lacks the latter’s capacious cargo tub, the longtail form factor provides a much more bicycle-like riding experience. I’d rate its ride as superior to the Trek because of its frame and front suspension made for a more pleasant ride. I also prefer Shimano’s drive system to the Bosch used by the other two bikes. It just felt better integrated with my pedaling, plus a bit quieter and less intrusive. The electronic shifting is a bonus—it’s not needed, but it definitely makes for a nice experience, and one where Shimano’s integration means you don’t have to worry about the status of the two additional batteries needed by many electronic systems. I don’t think I’d use the automatic shifting, but I could see where a less experienced cyclist might feel they benefit from it. So while it’s on the pricier side, the cost seems to be in line with the features it offers. If those features are important to you, the Swoop is definitely worth consideration. In the end, though, I’m not a huge fan of electronic shifting. I hate worrying about battery levels just to ride my bike, and I like the feel of a well-aligned mechanical shifter clicking into place. But even I have to admit that Shimano’s software does a fantastic job of shifting at the right times, and I think the system could really work well for anybody who hasn’t learned to associate a clunky-sounding shift with bad things.

The Falcon 9 rocket has been SpaceX’s workhorse, routinely launching batches of satellites for the company’s Starlink network, primarily from launch pads at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station and NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida. There were 165 Falcon 9 launches last year—about three flights per week—and about 75 percent of them carried Starlink satellites. This year is different. It wasn’t long ago when two launches per month was the norm, an indication of just how quickly SpaceX’s launch cadence has grown. Cape Canaveral and Kennedy Space Center sit on adjoining property and share the same range on Florida’s east coast, so it is worth considering them a single spaceport. Viral videos of the World Humanoid Robot Games show sprinting robots beating the human 100-meter record held by Usain Bolt—but also feature running robots crashing into barriers and falling down because they cannot readily stop running. A few fallen robots even break at the waist in a shower of sparks or catch on fire. That demonstration of both robotic prowess and limitations came from the second edition of the World Humanoid Robot Games hosted in Beijing from August 22–26. Like the inaugural event held in 2025, this year’s event challenged humanoid robots to run fast, perform kung fu or dance moves, fight one another in kickboxing, and play soccer or table tennis. This year’s games also tested robots in more humdrum practical scenarios like washing and hanging laundry, where they appeared to lag behind typical human working speeds. That suggests the event organizers, including Chinese state media and the Beijing city government, are keen to showcase the commercial potential of humanoid robots at a time when China’s robotics industry is rapidly developing and testing such robots. The fact that humanoid robots went from lagging behind human record-holders last year to surpassing human world records in the 100-meter sprint and standing high jump events this year suggests significant progress in robotic whole-body control for accomplishing specific tasks, Dipam Patel, a PhD candidate in computer science at Purdue University and a research assistant at the US Army DevCom Army Research Lab, told Ars. “All of that does display whole body control, because you need to move all the joints, hands, legs, arm, body, everything to accomplish that task,” said Patel, who is also a graduate student member of IEEE. “So there has definitely been a huge push in engineering from where they were last time to this time.” However, the robots on display in the World Humanoid Robot Games are still very far from general-purpose autonomous robots capable of handling practically any task. Instead, these robots were engineered to accomplish just one task, as shown by the fact that the running robots sprinted in a straight line and could not even slow down by continuing around the track after the finish line. “If you cross the finish line, that’s the goal—it doesn’t matter if you stop and it doesn’t matter if you fall into different pieces,” Patel told Ars. “Some companies had different types of robots for different games or different tasks, because they were engineered for that task to be the best in that.” The robotic races and other flashy events provide for an entertaining spectacle that can lead to easy news headlines and viral video clips. They also give robotics companies an opportunity to attract more publicity and investor money by potentially standing out from the competition, Patel said. One type of humanoid robot developed by the Beijing-based company X-Humanoid gained viral online fame for winning the small-group 400-meter race with an unusual “shy person hiding behind their hands” posture. Measures of robotic progress at the games could prove even more meaningful if, for example, they challenged robots to run the same race 10 times, Patel suggested. That would require the robots to demonstrate they have the software and hardware to perform reliably over multiple trials—and maybe even learn to slow down safely without endangering themselves or any bystanders. The less flashy events that debuted during the World Humanoid Robot Games this year may ultimately prove more useful benchmarks for robotic progress. Such scenario competitions included household chores, like washing laundry, folding clothes, and cleaning up living rooms, according to the Chinese state media publication the Global Times. Robots were also challenged by an unscheduled interruption when they had to bring a delivery package inside. That required them to switch tasks from the household chores to deal with receiving the delivery. The Beijing Continental Grand Hotel provided robots with a different set of timed challenges, including moving wheeled luggage to assigned hotel rooms, cleaning up beds and restocking supplies. One of the livelier practical scenarios involved an outdoor firefighting and rescue event. Robots had 30 minutes to identify hazardous substances, locate and shut off three open valves of different types, spot a fire, and then find a fire extinguisher to put it out. Only three out of 12 competing robot teams completed the entire challenge, according to the Global Times. Robots even participated in six events challenging hand dexterity, including picking up beans and driving screws. They also competed to precisely hammer nails into a corkboard without driving them in at an angle. But Patel observed that many of the robotic competitors were still being teleoperated by human operators who directly controlled the robots’ actions. The World Humanoid Robot Games allowed for human teleoperation of robots in certain events, but penalized the final score if robots were being remotely controlled rather than performing autonomously. The fact that many events still permitted humans to directly control robotic motions shows that autonomous robot systems still have a long way to go, Patel said. Whereas humans can quickly learn many tasks on the fly, robots require much more training through repeated trial-and-error attempts either in simulation or in real-world scenarios. “You need to have all possible edge cases for a robot to learn from interaction, which is not really feasible,” Patel told Ars. “You can simulate something like 100,000 scenarios for hammering a nail, but that’s just one task from millions of tasks in the world.” The AI models powering many robots can also learn from huge amounts of visual data involving human demonstrations. The real test for humanoid robots’ capabilities will necessarily take place in the real world beyond the demonstrations at the World Humanoid Robot Games. Investors have poured more than $6 billion into humanoid robot companies in 2025 alone, with Chinese companies being especially aggressive in testing and deploying such robots. Meanwhile, US companies like Boston Dynamics and Agility Robotics are also ramping up their own commercialization and deployment plans in factories and warehouses. The US government has even banned the import of foreign-made robots, including China’s most popular humanoid robots. But not all robotics researchers are convinced that humanoid robots are necessary, especially when the most useful robots currently doing productive work usually come in other shapes and sizes. For his own part, Patel is focused on developing software algorithms that allow many different types of robots to seamlessly switch between tasks.

Preserving glow-in-the-dark art and fashion for future generations

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-08-25

Visit Original Website
Fashionistas of a certain age no doubt remember the late designer Stephen Sprouse, who gave Blondie’s Debbie Harry her distinctive Bowery punk style. Sprouse’s first collections in the mid-1980s made a major splash, melding graffiti prints, a playful ’60s aesthetic, and bright Day-Glo colors that won admirers such as Andy Warhol. Several examples of Sprouse’s work are preserved in the textiles collection of the Indianapolis Museum of Art at Newfields, where conservationists are trying to learn more about the phosphorescent and fluorescent pigments the designer used in his creations in order to keep the colors from dulling over time. They described recent advances in that research at a meeting of the American Chemical Society (ACS) held this week in Chicago. Scientists began describing minerals that glowed in the dark as phosphors in the Middle Ages. Typically, such materials take on a charge when exposed to light, storing the absorbed energy and releasing it gradually as re-emitted light. Henri Becquerel discovered radioactive decay in 1896 because of his work with phosphorescent uranium salts: He left the salts in a closed drawer with photographic plates, which fogged up even without a light source. DayGlo paints and similar materials exploit the related phenomenon of fluorescence. DayGlo pigments fluoresce in daylight by essentially converting UV light into visible light to produce brighter, more vibrant colors. Sprouse is not the only one to find inspiration in DayGlo and similar phosphorescent and fluorescent materials. All glow-in-the-dark toys, stickers, paint, coatings in fluorescent lamps, cathode-ray tube TVs, and some clock dials rely on phosphorescence. (By contrast, glow sticks rely on a chemiluminescent process.) They’re also commonly used for traffic signage and on safety gear. DayGlo pigments were used extensively during World War II to make fluorescent fabric panels for US ground troops, while DayGlo’s black light paints were used on Navy aircraft carriers to enable planes to land at night. Artists have used both phosphorescent and fluorescence materials extensively from the mid-20th century on, most notably for psychedelic art and Sprouse’s designs. “For artists, the effect isn’t simply cosmetic, because they intentionally use these colors for their visual impact,” said Sarah Schmidtke Sobeck, a photochemist at The College of Wooster who spoke at the ACS meeting. Sobeck and Gregory Smith, a conservation scientist at the Indianapolis Museum of Art, have been collaborating on researching such materials for the last decade after meeting at a cultural heritage science conference. Their partnership has already produced a two-part study on the composition, spectral properties, and light stability of daylight fluorescence artists’ pigments. That earlier work showed that the optical brightener compounds that give fluorescent pigments their brightness degrade more quickly than the pigment dyes. Thus, even when there is no degradation in the pigments, the colors can appear to darken. That poses a unique challenge for conservationists responsible for long-term restoration of these works. Not only must they match colors under both visible and UV light, but the pigments will also change subtly over time. Gaining a better understanding of the unique photochemistry of such materials is critical for this purpose. During her talk, Sobeck reported on the group’s most recent work, extending their research to glow-in-the-dark phosphorescence pigments, with particular focus on the museum’s Sprouse collection. Among the early findings: Such materials have a markedly different composition from fluorescent colorants, which typically use organic dyes. The phosphorescent materials contain mineral-based pigments and inorganic compounds. Also, humidity turns out to be a significant factor in the breakdown of phosphorescent pigments—as much if not more so than prolonged exposure to light, which will help the museum improve its storage and exhibition practices for such artworks. The next step in this ongoing project will focus on the white pigment powder lithopone, testing how long phosphorescent materials containing that pigment will glow after each charge.

Some people are cursed with motion sickness, which can make it impossible to read or use a phone in a moving vehicle without suffering waves of nausea. There’s a surprisingly simple trick that can make smartphones usable in such situations. Apple deployed it on the iPhone a couple of years ago, and now Google’s own version is finally coming (slowly) to Android. Motion sickness is caused by a disagreement between what your inner ear senses and what your eyes see. If you’re looking at a book or a screen while in a car, the sensory mismatch can lead to a pretty unpleasant time. As part of iOS 18 in 2024, Apple released a feature called Motion Cues that places a network of moving dots on the screen to alleviate motion sickness, and it seems to work surprisingly well. Google’s version is similar, but it’s called Motion Assist. Google began testing Motion Assist back in 2024, but it never expanded beyond an internal test until now. Android Authority reports Motion Assist is appearing on Pixel phones running Android 17, but there’s no app or update you can install to get the feature—it’s being delivered by Play Services as part of a staged rollout. When activated, Motion Assist adds small dots around the edges of your phone screen over top of the content. It plugs into your phone’s accelerometer and gyroscope, allowing the dots to move in concert with the motion of the vehicle; they travel down the screen while the vehicle is moving forward, to the left when turning right, and so on. They basically mimic the way objects in the real world would move through your vision if you weren’t glued to the screen. Here’s how Apple’s version works. People who have used Apple’s anti-nausea dots have reported that the feature works wonders in reducing motion sickness. So there’s every reason to expect Google’s version will be similarly effective. Motion Assist can be activated manually as needed from the menu or using a quick settings tile. You can also let your phone turn it on automatically when it detects you’re in a moving vehicle. Google’s version of this feature also includes a few customization options, letting you change the shape, size, color, and opacity of the anti-nausea dots. If Motion Assist is available on your phone, you’ll find it in the Google services settings, usually nested one level below the main settings: Settings > Your profile > All services. Motion Assist will be under the Personal & Device section near the bottom. So far, Motion Assist has only appeared on Pixel phones running Android 17. The feature was obviously tested on earlier versions of Android, but it’s unclear if the final version will be exclusive to either Google’s phones or the latest version of Android. Well-wishers have raised more than $300,000 for his family, and made his upcoming memoir a best seller in Britain. At a rally in central London held in his memory, attendees carried placards reading Rest in Power Professor Jason Arday, even though he had resigned the title days before his death. They wanted to make clear their belief that he was unfairly deprived of his academic status. Insisting that the academic was completely innocent, in the face of all the evidence, sends a strong social signal that you hate the right-wing media and care about racism. The central question in this dispute is whether the treatment of Arday—the subject of more than 200 articles in print this year, according to CNN, and hundreds more online—was proportionate. The smaller his alleged offenses, the more easily one can argue that the media hounded him to death. And so Arday has been recast as a small-time plagiarist; academics have lined up to argue that, You know, even that isn’t a very big deal. The “meaning of plagiarism does not cover mere overlap of sentences,” the academic Jason Stanley asserted in a blog post that also bizarrely noted that he hadn’t paid much attention to the story because he had been on vacation in Europe. Stanley’s blithe dismissal will be news to students who are told the exact opposite early in their courses. Or to Ross Barkan, who was dropped from New York magazine after getting caught recycling other writers’ words. When concerns were raised about his dissertation, Cambridge University declined to investigate, saying it was a problem for Liverpool John Moores, which had already stated that it stood by his work. Just before Arday’s death, however, Cambridge reversed its position, pledging to investigate, as did other institutions that had employed Arday. Imagine that it’s October 2026, and one of Germany’s 16 federal states has come under the control of a far-right government for the first time since the Nazi era. With an absolute majority in the state Parliament and one of its own installed as state premier, the Alternative for Germany, or AfD, quickly begins implementing its agenda. The interior minister announces an emergency remigration program: abolishing asylum, revoking the residence permit of any foreigner convicted of a crime, pledging to review all naturalization decisions made during this century, and incarcerating those designated for deportation. The government stops classifying Ukrainians as war refugees and ends their social benefits. European Union flags disappear from public buildings. The mainstream press is shunned in favor of influencers aligned with the party’s ideology. The government authorizes vigilante groups to act as an auxiliary police force in regions with high crime rates. This hasn’t happened, but it’s not merely hypothetical either. The AfD is projected to win an election next month in Saxony-Anhalt, a central German state of some 2 million people. An anti-racism NGO in Berlin, the Amadeu Antonio Foundation, created this scenario based on the far-right party’s own election manifesto and the statements of its representatives. For decades, right-wing politics have carried particular weight in Germany, where atonement for the Holocaust includes mandatory education about the rise of National Socialism. The far right was long relegated to the fringes, its most ardent members easily identified as skinheads decked out in leather. But the AfD, founded in 2013 as a mild euroskeptic party led mostly by conservative economists opposed to eurozone bailouts, is now Germany’s most important right-wing force since the Nazis. At the national level, the AfD is the most popular party, supported by more than a quarter of voters in recent polls. It leads both of the traditional broad-based parties, the center-right Christian Democrats and the center-left Social Democrats. Its base of support is in the formerly Communist east—generally poorer, older, and angrier than the rest of Germany. And that’s where it’s poised for a breakthrough later this summer. In past state elections, other parties have contorted themselves to keep the far-right faction out of governing coalitions, maintaining a so-called firewall even when the AfD has won the most votes. That may not be possible in Saxony-Anhalt, where ahead of a September 6 vote, the AfD is polling at more than 40 percent. If smaller parties fail to reach the required 5 percent threshold to enter Parliament, this kind of showing by the AfD could be enough for it to win an outright majority, meaning that it wouldn’t need cooperation from other parties to govern. Suddenly, Saxony-Anhalt would become a testing ground for AfD policies. If the AfD doesn’t reach that threshold, the spotlight would turn to the Christian Democratic Union, the party of German Chancellor Friedrich Merz. The Christian Democrats would face a dilemma: whether to breach the firewall and work with the AfD or join hands with the far-left party Die Linke in a last-ditch effort to keep the far right from taking power. In fact, the Christian Democrats have a policy, formalized in 2018 and known as an incompatibility resolution, against working with either party. Andreas Rödder, a historian who previously headed the center-right party’s fundamental-values commission, told me that the decision didn’t generate much controversy within the party at the time because the matter was mostly symbolic; the center-right party, then dominated by Chancellor Angela Merkel, had other choices for governing partners. At that time, German politics remained fairly stable, relatively speaking, as anti-establishment forces shook up other Western democracies. For many in Germany, this was a point of pride, a sign that the country had learned from its history, that it knew where political fanaticism led. Less than a decade later, Germany no longer appears immune to extremism, and the stable politics that once attested to its moral redemption are “on the brink of being fundamentally shattered,” Rödder said. The country’s domestic intelligence agency has classified Saxony-Anhalt’s branch of the AfD as a right-wing extremist group. Some voters support the party because of that hard-right agenda. Others are drawn to it for contrarian reasons: to register their dissent against elite gatekeepers, whether in the security service, the judiciary, or the media. In this way, their vote is a version of the declaration once made by the Russian literary critic Vissarion Belinsky: “Negation is my god.” “Every organ of elite opinion tells people they can’t vote for the AfD,” a German diplomat told me. “And many people respond by saying, ‘Hell yes, we can.’” Why does an election in a relatively small German state matter? Because what happens in Germany matters. That’s been the nature of the German problem for almost two centuries—what to do with this country, larger and more prosperous than its neighbors, that has caused so much torment for Europe and for the world. The problem arose with the Franco-Prussian War and the unification of Germany in 1871. It returned in monstrous form during both world wars. And it recurred with German reunification in 1990. The German question is back as the country breaks with postwar taboos. Germany is rearming, spending billions to turn its army into the strongest in Europe. Its steadfast support for Israel has been shaken by the extremism of Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s government. And in Saxony-Anhalt as well as other German regions, even if not yet nationally, the far right appears on the cusp of claiming power. From the January issue: The new German war machine Germany’s situation isn’t unique; what’s striking in a country where historians have debated whether a Sonderweg, or “special path,” produced destructive nationalism is how much it mirrors what’s happening elsewhere in Europe. Marine Le Pen, the far-right French firebrand, is leading in polls ahead of a presidential election next year. Nigel Farage’s Reform UK remains in a strong position in Britain. Giorgia Meloni already leads a far-right government in Italy, representing a party that traces its roots to the country’s postwar neofascist movement. Saxony-Anhalt is following these same trends. But the circumstances command greater attention. As Henry Morgenthau Jr., President Franklin D. Roosevelt’s treasury secretary, titled his postwar book on the defeated Axis power, Germany Is Our Problem. Saxony-Anhalt is sometimes called the heartland of German history. It’s the birthplace of the Protestant Reformation, where Martin Luther nailed his 95 theses to the church door in Wittenberg. Cities such as the capital, Magdeburg, were important centers of the Holy Roman Empire. In the 20th century, modernism flourished in the region, which has Germany’s highest concentration of Bauhaus architecture. It’s long been a hub of the chemical and coal-mining industries. During the Cold War, the territory fell on the eastern side of the Iron Curtain, and the state still faces structural problems left over from post-reunification deindustrialization and out-migration, including higher rates of poverty and unemployment than the national average. The psychic effect also lingers. Until 1990, there was no genuine democracy; freedom and civil rights were curtailed. “In some quarters,” Detlef Gürth, a member of the ruling Christian Democrats in Saxony-Anhalt’s state Parliament, told me, there is still a “pronounced desire for a ‘strongman,’ a messiah who dictates the course of action and takes care of everything.” Two recent events crystallize the state’s misfortune and the feeling among many residents that things aren’t working. In December 2024, a Saudi-born doctor drove a rented SUV through a crowded Christmas market in Magdeburg, killing six people and injuring hundreds more. Authorities said the perpetrator was an anti-Islam activist sympathetic to far-right groups, but terrorist violence of any kind, the facts notwithstanding, played into the far right’s hands. The following year, the California-based Intel Corporation canceled plans for a €30 billion chip factory in Magdeburg, which would have been the biggest-ever foreign direct investment in Germany. The episodes were unrelated; the company said it had simply grown too fast, although a government minister also found an explanation in “Donald Trump’s ‘America First’ philosophy.” Together, however, they contributed to a gathering feeling of precarity and bad luck. The AfD promises to shake up the system. Its 35-year-old front man, Ulrich Siegmund, is a perpetually smiling social-media sensation. He is a salesman by trade, the founder of a fragrance company called Berlin-Duftet, whose name means literally “Berlin smells good.” Der Spiegel, the prestigious German news magazine, recently branded him “the most dangerous man in Germany,” which turned out to be great publicity; at campaign events, he autographed copies of the magazine cover. As Siegmund racks up likes on social media, he leaves the most incendiary topics to others in the party. When some members of the state AfD branch traveled to the United States last year to attend a gala of the Trump-aligned New York Young Republican Club, Siegmund stayed home. The party’s ideological engine in Saxony-Anhalt is said to be Hans-Thomas Tillschneider, a member of the state Parliament who has likened Islam to a fungus, condemned the “sacralization of the Holocaust,” and suggested that Jewish institutions are using Muslim immigration to achieve the “abolition of our people.” He has called AIDS patients the “price we pay for a decadent social model” and argued that the Bauhaus movement “raped the human need for comfort.” On foreign policy, Tillschneider sides with Russia in its assault on Ukraine, praising President Vladimir Putin as “a real man with a sound set of values” and insisting that Moscow is “defending itself.” When I asked Tillschneider to explain his party’s success in the region, he said it didn’t stem from one particular issue or another but amounted to a rejection of the mainstream’s whole approach to policy making. He argued that the “established parties have formed a consensus against the interests of the citizens” and refuse to entertain ideas that go against their orthodoxy. The AfD is testing the limits of what’s acceptable in German political culture, especially on immigration. The party’s election manifesto declares ominously, “Our streets and squares are no longer safe.” And it promises a “deportation and remigration offensive.” The term remigration, once a fringe concept linked to ethnic cleansing, is now a focus of far-right immigration discourse, and has flexible meanings. Tillschneider defined it as “the maximum possible deportation within the framework of the constitutional order.” In addition to a “180-degree turn” in migration policy, the party is also vowing to revamp education and art, instill pride in German identity, and end the country’s “guilt complex.” The AfD also objects to the established parties’ anti-Russia policies and seeks to expand Russian-language instruction in schools and revive student exchanges with Russia. The Christian Democrats, who currently govern Saxony-Anhalt in a coalition with the Social Democrats and the liberal Free Democrats, have struggled to respond. The state premier, Sven Schulze, took umbrage at the far right for “constantly belittling Saxony-Anhalt and acting as if everything here is doomed to failure.” AfD representatives can sense the desperation. Tillschneider said the firewall had become a “tomb wall” that the Christian Democratic Union has built for itself. He envisioned only two possibilities after the September election: either an AfD government or an “all-party government against us.” On this there seems to be some agreement. Gürth, the Christian Democratic lawmaker, said his party can’t justify cooperation with the AfD, which “operates like the Nazi Party” in its methods: “It exaggerates real or perceived problems, taps into public discontent, and paints a dystopian picture of Germany teetering on the brink of collapse. Dolly Parton’s death at age 80 comes at a time when her influence seems at its peak. Country music is surging, female singer-songwriters are on top, and audiences seem hungry for honest idiosyncracy—for figures who, like Dolly, transmit their inner truth to mass audiences in a voice that’s clear yet scraggly. But Parton’s greatest legacy is existential in nature. Piling on wigs and feathers, she showed that you can be yourself while playing a character. Parton’s self-construction project began in her childhood. She was born the fourth of 12 kids in a tiny cabin in Pittman Center, Tennessee. Her parents were so poor that they paid the doctor who delivered her with a sack of cornmeal. As a girl, she sang songs on her porch into a fake mic fashioned from a tin can and a tobacco stick, envisioning herself at the Grand Ole Opry. Her talent drew attention from local radio and TV personalities, and by age 13, she was singing on the real Opry stage. The craftiness and self-consciousness that came from growing up impoverished shaped her outlook for life. As she’d sing in her 1971 song “Coat of Many Colors”—her signature (and favorite) hit—her mother used to sew rags into garments that the young Parton would flaunt at school. Mockery from other kids taught her the lesson in self-determination that she’d turn into one of her defining lyrics: “One is only poor only if they choose to be.” As a young woman, she modeled her fashion off a “town tramp”—as she later put it—whom she’d grown up around. Her preacher grandfather whipped her for dressing that way, but to her, women with heels and push-up bras weren’t trashy; they were glamorous and confident. A fascination with stereotypes—with fulfilling them and subverting them—gave her an edge when she moved to Nashville. In 1966, her first solo hit, “Dumb Blonde” (written by Curly Putman), saw her singing that despite her looks, she “ain’t nobody’s fool.” On the TV variety series The Porter Wagoner Show, she played the aw-shucks second fiddle to the titular star even as she wrote songs that outcompeted Wagoner’s music. Her 1970 hit “Joshua” spun a tale in which the narrator approaches a mean-looking backwoods hermit, someone whom the rest of the town fears, only to realize that he has a heart of gold. It was an example of how her penchant for social criticism and her interest in flipping expectations made her songwriting stand out. She left Wagoner behind in 1974 to court an audience that transcended country music. Her songs sparkled with fun production touches, clever lyrical twists, and melodies that seemed at once as winding as a lazy river and as smartly constructed as good Levi’s. For 1980’s “9 to 5,” the sound of her fingernail clicks did as much work as the lyrics—and the movie the song accompanied—in celebrating the hustle of women at work. “Jolene,” her enduring ballad of jealousy, built itself around a chorus so plaintive that it seemed surely scraped from a harrowing chapter of Parton’s own life—but she was quick, in interviews, to say that the song’s story was more or less fiction. As she settled into icon status, she seemed to revel in the franchisability of her image: Dolly Parton was an idea that everyone could benefit from. She founded a theme park in her home state, selling a blend of rustic realness and fun kitsch. She only ever seemed tickled that Whitney Houston’s cover of “I Will Always Love You” eclipsed Parton’s original in eminence. She made childhood literacy the central focus of her philanthropic efforts—another subversion of the “dumb blonde” image (and one that channeled her upbringing by a father who couldn’t read). In recent years, Parton’s persona has come to take on political significance. She was, as my colleague David Graham has written, the rare public figure who seemed beloved by both red and blue America. She maintained that status through careful circumspection regarding partisan politics. But when it came to questions about sexuality and identity, she took a stand by repeatedly supporting gay, trans, and gender-nonconforming people. After all, she once told RuPaul, she herself was a drag queen. Many of the most important stories in music this decade owe a lot to Parton. Sabrina Carpenter has brought Dolly-style humor (and hair) back to the charts; Beyoncé enlisted Parton to play emcee on her 2024 album, Cowboy Carter; Ella Langley is blending backwoods scenery with wry, shrugging wisdom that recalls Parton’s catalog. But Parton set an example bigger than music—by showing how to take the scraps you were given and stitch them into something bright and new.

ICE quietly enacted new rules this month that will keep officers involved in deadly-force incidents—such as firing a gun and using a choke hold—off the streets until the completion of an investigation. Previously, these officers were able to return to their regular job in as few as three days. Now they must remain on desk duty until they are cleared by investigators, according to two senior ICE officials with knowledge of the change, who spoke with me on the condition of anonymity because they are not authorized to discuss agency matters with reporters. Under the old rules, ICE supervisors could lengthen an officer’s period of leave, but such an extension was often viewed internally as signaling a lack of confidence in the officer and their version of events. At ICE and other law-enforcement agencies, officers are typically eager to return to full patrol duties as quickly as possible, and not remain in limbo as an investigation plays out. The change, which has not been publicly announced, means that officers who fire their guns, choke someone, or use some other form of deadly force—even if no one is killed—will potentially be limited to performing administrative duties for much longer periods. Acting ICE Director David Venturella and other senior ICE leaders ordered the change late last month, the two senior officials said. ICE and the Department of Homeland Security did not respond to my questions about why the policy change was made or how many such incidents have occurred since President Trump returned to office. ICE officers have shot at least 16 people since last January, four of them fatally, according to public records. Last month, there were two fatal shootings during traffic stops: On July 7, ICE officers in Texas killed Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, a 52-year-old contractor from Mexico, and six days later, an officer in Maine fatally shot Johan Sebastián Durán Guerrero, a 25-year-old Colombian immigrant. DHS said the officers fired in self-defense, but the incidents are under federal investigation. Veteran ICE officials have worried for months about the competence of the thousands of new recruits who were added during a hiring binge last fall, many of whom were rushed onto the streets after taking a fast-track training course. The ICE officer in Maine, David Brouillette, was a new hire, and his relatives told reporters he had a history of mental-health issues and violent behavior that should have disqualified him from getting a badge and gun. Violent confrontations during traffic stops particularly concern ICE leaders; the agency has not typically had so many officers pulling over vehicles and attempting to arrest people who are in cars. After the shootings last month, ICE and DHS pledged to expedite the deployment of body-worn cameras for ICE officers. Although most of the cameras have been delivered, the officials told me that they don’t know when all of ICE’s roughly 15,000 deportation officers will be wearing them. After the killing in Maine, Venturella and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin halted the use of nonemergency traffic stops—but Trump reversed the directive when immigration hard-liners said it made the White House look weak. The president has said that ICE officers “make mistakes sometimes” and can be “too rough,” but they’re doing a “GREAT job.” Arrests by ICE totaled nearly 50,000 in July, the highest level in a single month since Trump returned to office, the officials told me. The agency is no longer using a city-by-city approach to surge arrests, as it did last year under then–Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, and this has led to fewer clashes with protesters. But officers remain under pressure to maximize arrests and deportations, and new videos continue to emerge showing officers tackling immigrants and pointing guns at people who are filming them or are allegedly disobeying their orders. ICE leaders say they’re looking for ways to reduce the use of deadly force by giving officers more nonlethal tools. The agency is planning to spend up to $20 million on electrified gloves that allow officers to deliver painful shocks. The gloves, which have been used by some police departments and public-school security officers, will be assigned to ICE teams involved in traffic stops and other potential confrontations, according to the officials I spoke with. They told me the shocks delivered through the gloves are painful but less intense and more localized than those from a Taser, and will be easier to direct than chemical sprays. Prior to the current Trump administration, the use of deadly force by ICE deportation officers in U.S. cities was more rare than shootings by U.S. agents along the Mexico border were, Scott Shuchart, an attorney and a former ICE official who worked at the agency’s Office for Civil Rights and Civil Liberties from 2010 to 2018, told me. The U.S. Border Patrol reduced its annual number of shooting incidents more than a decade ago by hiring independent consultants who recommended giving agents more nonlethal tools and better training. The number of Border Patrol’s firearm-use incidents, measured across fiscal years, fell from 55 in 2012 to eight last year. Shuchart said the administrative-leave extension “seemed like a healthy signal” that ICE leaders knew “they might have a real problem” after the shooting in Maine. “They don’t want to be creating more risk by putting people back out on the street,” he said. Deborah Fleischaker, a senior ICE official during part of President Biden’s term, told me that the change to ICE’s rules will matter little if internal oversight mechanisms don’t hold officers accountable for wrongdoing. Trump officials gutted offices at DHS that had reviewed complaints against ICE personnel and about poor detention-center conditions. “If the goal is to ensure that people are on administrative leave through the entirety of an investigation, that’s a good goal, but it doesn’t speak to the quality of the investigation,” Fleischaker said. Fleischaker, now an immigration-policy adviser at the Hispanic-advocacy group UnidosUS, said Trump officials have conveyed a clear message to the federal workforce that “mass deportation is a high-profile goal of this administration and people need to be real careful of getting in the way of anything that slows that down.” In October, Stephen Miller said on Fox News that ICE officers had “federal immunity” to perform their duties while carrying out Trump’s immigration crackdown. But since then, Democratic leaders in several states have vowed to bring charges against ICE officers for use of excessive force. In Minnesota, state prosecutors charged the ICE officer Christian Castro with four counts of second-degree assault and one count of falsely reporting a crime for his role in the nonfatal shooting of a Venezuelan immigrant on January 14—a day after DHS published a clip of Miller’s “immunity” remarks. Minnesota has been seeking Castro’s arrest and extradition from Texas, but Governor Greg Abbott, a Republican, is challenging the request in federal court. There were hissing accusations of lies, corruption, and megalomania; comparisons to sadistic Nazi scientists; and statements, as if of undisputed fact, that Fauci killed millions of people. The nation’s most powerful Republicans, without any significant pushback, joined forces to shout down and criminally pursue the face of the COVID pandemic’s health authority based on wild, speculative, and facile conspiracy theories. It all prompted a kind of deeply existential question: If this is how we now treat a onetime pandemic hero, what will happen the next time we face a similar public health crisis? Public health officials say with the rise of conspiracy thinking at the highest echelons of our national government, the next pandemic response could be so much worse. But they did have one potentially innovative and surprising, if disconcerting, solution to our national public health information crisis: A.I. has the potential to help stave off a conspiratorial catastrophe during the next pandemic. Given everything that’s happened with our public health leadership since Donald Trump returned to power, most public health professionals Slate spoke with took a dim view of our possible responses to a future crisis. After a summer of foodborne illness crises, it’s clear our public health officials are struggling to handle basic public health. Historically, pandemics have happened multiple times a century, and many scholars believe that they’ll become more frequent due to climate change and deforestation. The situation appears bad enough that when Slate asked experts how to conspiracy-proof a pandemic, responses often offered such bleakly unhelpful tidbits as “We need to study the effectiveness of our communication” and “This is going to take careful thought about strategic communications.” As Tara Sell, a senior scholar at the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security, told Slate, “We’re in a bad spot right now.” But several experts, prodded for any note of optimism they could find, offered hope from artificial intelligence. Admittedly, looking to A.I. for rescue is usually an eye-roll-worthy impulse. And coming from the field of disinformation in particular, A.I. is a Pandora’s box of yet-to-be-imagined evils. Generative A.I. certainly has the potential to worsen pandemic conspiracy theories by equipping them with highly convincing fabricated evidence. This applies only to the A.I.-generated search results summary and not to chatbots, which are inclined to give users the answers they want. The hope is that A.I. will help by encouraging laziness—the less time we spend pursuing answers, the better. Of course, Mackey said, there’s no guarantee that the A.I. won’t go off the rails. If A.I. companies got into certain commercial models, they could start promoting sponsored results, such as pseudoscientific cures. Or they could bend to pressure from conservative activists or tech oligarchs who see the weeding out of conspiracy theories as the suppression of political speech. So the possibility that A.I. might help is admittedly a flimsy source of comfort. But it’s something. There’s a second theory that might point to A.I. as our savior, though. Neil Johnson, the head of the Dynamic Online Networks Lab at George Washington University, sees the problem of conspiracy theories in health emergencies as the natural result of the huge scale of online communities. Before COVID even had a name, he said, online parenting communities on Facebook were reaching out to other communities to get information about the new flu-like virus in China. They found ready information in anti-vax groups, which claimed to be able to answer questions that the public health experts could not; conspiracy theories will always move faster and more decisively than legitimate scientific inquiry. And because of the points of connection between different Facebook groups with shared interests, the anti-vax ideas spread like wildfire, reaching millions of users and, by extension, their loved ones. The scale of the problem is so vast, Johnson said, that it requires an equally ambitious strategy. And his idea is this: Have tech companies use A.I. to spur interactions among conspiracy theorists, pro-science activists, and the middle-ground groups, such as the parenting communities. Studies show that when you put all three together, he noted, the extremes tend to soften. “And over time, it will soften the entire universe,” he said. Many people do not have primary care physicians, and often those who do cannot easily call up their doctors to ask questions. We don’t know who to go to for trusted information. These are systemic problems without easy solutions. Pandemics worsen the suspicion. They are fast-paced, overwhelming, and chaotic, and public health officials inevitably make mistakes. Responses involve multiple agencies and levels of government, and because of our polarized environment, any information coming from a presidential administration will be met with automatic suspicion by the president’s opposing party. That people will acknowledge the pain and sacrifices that come with public health measures. That community leaders and representatives of the business and faith communities will feel included in the decisions. That we will not try to drive a one-size-fits-all response across the states. But none of those improvements will stop conspiracy theories. On Monday, the Supreme Court allowed the Trump administration to begin implementing Donald Trump’s mail-ballot executive order, which seeks to nationalize an election procedure that the Constitution has assigned to the states by ordering the United States Postal Service to refuse to mail certain ballots. Even though nearly all midterm primaries have already happened and the general election is only months away, the court bought the government’s argument that it’s simply too early to consider a lawsuit about the order’s legality. As Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson pointed out in her dissent, this decision is another piece in this court’s deteriorating democratic docket, letting the president sow chaos over voting rights across an electorate increasingly dissatisfied with his governance. In March, Trump issued an executive order, “Ensuring Citizenship Verification and Integrity in Federal Elections,” that sought to give the White House and the executive branch control over mail ballots. The order creates a three-part behemoth of new voting measures. First, it directs the Department of Homeland Security to create citizenship lists of people in every state who the federal government believes are eligible to vote. Second, the order directs the attorney general to “prioritize” prosecutions against local election officials who violate election law by allowing people to vote who shouldn’t. Finally, the order launches a process for the USPS to issue a rule about new special barcodes on mail ballots; through that rule, the Postal Service is directed to refuse to mail ballots that don’t contain this brand-new barcode. Given that the Constitution gives the president no authority over election processing, no lower court could stomach the EO’s illegality. After a group of 23 states sued, multiple federal courts issued injunctions blocking its implementation. But then Trump’s government went to the Supreme Court, as it often does. At no point did the government attempt to argue that the EO is legal. Instead, it argued solely that the states lacked standing to bring the lawsuit to begin with. The Republican appointees sided with the government, reasoning that the order technically required other federal agencies to take action, rather than the states. Therefore, the states did not meet the injury required to bring their case at this time. But throughout the opinion, the court made no claims as to whether the court would rule in favor of the administration once the legal issues are squarely before the court. And to make matters more confusing, a separate court still has a separate injunction blocking parts of the EO in a case brought by the League of Women Voters. There’s a more twisted point that’s worth zeroing in on, and that’s the relationship between Trump’s mail ballot–suppression efforts and the Supreme Court’s horrid election law jurisprudence of the past few years. Jackson flags this exact relationship in her dissent, as the court’s new ruling Monday “lets another shoe drop in the Kafkaesque nightmare that our precedents have been steadily creating for certain plaintiffs.” The nightmare works primarily by manipulating timing. As state and federal governments began taking increasingly controversial election actions over the past decade, such as gerrymandering or throwing out lawful ballots, voters and civil rights groups have filed more suits. But the Supreme Court has developed two new limitations on how a lower court is able to rule on these election issues. Understanding both these limitations together is key to seeing just how much of a democratic crisis this court is enabling. First, in a case from a couple of terms ago called Trump v. CASA, the court held that lower district courts do not have the authority in most instances to issue what are called nationwide injunctions. A nationwide or universal injunction is just an order requiring the government to stop implementing an unlawful rule against the entire country. Instead, after CASA, the court can usually prevent only a losing government from enforcing its unlawful rule against the specific parties that filed the lawsuit. But after hamstringing what types of remedies a court can order, SCOTUS also restricted when any orders can come down with its second restriction. This is the controversial Purcell principle, which states that courts are not permitted to change voting rules close to Election Day. This restriction holds true even if the government’s own delay is the reason the lawsuit arrived so late. Purcell creates an absurd situation in which a plaintiff can challenge an election rule in court, the court can agree the rule is illegal, but then no one is allowed to remedy the illegality because it’s too close to an election. Now pile onto Purcell Monday’s mailing order. In its unsigned opinion, in which the three liberal justices dissented, the majority alleges that it is simply too early to challenge the forthcoming USPS rule because, well, who could possibly know what that rule might say? It characterizes the lower court as engaging in “a string of speculation” about what the federal agencies might do when, and how that speculation illustrates that the state’s claims aren’t ripe for review. The majority goes so far as to assert that the lower court “had to speculate” about whether the USPS would even issue a final rule whatsoever. This charge is especially absurd given that the USPS had already issued the final rule days before the Supreme Court’s order, publishing the 95-page final rule last Friday. To recap: Purcell punishes a plaintiff in voting cases for being too late to court. But Monday’s order says that a plaintiff, like the states that sued, can also come to court too early. See the issue? The court’s precedents are creating an invisible tightrope very few plaintiffs can cross successfully. And even if the plaintiffs eventually succeed in challenging the EO, thanks to the CASA decision, only voters in the blue states that sued will likely get any protection. What makes this tightrope even harder to navigate is that these legal developments don’t happen at the predictable cadence Americans might want from their election procedures. It’s the government itself that controls when it releases drafts and final versions of all these rules and regulations. The government can therefore strategically delay or speed up parts of the process, as it’s doing here, based on what it needs to argue to the judge in front of it and when. Jackson rightly describes this “Catch-Me-If-You-Can approach” to legal strategy as what it is: a cynical gotcha game designed to dodge legal accountability for as long as possible.
