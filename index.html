FCC chair warns media not to run the "fake polls" Trump keeps complaining about

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-21

Federal Communications Commission Chairman Brendan Carr today warned broadcasters about airing “fake polls,” amplifying one of the many complaints that President Trump has regularly made about the news media. Carr’s post on X said, “Many journalists and broadcasters have been amplifying or airing these Polls without regard to basic journalistic ethics and without informing the public that those Polls lack a scientific, accurate, or even real basis… Broadcasters are advised to avoid engaging in News Distortions or running Broadcast Hoaxes by airing Polls that would violate their regulatory obligations.” Carr urged broadcasters to “provide their viewers with additional context and information about Polls they have covered.” In fact, one small polling outfit was exposed as a hoax this week, and a similar incident occurred in August. News outlets have reported on these hoaxes, providing the very “context” that Carr said is needed. Carr’s post did not mention any specific fake polls or any specific broadcasters that aired them. The vagueness of Carr’s post may be useful for his and Trump’s ongoing battle against the news media. In August, Trump demanded that the FCC punish or rebuke an NBC journalist for saying he has “mixed results” in election endorsements, and wrote that the FCC must do something about “THE FAKE POLLS USED BY OUR CROOKED MEDIA.” Carr’s latest warning to broadcasters came as no surprise, given that he has spent much of the past two years threatening broadcast licenses and opening investigations into Trump’s least favorite news companies. Former Republican and Democratic FCC chairs and commissioners recently said Carr is seeking to censor and chill the speech of broadcasters through a proceeding that threatens the licenses of ABC-owned stations. “FCC Chair Brendan Carr’s threat against broadcasters over their reporting on political polls is yet another truly outrageous attempt to censor and control the information available to Americans as they prepare to vote… Carr is now seizing on reports about fabricated political polling as an excuse to once again try to scare broadcasters into self-censorship,” said a statement today from a group of former FCC chairs and officials. Today’s statement was attributed to former Chairman Tom Wheeler, a Democrat; former Chairman Mark Fowler, a Republican; and seven former high-ranking FCC officials. “Good-faith reporting on public polls—even ones that turn out to be made up—is not a violation of the FCC’s news distortion or hoax rules. And the FCC has zero authority to dictate editorial decisions,” they said. Wheeler, Fowler, and others previously signed a letter objecting to the ABC investigation. The letter called it “the latest attack in a sustained campaign by Chairman Carr and the Trump administration of threatening legal sanctions against licensees whose speech President Trump dislikes.” Anna Gomez, the only Democratic commissioner on the current FCC, wrote today that “Pollsters who intentionally mislead the public should answer for it, but broadcasters who report publicly available polling data should never be punished when a poll is wrong. FCC rules on news distortion or hoaxes do not apply to a station that reports this data in good faith.” An entity called Wedgewood Polls said yesterday that it published fabricated polling data “to test whether media outlets, aggregators, and commentators would report on data without verifying the source, methodology, or underlying firm legitimacy.” It claimed to be running an experiment on the modern political polling industry. “Despite having no history, formal credentials, or real data behind the vast majority of our releases, Wedgewood Polling was added to major polling aggregators, including The New York Times, FiftyPlusOne, and VoteHub, and repeatedly treated as a legitimate news source,” the statement said. Notably, the three polling aggregators mentioned in the statement are not broadcast stations or news networks whose programming is carried on local broadcast stations. That’s relevant because Carr has repeatedly said he merely uses the FCC’s regulatory authority over broadcasters with licenses to use public spectrum, and not media outlets that do not have FCC licenses. Carr’s post yesterday warned broadcasters to “comply with their public interest obligations” under “the terms of their federal licenses.” While Wedgewood Polls claimed that its data faced no scrutiny from news organizations, FiftyPlusOne said it was preparing to publish an article exposing the fraud and had sent a request for comment to Wedgewood. “They did not respond to our request, but instead posted a public statement on social media admitting that they had been fabricating data,” FiftyPlusOne wrote. FiftyPlusOne, The New York Times, and VoteHub all say they did some vetting of Wedgewood before deciding to use its polls. FiftyPlusOne wrote: Their statement alleges that FiftyPlusOne, The New York Times, and other poll aggregators did not conduct appropriate vetting of their firm prior to aggregating their polls; this is false. We reached out to them to ask who they were and confirm the methodology statement on their website. We were given two names, Jack Sims and Andrew Prater. We were able to independently confirm that Sims had previous experience in the polling industry and reviewed their methodology statement in detail. It is not atypical for a pollster to leave an existing firm, form their own, and self-fund some surveys to build reputation; we had no reason at the time to believe this case was any different. Wedgewood operated as if it were a legitimate polling firm, including collecting money from donors to conduct crowd-funded polls, and sharing raw survey data (which we now have confirmed was fraudulent) with FiftyPlusOne on Oct. 15, 2026. VoteHub Head of Data Science Zachary Donnini said that after talking to Wedgewood’s “head pollster,” Votehub “assigned them a D grade and included their polling data on our site.” Meanwhile, the New York Times updated its polling page with a statement about Wedgewood. The New York Times said it sends all new pollsters “a series of questions regarding the identities of the pollsters; the methods they were using to draw their sample, contact respondents and weight their polls; and the source of their funding.” Wedgewood answered the questions, and the NYT used 15 of its polls for state and congressional-district races. The Wedgewood “polls were never included in the tracker or average of President Trump’s approval rating or of the national polls tracking overall preference for control of Congress,” the NYT said. In the previous incident this year, a group called Median Strategies admitted it faked poll results as part of a “social experiment.” The NYT said it never included that group’s results in its trackers “because the group did not respond to our questions about its methodology.” Nate Silver removed Wedgewood from his polling site today. Wedgewood polls tended to be about 1 percentage point more favorable for Democrats than other polls, according to Silver.

In September, when Microsoft severely limited the game-streaming buffet available to Game Pass subscribers, the company said non-subscribers would soon be able to “purchase cloud playtime hours through the Xbox Store and stream eligible games they own on supported devices.” Today, Microsoft rolled out those a la carte Cloud Playtime purchases to Xbox Insiders, with prices that might be a bit galling for players who have already purchased these streamable games. The “introductory launch pricing” for Xbox’s Cloud Playtime purchases is as follows: Purchased playtime “remains available until it’s used,” Microsoft writes, so you don’t have to worry about your 50-hour purchase expiring before you can use it fully. Paying for playtime directly like this evokes images of old arcades, where players would invest a quarter or two (in 20th century dollars) often for just a few minutes of gameplay. That kind of metered play has fallen heavily out of fashion, though, in favor of one-time purchases for an all-you-can-play experience at home. On the surface, paying as low as 24 cents for an hour of streaming game time doesn’t sound completely unreasonable. Remember, though, that these streaming costs come on top of whatever you paid to buy the game in the first place. Unlike an Xbox Game Pass subscription—which includes a few hours of monthly streaming access to hundreds of games as part of the subscription price—these Cloud Playtime packs “require cloud playable game purchase or, for select games, free-to-play access,” Microsoft writes. Say what you will about the old arcade model, but you didn’t have to buy the cabinet and then pay a quarter per play. Microsoft’s metered Cloud Playtime packs differ from Google’s failed Stadia streaming experiment, which offered unlimited streaming playtime across devices for games you purchased on the platform. Of course, Stadia game purchases also didn’t let you download games for local play on a PC or console, making the overall value proposition quite different. That was especially true after Stadia’s 2022 shutdown made those purchases unplayable (though Google offered full refunds for any and all Stadia purchases at that point). In 2014, Sony offered a la carte streaming game access for set rental periods as part of its new PlayStation Now service. While that system didn’t require up-front game purchases, the initial pricing was exorbitant, rising to $30 for 90 days of real-time streaming access, even for games that could be purchased outright on disc for less. Those early streaming price experiments quickly gave way to an all-you-can-stream PlayStation Now subscription plan, which has now morphed into unlimited streaming of select PlayStation games through the top-tier, $20/month (or $160/year) PlayStation Plus Premium plan. Microsoft is also reminding cloud-curious gamers that Xbox Insiders can test free, ad-supported streams of games they own without buying any playtime packs. That might be the best option for gamers who want to test out a quick Halo session on their phone, rather than maintaining regular streaming access to their Xbox libraries. But Microsoft also says that paid users will get “shorter wait times [and] high-quality streaming,” meaning a deteriorated experience for those who use the ad-supported tier. In the end, it’s a question of how much of a mass market there is for Xbox Cloud Streaming on its own, rather than as an outgrowth of Xbox Game Pass subscriptions. Last month, Microsoft said that only 4 percent of Game Pass subscribers would be affected by the subscription’s new limits of five to 15 hours of streaming playtime per month. That suggests only a small minority of Xbox users want to use cloud streaming for a significant portion of their gaming time, even when it was included as a free perk with their existing subscription. How much less interest will there be among non-subscribers who have to pay by the hour for streaming access to games they already own?

Enterprises keep buying software upgrades that IT thinks will make things worse

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-21

Visit Original Website
Enterprise-sized businesses spend, on average, 28 percent of their software budgets on unwanted upgrades, migrations, and licensing changes. Often, those firms’ IT heads think those changes are detrimental. Those results are according to a survey of 2,018 chief information officers and senior IT decision-makers at companies with at least 10,000 employees that Origina commissioned for its report, “The Software Tax.” About half of the respondents (1,011) are from the US, and the rest are from the United Kingdom. All respondents worked at companies with at least $1 billion in annual revenue. Keep in mind that Origina provides maintenance for various enterprise software. Its report points to independent software maintenance as a way for IT leaders to regain control over software budgets, illustrating its potential bias. A third-party research consultancy, Censuswide, conducted the survey. Among the report’s most concerning findings, enterprises often make software purchases that IT heads believe could hurt the organization. Ninety-five percent of respondents said “their organization has paid for a vendor-driven upgrade, migration, or licensing change in the past three years that it believed at the time would reduce performance, reliability, or business value,” the report says. Fifty-three percent of respondents “have done so more than once,” the report notes. “Some 76 percent say their organization has very little real leverage in negotiations, and 80 percent say moving away from major vendors would cause significant disruption,” the report says, referring to the share of respondents who selected “strongly agree” or “somewhat agree” to those statements. “When the alternative to an unwanted upgrade is losing support on systems the business runs on, saying yes is often a rational response,” Tomás O’Leary, one of the report’s authors and Origina’s CEO and founder, said in a statement. “That’s exactly the problem. The market has left some of the most experienced technology leaders in the world with little room to refuse, and that cost shows up in budgets, in engineering time, and in innovation that never gets funded.” The result is less funding for other areas, particularly, per the report, innovation (named by 36 percent of respondents), human resources (32 percent), security (31 percent), and “customer-facing investment” (31 percent). In a statement accompanying the survey’s announcement, Liberty Mutual’s CIO, James McGlennon, pointed out that an upgrade cycle could occupy top technical workers for “the better part of a year.” “That’s not a one-off cost, it’s a recurring drag on capacity, and every year it repeats is a year those people aren’t working on the initiatives that actually differentiate the business,” he said. The majority (91 percent) surveyed would support laws that give enterprises “protections comparable with those available to long-term residential tenants.” Only 1 percent opposed this (with the rest signaling that they neither support nor oppose the idea). CIOs should “negotiate the exit before the entry” to have more control over software budgets, Marshall Van Alstyne, the report’s co-author and an information sciences professor at Boston University, said in a statement. Origina’s report suggests “settling the terms of a potential exit,” including setting a price for migration assistance and caps on renewal and expansion prices, during initial negotiations with the software vendor. Origina’s report didn’t call out any specific vendors as forcing enterprises’ hands. However, every survey respondent currently runs “at least one of IBM, HCL, VMware/Broadcom, or SAP at meaningful scale.”

Tesla can no longer rely on selling regulatory credits to other automakers: these fell to $113 million this quarter, down from $417 million in Q3 2025. Now, virtually every other automaker has a portfolio of electric vehicles and no longer needs to offset its emissions by pooling with Tesla. In the US, Tesla CEO Elon Musk’s support helped elect a president who has abolished such credits entirely, with Musk’s blessing. Tesla’s letter to investors says its flat revenues were due to lower average selling prices, selling fewer cars, not receiving as much in credits, and also the decline of its energy storage business. Together with the reduced credits and battery and solar sales, a big jump in operating expenses is responsible for the diminishing profitability; some of this is Tesla spending rampantly on AI hardware, but some is also the CEO’s extravagant compensation package.

But then I happened to catch Apple’s behind-the-scenes video in my YouTube recommendations, and I realized I did have thoughts. It’s a short clip, respecting your time at just a little over a minute long if you want to watch for yourself. Let’s get the obvious part out of the way: It’s pretty peak Apple. More than a little pretentious, with all the resources a $5 trillion market cap can bring to bear on every problem. There’s a design agency (TBWA/Media Arts Lab), a pop hitmaker music producer (Fred again.. and yes, that’s how you spell it), a whole crewed set with cameras and lighting, and a mineral oil tank equipped with a transducer to effectively transmit audio signals into physical ripples. They shot a ton of footage of the oil (chosen for its higher viscosity) interacting with the branding printed on transparent panels. Fast Company has a nice detailed breakdown of the technical aspects if you want to dig in more. It’s a little eye-rolly with the narration, but honestly also lovely in the execution and care. And it made me realize how much I think we’re all going to value this kind of hands-on, human crafting in an era where anything can be generated out of a prompt. Like many Ars readers, I have feelings about generative AI imagery. It’s feeling increasingly impossible to avoid in daily life. My new favorite term is “slop ghouls.” One really unfortunate side effect of that persistence is a new natural tendency to be suspicious of everything you see. Was that picture you just saw hand-drawn, or did someone prompt it into existence with styles stolen from human artists? I see us increasingly place genuine value on not just human-created craft, but seeing the process behind it that shows the hands at work. This Apple video was a neat bit of promotion, but it also provided the receipts. Real people did real things in the physical world to make the final images and videos. The final effect is obviously cleaned up and post-processed. But when I look at it now, I don’t just see Liquid Glass; I see the humans and their time and energy and creativity. To me, at least, that matters. Showing your work, proving the time you put in—I appreciate it. I’m slightly less grumpy for it! Generative AI is likely here to stay. There’s no stuffing the genie back in the bottle. I probably need to find some peace with that, though I reserve the right to still yell at clouds about it. In the meantime, I think we should do more to celebrate human involvement in things and not be shy about showing the process and behind-the-scenes work.

The U.S. market has never been more focused on one single bet—but AI’s payoff will likely take decades

Fortune | FORTUNE | 2026-10-22

Visit Original Website
Ming Lu is a senior advisory partner at KKR and previously was the executive chairman of KKR Asia Pacific. The views expressed here are his own. Americans have placed an unprecedented bet on the transformative power of artificial intelligence. The past 50 years of economic history suggests it won’t pay off, at least not on a timetable the market expects. Not since the Gilded Age—when, in 1885, railroad stocks accounted for more than 80% of active trading on the New York Stock Exchange—has the U.S. economy wagered so much on a single theme. When that bubble burst in the Panic of 1893, a third of railroads went bust, more than 500 banks failed, and the economy plunged into a four-year depression. Today’s frantic AI buildout may be on a similar trajectory. AI-related capital spending has surged to about 2% of U.S. GDP, from near zero five years ago. But this time, the AI wager is concentrated in far fewer companies than the railway mania ever was. In January, the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis reported that investments in information processing equipment, software, research and development, and data centers—four investment categories economists consider the closest proxies for AI investment—accounted for 39% of U.S. economic growth in the first three quarters of 2025, higher than the 28% those activities contributed in 2000, the peak of the dot-com boom. Pantheon Macroeconomics calculates that without AI spending, U.S. corporate investment in equipment was negative last year. Consumption, too, is heavily dependent on AI. The top 20% of income earners now account for approximately 60% of all U.S. consumer spending, according to Moody’s Analytics. The source of their wealth? The AI stock rally. AI has become the dominant theme across all segments of U.S. capital markets. In equities, the “Magnificent Seven”—Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia, Tesla—boast a combined $25 trillion in market capitalization. That’s 35% of the value of the S&P 500 and the highest concentration in a handful of stocks since just before the crash of 1929. Separately, research from the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City shows that AI-related companies’ share of investment-grade bond issuance increased from 2% in 2023 to 10% in 2026, highlighting the growing concentration of this kind of borrowing in credit markets. In venture capital, AI captured 81% of a record $300 billion in global venture funding in the first quarter of 2026, and just four companies—OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, and Waymo—absorbed two-thirds of the total. The result is a self-reinforcing loop. Hyperscalers will spend $800 billion this year on chips, data centers, and the power to run them, propping up GDP growth. Investors are bidding up AI stocks, fattening the portfolios of the richest 20%, whose spending boosts consumption. As the buildout outgrows hyperscalers’ cash flow, new investment is increasingly financed by debt and off-balance-sheet partnerships. The U.S. growth engine now runs on a narrow set of mutually reinforcing drivers. If one stalls, the others lose power too. The Bank for International Settlements warns in its 2026 Annual Economic Report that AI exuberance risks ending in a “lengthy investment bust.” The BIS flagged “circular financing” as a core vulnerability, more than half of investment in AI firms comes from other AI firms, with recipients using that money to buy their backers’ products. Profits are being funded by investors rather than end customers. America’s AI bet is underwritten by a single assumption: That by 2027, growth driven by infrastructure will hand off to growth driven by AI-powered efficiency gains across the broader economy. I think that’ll come much later than the market expects. As I argued in Fortune a few weeks ago, five decades of digital innovation achieved broad consumer adoption yet came with a decline in aggregate productivity growth. This is the Innovation Paradox: more innovation and slower growth. Technology diffusion follows a double K-shaped pattern. Consumers adopt almost immediately; enterprises, at a glacial pace. Within enterprises, a handful of superstar firms embrace new technologies quickly while the rest struggle to make use of them. The structure of the U.S. economy makes things even worse. The U.S. has shifted from vertically-linked industrial sectors—where innovations could naturally travel through supply chains—to horizontally spread service sectors that resist transformation because they depend on human judgment. These “stagnant” sectors, as the economist William Baumol called them, now account for roughly half of GDP in advanced economies. Firms are also saddled with debilitating administrative and regulatory compliance costs, which I estimate are now past the topping point at which they strangle innovation. Adding to the paradox: the concentration of capital in AI-linked companies starves the rest of the economy of investment needed to be AI-ready. If hospitals, schools, logistics companies, and retailers can’t invest in digital infrastructure and workforce retraining, the broad-based productivity gains that justify today’s AI valuations will never arrive. AI could still defy the paradox: unlike previous digital waves, it is a cognitive technology that can reason and make judgments, precisely what’s needed to unlock productivity in labor-intensive Baumol sectors. But only deep adoption across a broad spectrum of the economy will realize that potential. Firms need to allocate capital to workers and workflows, not just chips and data centers. That’s a possible silver lining if the AI bubble bursts—that the assets financed by irrationally exuberant investors end up reshaping the economy for the better. Canals in the 1830s, railroads in the 1880s, electrification in the 1920s, telecoms and the internet in the late 1990s—each ended in painful collapse, yet the physical infrastructure created in those speculative booms laid the foundations for decades of later prosperity. Will data centers, semiconductors, and AI models prove equally long-lasting? Perhaps. But that process will take decades, not months or years. In the short term, the more concentrated the bet, the more severe the potential reckoning. No prior bubble spanned so many dimensions of the economy simultaneously—GDP growth, consumer spending, equity markets, debt markets, and venture capital all tethered to a single theme. That is not an economic launchpad. It is a vulnerability of historic proportions. As the extent of Zimbardo’s manipulation came to light, the 1971 study lost credibility in academic circles, even as its pop-cultural legend kept growing. Fifty-five years after it was conducted, perhaps the only definitive conclusion to draw from the Stanford Prison Experiment is that the Stanford Prison Experiment is not worth repeating. But just try telling that to the reality-TV machine, let alone a platform currently at work on the third, all-celebrity season of a competition series that cheerfully (and, of course, non-lethally) recreates the horrors of its international hit anticapitalist battle royale thriller Squid Game. On Thursday, a 16-year-old armed with a pump-action 12-gauge shotgun that his mother allegedly bought him for his birthday and a backpack full of ammunition opened fire at Silver Rail Elementary School, in Bend, Oregon, killing three children and a teacher. A 7-year-old student and another teacher were critically injured. The tragedy could have been worse if not for the heroic actions of an unarmed teacher who threw her cellphone at the gunman before tackling him, and a father of two students at the school, who heard the commotion and used his pistol to kill the shooter. While both people who intervened to stop the shooter deserve high praise for their selfless heroism, some commentators—including President Donald Trump and the National Rifle Association—are celebrating the “good guy with a gun” while ignoring the unarmed teacher who intervened first. This “good guy with a gun” myth is a seductive marketing tool for the gun industry. Many find comfort in the promise that the solution to our most urgent insecurities is readily available at the nearest gun shop. If we are afraid, we need only purchase a firearm to ensure we are ready when a “bad guy” breaches our cherished boundaries. But there are critical problems with the armed “good guy” trope. One is that the most reliable empirical research on defensive gun use demonstrates that incidents in which a “good guy with a gun” stops a “bad guy” are vanishingly rare. The overwhelming majority of fact-based, peer-reviewed research shows definitively that more guns are associated with more gun-related deaths and injuries. Consistent scientific evidence demonstrates that the net effect of greater intrusion of guns into public life increases homicide, so any benefits from the relatively rare cases of defensive gun use can’t alter the calculus that more innocent lives will be lost as guns become easier to acquire and carry. Another problem is that our cultural assumptions about “good guys” are laden with biases, particularly racist assumptions that undermine the safety and self-defensive rights of people of color. We have witnessed the exclusionary effects of the “good guy” narrative when law-abiding Black gun owners, like Philando Castile, are shot and killed by police who see them as a threat in spite of their lawful comportment. Armed Black “good guys,” like security guard Jemel Roberson and U.S. Army veteran E.J. Bradford—who responded to public shootings in November 2018 with armed heroism—have been shot and killed by police, who misidentified them as armed “bad guys.” Similarly, states with so-called “stand your ground” laws, enforce them in ways that disproportionately favor leniency toward white people who have killed Black people. Women who “stand their ground” against men they know, meanwhile, are more likely to be punished than to be exonerated or celebrated as “good guys.” “Stand your ground” laws have spread to a significant majority of the states in spite of these biases and studies demonstrating their connection to increased rates of firearm homicide. Despite data showing that true incidents of self-defensive gun use are rare, the “gun rights” lobby has continued to promote the “good guy with a gun” narrative in the service of deregulatory firearm policies. Recently, it succeeded in persuading the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention to post faulty and misleading data on the agency’s website. The site currently presents a vastly overblown estimate of 500,000 to 2.5 million defensive gun-use incidents annually. These figures are based on suspect “research” by a small handful of people whose work has been soundly discredited. Much of this research depends on surveys, which are inaccurate metrics for determining whether someone was actually defending themself when they pulled a gun on another person. Reliable scholarship—including a 1997 study by Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health scholar David Hemenway—reveals a significant confirmation bias in such surveys: A person who pulls their gun is more likely to depict their actions in a positive and even heroic light. Even though multiple reliable studies show that self-reported defensive gun-use incidents are significantly exaggerated, the propaganda of armed self-defense remains influential, informing policies that continue to deregulate firearm ownership and carry. Twenty-nine states allow people to carry firearms into public spaces without a license or training, and approximately two-thirds of the states have adopted some version of a “stand your ground” law. There are deadly consequences to the mass distribution of this misinformation. The most respected peer-reviewed data show that guns are not reliable tools of self-defense. To the contrary, guns in the home significantly raise the risk of intimate partner homicide, and guns are the leading cause of death for American children. Instead of protecting ourselves and each other, Americans are using guns to kill each other, and—more frequently—to kill ourselves. According to the K–12 School Shooting Database, 2025 saw 235 shooting incidents at K–12 schools across the U.S. In 2026, we have witnessed approximately 150 school shootings thus far. Perhaps, instead of promoting more guns as the solution to this ongoing nightmare, we might focus on prevention. The data reveal a very clear picture: Over the past 20 years, the 10 states with the highest levels of gun regulation enjoyed a substantial 45.3 percent drop in the murder rate, while the 20 states with the lowest levels of gun regulation showed only an 11.9 percent decline. When our nation’s primary source for public health information has been captured by the purveyors of “gun rights,” we have capitulated to a defeatist fantasy of armed safety, ultimately placing profit for the firearms industry over public health. It’s going to be another bad year for trans rights at the Supreme Court, and this time, it may cost lives. The justices will soon consider the case of Alishea Sophia Kingdom, a transgender woman who’s incarcerated in the federal prison system. In a statement filed in court, she explains that she knew from the age of 7 that she was a girl, and when she was incarcerated, the doctors at the Federal Bureau of Prisons diagnosed her with gender dysphoria and prescribed her hormone therapy. But on Jan. 26, 2025, less than a week after Donald Trump took office, BOP staff told her she couldn’t get hormones any more. They started misgendering her, and they even removed the books with LGBTQ+ themes from the prison library. The denial of care, Kingdom says, made her gender dysphoria become incapacitating. She experienced anxiety, panic attacks, and mood swings. She can’t sleep. She began having suicidal thoughts. The ACLU filed a lawsuit on her behalf, and a federal judge entered a preliminary injunction finding that the policy was probably unlawful and directing the government to continue providing medical care while the litigation goes forward. Cathy Thompson, a psychologist and prison administrator who formerly worked for the BOP as its acting national administrator for psychology services, explained to the court that cutting off care for trans inmates is not only unhealthy but dangerous, “potentially causing attempts to self-castrate, self-harm, suicide, and aggression.” As the ACLU’s brief observes, the Trump policy is a sudden about-face for an agency that has been providing healthcare for trans inmates for decades. Before 2026, the rule has been that if you were getting hormones at the time you’re incarcerated, you’d continue to get them during your incarceration. And in 2010, the Bureau of Prisons made a new rule that prisoners could start hormone treatment in prison if it was “medically indicated.” That means hormone treatment was the policy under the first George Bush, Bill Clinton, the second George Bush, Barack Obama, Donald Trump (the first time), and Joe Biden. To Trumpists, this might all sound radical. In fact, it’s a straightforward application of a fundamental legal principle: If the government imprisons you, they have to provide for your basic medical needs. This isn’t charity, socialism, or some act of benevolence: Incarcerating someone makes them dependent on the government for their medical needs, so a toothache that would be just an annoyance in the outside world can, if untreated in prison, quickly become a form of torture. Gender dysphoria is a medical need like any other. It’s hard to express this to cisgender people who’ve never experienced it, and every trans person experiences things differently. But here’s one example: Before I had electrolysis to remove my facial hair, I hated seeing and feeling the stubble on my face. I have other things about my appearance I’d like to change. But the stubble was different. Imagine looking at the mirror and seeing ants crawling all over the lower part of your face. That’s how having stubble felt to me. So I can only imagine how it would feel to be incarcerated and barred from getting treatment. This isn’t something a society that thinks of itself as decent should do to anyone. If the Supreme Court allows the policy to take effect, it will be undermining one of our most fundamental constitutional protections. But that’s not the only principle the court will have to shred before it can uphold this policy. One of the most basic rules of government in the United States, albeit much less famous than the rule against “cruel and unusual punishment,” is the rule that government agencies’ decisions must be “reasonable and reasonably explained.” In the technical language of the law, decisions can’t be “arbitrary and capricious.” As the lower court explained in Trump v. Kingdom, “An action is arbitrary and capricious when the agency relies on inappropriate factors, fails to consider important aspects of the problem, or ignores relevant evidence.” To let the Trump policy go into effect—barring incarcerated trans people from receiving the healthcare they need—the Supreme Court is going to have to walk all over this principle, because the Trump administration’s reasons for denying people necessary medical care are no reasons at all. Instead of trying to assess whether incarcerated trans people genuinely need medical care—a question on which there is zero legitimate medical disagreement—the Trump administration portrays these denials of medical care as part of a struggle against something it calls “gender ideology.” This is not a real thing. On the day he was inaugurated, Trump signed an executive order announcing that “[f]ederal funds shall not be used to promote gender ideology.” It ordered the Justice Department to revise policies on medical care in prison and specifically to “ensure that no Federal funds are expended for any medical procedure, treatment, or drug for the purpose of conforming an inmate’s appearance to that of the opposite sex.” Within a month, the BOP had banned “any medical procedure, treatment, or drug for the purpose of conforming an inmate’s appearance to that of the opposite sex”—and “any items that align with transgender ideology.” All of this is part of a broader campaign. Nine days after Trump took office, the federal Office of Personnel Management ordered agencies to suspend any employee “whose position description involves inculcating or promoting gender ideology,” and to get rid of any websites or documents that “inculcate or promote gender ideology.” It wasn’t ideology that made me want to claw the stubble off my own face. And it isn’t “ideology” that leads some children at the age of 3 or 4 to announce to their parents that they are girls, even though everyone has been calling them a boy, or vice versa. This is a well-documented phenomenon, even among children whose parents have never heard of transgender people and who are presumably not 3-year-old ideologues. Studies have shown that trans kids are consistent and resilient about their gender identity. But targeting a marginalized group, and pretending they’re followers of some dangerous ideology, is a strategy familiar to anyone who’s studied oppressive and totalitarian governments. The technique has been used with great effectiveness here in the United States, where segregationists and white supremacists love to describe civil rights protesters as “outside agitators” who believe in “anti-American” ideologies. During the protests that followed George Floyd’s murder, we saw a resurgence of the term being used to smear Black Lives Matter protesters. The mundane, everyday requirements of the Administrative Procedure Act are a great tool for combating this paranoid strategy for targeting the marginalized. The Trump administration’s brief to the Supreme Court, remarkably, makes no effort to claim that medical evidence supports denying healthcare to trans prisoners. Instead, it says that well-established standards of trans healthcare have been “called into serious question by medical professionals in the United States and other countries” (which is untrue, as experts explained to the lower courts) . Therefore, the brief says, in the absence of reliable evidence for trans healthcare, the BOP is justified in rejecting the advice of the doctors who work for it as merely “anecdotal.” The brief makes no mention of “gender ideology,” although it’s the driving concern in the executive order that directed this policy change. None of this is reasoned decision-making. Trans people suffer less when they get the care they need and are allowed to transition. The Supreme Court’s majority, it seems likely, will dance around this obvious truth in its determination to let the Trump administration target trans people for greater suffering. The new link lets lawyers using Harvey draw on that checked record while they research and draft, rather than starting from scratch. The record keeps track of where evidence conflicts, where there are gaps and which documents back up a claim, Mary said. It also shows which documents were used and which were left out. “The value of AI in litigation depends on the quality of the factual record it works from,” said Daniel Lord-Doyle, Mary’s co-founder. Trial lawyers are already leaning on AI. Mark Lanier, who won a $6M verdict against Meta and Google in June, said AI tools that reviewed daily trial transcripts cut his workload by about two-thirds. When new documents change the picture, Mary suggests updates, which lawyers must approve. Mary says firms keep control of who can access the data, and that client files are not used to train AI models. The link runs through Harvey’s Connector Library, which lets law firms plug Harvey into the tools they already use. Workflow software firm Autologyx also joined on Wednesday. Mary raised $7M in seed funding earlier this year to expand in the US, according to SmartCompany. Harvey has been growing fast. It raised $550M in September, has more than 3,000 customers and released its own legal AI model in August.
