A 1,000-mile cloud on Mars may reveal physics never seen in nature

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-24

Visit Original Website
Every morning during the Martian spring and summer, a thin and extremely long cloud emerges, only to disappear again. The wispy formation appears near the volcano Arsia Mons and gradually elongates until it reaches over 1,000 miles—about the distance from New York to Kansas City as the crow flies—before dissipating. This cycle then repeats for several months. Since it was first recorded by a probe in 2018, scientists have been unable to find a plausible explanation for the existence of this cloud, often referred to as the Arsia Mons Elongated Cloud (AMEC). Until now. Recently, researchers successfully modeled the cloud’s formation for the first time, their results suggesting that it may be driven by a physical process that has never been directly observed in any planet’s atmosphere. “We found that we needed to include some exotic physics… physics that, while included in textbooks, is treated as theoretical and usually thought not to happen in nature. It certainly hasn’t been seen in action before,” Jorge Hernández-Bernal, a planetary scientist at Paris’ Sorbonne Université and lead author of the study, said in a press release. “Once we included this physics in our simulations, the AMEC emerged just as we hoped.” On Earth, clouds usually form when water vapor creates droplets or ice crystals in the presence of tiny particles, such as dust or salt. The new study, published in Nature Geoscience, instead suggests AMEC could be forming through so-called homogeneous nucleation, where vapor changes directly into ice crystals on its own without any help from debris. “It’s akin to droplets of condensation appearing in the middle of a room, rather than on a window,” Hernández-Bernal explained. “It’s wholly unexpected.” This phenomenon requires extreme conditions—a sharp temperature drop of 30° Celsius within 10 minutes and high relative humidity. The Arsia Mons volcano also plays a key role. When wind current hits the 65,000-foot-tall volcano, it creates a powerful wave that launches moist air high in the atmosphere in a matter of minutes. Scientists have assumed a similar phenomenon could occur in the upper atmospheres of other planets, including Venus and even Earth, but it has never been observed. That’s not too surprising given the extreme air humidity required for this phenomenon, which is more than 100,000 times greater than any value measured on Earth. Before this modeling, it was not known that these humidity levels existed on Mars either. “We don’t have nearly as much information about Mars’ atmosphere as we do about Earth’s, so reproducing the AMEC to this degree is a big success,” said Hernández-Bernal, adding, “the result is remarkable.” This story originally appeared on WIRED Czech Republic and Slovakia and has been translated from Czech.

Ice sheet expansion may have started 2,000 years ago, but we canceled that

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-24

Visit Original Website
Barnes Ice Cap sits on the central plateau of Baffin Island, in the Canadian Arctic. It is the last surviving fragment of the Laurentide Ice Sheet, the largest of the Pleistocene ice sheets, which at its peak buried most of Canada under two miles of ice and locked away around 80 meters of sea level. That remnant has now yielded direct physical evidence that ice sheets might have reversed their retreat and started to grow again thousands of years ago. In a recent Science study, researchers show that the Laurentide reached its smallest size about 3,600 years ago, then started re-expanding roughly 2,000 years ago, which the team interprets as the opening move of the next glaciation. Apparently, this brand-new ice age was on its way until about 1960, when human-caused climate warming reversed it. “Barnes is the last remaining piece of one of the domes of the Laurentide Ice Sheet,” says Nicolás Young, a geologist at Columbia University’s Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory and lead author of the study. As that enormous dome thinned through the Holocene, its margin withdrew up onto Baffin Island, and what’s left today is the deflated core of something that was once two miles thick. The reason Barnes can testify about its own history, Young explains, comes down to the temperature at its base. Most glaciers are warm-based, meaning meltwater at the bed lets them slide, and sliding ice grinds up everything beneath it, erasing the record. Barnes is cold-based, and thus frozen directly onto the bedrock beneath it, which prevents it from sliding. “If there is an ice event where you live and you have to go and scrape off your windshield, that ice that’s plastered to the windshield of your car, that’s a good example of how cold-based ice operates. It’s sort of just stuck to what it’s on,” Young says. This stuck ice preserves what’s beneath instead of eroding it. As the Laurentide retreated across the plateau, tundra plants colonized the newly bare ground. When the ice came back later, it simply smothered them in place and held them there, intact, for two millennia. To pinpoint the exact moment of this turnaround, Young’s team used two independent dating methods. The first method measures beryllium-10, an isotope that accumulates in the top few centimeters of rock that is exposed to the sky. By sampling a boulder’s upper surface and counting the beryllium, researchers could learn how long it has been uncovered. That technique put a date on the ice sheet’s retreat. It turned out the Laurentide had withdrawn to within today’s ice margin by 3,600 years ago, probably shrinking further by around 3,000. The second dating method looked at the dead plants. “There were all these plants growing, just like today. And included in that are mosses, these little mosses that are rooted in the ground,” Young says. “So they’re there, they’re living their life, and eventually the Laurentide Ice Sheet starts to re-expand. And when that ice margin covers the moss, it literally kills it.” By radiocarbon dating the remnants of the moss, the team could pinpoint the moment it died, yielding what glaciologists call a kill date—the moment the ice arrived at that spot. By stringing multiple kill dates of moss samples taken from different spots, scientists reconstructed a map of an advancing ice margin. About 70 percent of the team’s radiocarbon dates cluster around 1800 CE, at the culmination of the Little Ice Age. “It’s all sort of the same thing,” he says. The Little Ice Age marks the point where Northern Hemisphere glaciers were at their most extensive in recent millennia. At Barnes, Young argues, it was just one late pulse within an advance that had been running for two thousand years. The most important plants for Young’s team, however, are still hidden under ice. Barnes’ margin has retreated 200 to 800 meters since 1960, exposing mosses killed from about 1000 CE onward. Anything older remains buried. To pinpoint the exact moment the ice started advancing, the team had to look at Barnes’ neighbors. Scattered across the same plateau are other small ice caps, close enough to share the same climate. Earlier studies collected nearly 200 kill dates from beneath them—not one is older than 2,000 years. “What that means is that all of those ice caps up there didn’t start advancing or re-expanding until about 2,000 years ago,” Young says. “Since the Barnes Ice Cap is right next door, it’s probably a good bet that it also started expanding 2,000 years ago.” Young argues that continued retreat of the Barnes Ice Cap should eventually reveal the decisive samples. “If we went back there in 20 years or something, as the ice margin has retreated a little more, we actually might be able to sample those 2,000-year-old plants,” he says. According to the study, the ice started re-expanding at this precise moment because two global forces were pulling it in opposite directions, and one of them eventually won. Solar energy reaching high northern latitudes peaked about 10,000 to 11,000 years ago and has declined ever since, pushing the planet toward the next ice age. Meanwhile, atmospheric carbon dioxide crept up by roughly 20 parts per million between 8,000 and 2,000 years ago, increasing the greenhouse effect that was heating the atmosphere up. “That drop in insolation and temperatures actually overwhelmed the small temperature forcing the little bit of carbon dioxide rise over the same time span,” Young explains. Eventually, 2,000 years ago, temperatures dropped far enough due to lower sunlight in the high latitudes, and the effects of carbon dioxide weren’t high enough, so ice sheets started to re-expand. This happened despite the fact that calculations of orbital forcings, together with modeling of how carbon dioxide interferes with glacial inception, have suggested the next ice age was postponed by tens of thousands of years. Young argues there’s really no contradiction between these models and the evidence his team collected in the field. “A lot of people have defined glacial inception as glaciers and ice sheets needing to be of a certain size—big enough that we’ll say, ah, yes, we are into the next ice age,” Young says. “And that’s perfectly reasonable, because until now there’s been no way to actually pinpoint when an ice sheet first turns around and starts to expand.” But a few geological moments after the Laurentide sheet started re-expanding, humanity entered the chat. Greenhouse gases had risen by about 12 ppm between 1750 and 1850, another 50 ppm by 1960, and a further 138 ppm by 2024. “Once we hit the Industrial Revolution, we’re now pumping way more carbon dioxide at a magnitude that has never occurred in the geologic record,” Young says. By 1960, Barnes’ mass balance went negative and its advance ended. Today, Barnes Ice Cap is dying. In the 1960s and ’70s, roughly 35 percent of its surface sat above the area’s snow line. By the early 2000s, about 3 percent did. Today, none of it does. “This means in the summer, it’s all melting. There’s no more accumulation zone. There’s no more new ice forming,” Young says. “An ice cap or glacier cannot survive if it does not have an accumulation zone.” According to his models, under any plausible emissions scenario, Barnes will disappear within a few centuries. So did we actually dodge the bullet by warming the climate so quickly? “Frankly, in modern times, no one wants to live in an ice age climate. Things would be quite hard and quite different,” Young says. He argues, though, that we did not exactly do ourselves a favor here. To get to a true, full ice age climate with big ice sheets, it would have taken several tens of thousands of years. “I would argue that the pumping of greenhouse gases into the atmosphere hasn’t really saved us from anything,” Young claims. What we have done instead is interrupt a cycle that was very old and very regular. “The pacing of ice ages over the last 2.5 million years has been one of the most consistent cycles in the Earth system that we have,” Young says. “We have evidence now that humans, and specifically greenhouse gases, have actually terminated one of the most consistent cycles on Earth.” The study is published in Science: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aed3379

NASA updates lander plans for Moon landings, finally solves long-festering Orion issue

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-23

Visit Original Website
During a lengthy news conference on Friday, NASA provided a detailed overview of how it plans to return astronauts to the lunar surface as soon as 2028 as part of its Artemis Program. Although much of the information behind these plans has been previously reported at Ars Technica and elsewhere, this marked the first time NASA has laid them out in detail and acknowledged some substantial changes in the architecture. Most notably, for SpaceX’s Starship vehicle, the revised plan calls for the Orion spacecraft to dock with the Starship lunar lander in high-Earth orbit before transiting to the Moon. And for Blue Origin, the mission architecture includes four launches of the New Glenn rocket, but no spacecraft-to-spacecraft refuelings. Additionally, the Blue Origin plan allows for the possibility of its Blue Moon lander rendezvousing with Orion in low-lunar orbit. During the presentation on Friday, NASA officials said they continue to anticipate launching the Artemis III mission—a demonstration involving docking with both Blue Moon and Starship near Earth—by “summer” 2027. And they are still tracking toward a 2028 launch of Artemis IV, which would involve an actual landing on the lunar surface. “This is an achievable path we are on right now,” NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said. “We have the mandate, we have the resources, and the support from our stakeholders to be able to execute, and that’s what we intend to do.” It is commendable that Isaacman and his leadership have taken a hard look at every element of the Artemis program and sought to make changes where they can to help the lunar lander companies execute and accelerate their plans. But can they hold to those dates? The biggest ask SpaceX engineers had in meetings with NASA in early 2026 was removing the requirement to dock with Orion in a near-rectilinear halo orbit around the Moon. This required more energy and had other difficulties associated with it. When NASA canceled the Lunar Gateway earlier this year, it solved this problem for SpaceX. Now SpaceX will fuel a Starship lunar lander in low-Earth orbit before docking with Orion in high-Earth orbit. The crew will fly to the Moon inside Orion before two of four astronauts transfer into Starship for a landing on the Moon. A key question surrounding SpaceX’s architecture is the number of refuelings required to top off a Starship for a lunar mission. On Thursday a NASA Safety Panel reported that the current number of tanker launches is “about 22.” SpaceX founder Elon Musk said that estimate was wrong and that the actual number will be six to eight. During a question-and-answer session on Friday afternoon, NASA officials declined to put a precise number on the refueling launches required. A lot of uncertainty remains in the lift capacity of Starship, as well as in how much cryogenic methane and liquid oxygen will boil off in orbit and how efficient the process of transferring propellants from one ship to another will be. However, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said this of the estimate of six to eight launches proffered by Musk: it was possible but represented the “ultimate potential end state” of Starship. In response to NASA’s call for a faster approach to reaching the Moon, Blue Origin has modified its plans such that it would no longer require refueling between vehicles for initial missions. Under the concept of operations released Friday, a New Glenn rocket (this could be an expendable version of the smaller 7×2 variant of New Glenn, or a reusable 9×4 rocket) would launch three “transfer stages” to low-Earth orbit. These transfer stages are modeled after the Blue Moon Mark 1 lander but have some significant modifications. Then New Glenn would launch the Blue Moon Mark 2 lander into orbit, where it would rendezvous with the transfer stages that will push the stack to the Moon. This is not new information, as Ars first reported this architecture in March. However, NASA did reveal a potentially significant change on Friday. The chart shared by NASA showed Orion docking with the Blue Moon lander in an elliptical lunar orbit, approximately 8,500 km above the Moon’s surface. However NASA is also working to have the option of docking with Orion in low-lunar orbit. According to Jeremy Parsons, the manager of the Artemis program for NASA, such an orbit a few hundred km above the Moon would significantly reduce the propellant needed by Blue Moon to bring astronauts down to the lunar surface and back up. This mass could be used elsewhere on the vehicle. The lower orbit would also offer more and faster opportunities for astronauts to get off the lunar surface if needed. In short, it makes some aspects of a lunar landing significantly easier for Blue Origin. As part of this, NASA announced Friday that it is working with United Launch Alliance to develop an “extended” version of the Centaur V upper stage. In concert with the core stage of the Space Launch System, this upper stage would allow Orion to reach low-lunar orbit. This is a big deal. It has long been a point of embarrassment for NASA that Orion, with its low-performing Service Module, does not have the delta-V capability to launch from Earth, reach low-lunar orbit, and return to Earth for splashdown in the Pacific Ocean. But no one had seemed prepared to fix this problem before. “Getting Orion to low-lunar orbit means solving propulsion and spacecraft integration problems that were left unaddressed for far too long,” Isaacman said Friday. Instead, NASA created workarounds like the Lunar Gateway, which was put in a near-rectilinear halo orbit that Orion could safely reach. The burden of performance to reach the lunar surface was pushed on to the lunar landers and the companies developing them. Given the urgency of the situation, NASA has been trying to help lunar lander companies in any way they can, Isaacman said. “We’re listening to the data, and looking at what’s happening around us,” he said. “What progress are our landing partners making? Where are they struggling? What can we do to be helpful? Their lives get easier if we can get Orion closer to the Moon, substantially. We could choose to continue on our track and say, maybe they’ll show up and meet our timelines. Or we can say, well let’s start turning those knobs to help.”

Hegseth warns prediction market bets warp military judgment, endanger troops

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-23

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth issued a memo warning military personnel against using nonpublic information to make bets on prediction markets, saying the wagers could give important clues to enemy countries. “Do Not Compromise Military Operations for Personal Financial Gain,” said the subject line of an October 20 memo published yesterday by CBS News. Hegseth’s memo was sent to senior Pentagon leadership, Combatant Command leaders, and Defense Department field activity directors. Our enemies watch movements in these markets. When an American Service member, intelligence professional, or other Department civilian engages in a financial transaction on a military outcome, they create a signal. That signal can be aggregated, correlated, and exploited by hostile foreign intelligence services to anticipate our movements, targets, and timing. The lives of our Service members often depend on the enemy not knowing what is coming. The men and women who carry our flag into harm’s way deserve a Force that protects them—not one that exposes them to the enemy for personal gain. In April, a US Army soldier was arrested and charged with using confidential information to make $410,000 in Polymarket bets on the timing of the military’s capture of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro. An August report by the Anti-Corruption Data Collective said the group found 152 suspicious wallets that placed longshot bets on military and defense markets and won a combined $8 million. Hegseth’s memo said the prohibition applies to “all financial platforms,” including traditional betting services and prediction markets such as Polymarket and Kalshi. Defense Department personnel may not make any financial transaction informed by nonpublic information, regardless of whether that information is classified or unclassified, Hegseth wrote. “All Department of War personnel are prohibited from engaging in financial transactions using nonpublic information, either directly or indirectly through a third party… Violations may result in administrative, disciplinary, and, where appropriate, criminal action under the Uniform Code of Military Justice and/or applicable federal law,” Hegseth wrote. The memo said betting on military matters related to a person’s job can warp one’s judgment. “Beyond operational security, financial stakes can warp judgment,” he wrote. “A logistician with money riding on a strike date has an interest in that date. An analyst with a position on an outcome has an interest in that outcome.” Kalshi and Polymarket both prohibit insider trading. But criminal charges of the sort described by Hegseth are sure to be a bigger deterrent than being banned from the Kalshi or Polymarket platforms. This was not the first warning issued internally by the Trump administration, The Associated Press wrote yesterday. “A group of new accounts on Polymarket made highly specific, well-timed bets on whether the US and Iran would reach a ceasefire just before President Donald Trump announced it in April, resulting in hundreds of thousands of dollars in profits for the new customers,” the AP wrote. “The White House warned staff against using private information to trade on prediction markets the same day the report came out.” In another incident, a Trump teleprompter aide reportedly made $100,000 on Kalshi by betting on what Trump would say in prepared remarks. Prediction markets have flourished under the Trump administration’s light-touch approach to regulation. The Trump administration is also trying to stop state governments from regulating the markets, arguing that state laws are preempted by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission’s exclusive jurisdiction. Courts have reached different conclusions, and state governments want the Supreme Court to uphold their right to impose restrictions on prediction-market gambling.

Using Google’s products and services has always been fundamentally precarious—doing so gives the world’s largest advertising company a lot of juicy personal data. Google has long said it won’t use the sensitive data from products like Gmail, Drive, and Photos for ad personalization, but a change to the company’s privacy policy appears to dismantle that firewall. On October 16, Google issued an update to its privacy policy, spotted by Android Authority. The ad personalization section used to include a clear limit on data from certain apps. “We don’t show you personalized ads based on your content from Drive, Gmail, or Photos,” the document said. Now, Google has updated it with the following language. This equivocation opens the door to using data from apps like Gmail and Photos in ads when it’s shared with other Google services, and it just so happens Google has a mechanism for doing that, with Personal Intelligence. The company released Personal Intelligence in early 2026, allowing users to hand data from multiple apps to Google AI products. The idea is that the added context from your existing data will lead to better AI answers, but it’s also proving to be a privacy minefield. Personal Intelligence connects your data to the Gemini app but also to features like AI Mode search. While the Gemini app does not currently have ads, you may see sponsored content in AI search tools. Google’s changes to the privacy policy appear to acknowledge that data shared with AI search via Personal Intelligence could, therefore, contribute to ad personalization. According to Google, this clarification still doesn’t mean ads are “based directly” on your personal data. Here’s the company’s full statement: “Connecting your apps is entirely opt-in. Ads will continue to be relevant to things like your query, the context of the response and your interests. Ads will not be based directly on your Gmail inbox or Photos library, and as always, sensitive information will not be used to tailor ads.” Google’s statement is essentially a roundabout way of confirming that Workspace and Photos data may inform ads. If you interact with AI Mode while Personal Intelligence is enabled, there’s a chance that summaries and insights from your data will inform the answers you get. Because that same data is used for ad targeting, it’s accurate to say that your personal data is an element of ad targeting in AI search. The privacy policy now makes that clear. A Google spokesperson tells Ars that its privacy clarification does not indicate a change in how it handles your data with respect to its advertising partners. It’s not scanning your entire inbox to enhance your advertising profile, and it’s not giving any of this data to third parties. But if you pipe personal data into an AI search tool, the ads it shows you could be based on that information. The core of the problem is that Google wants its products and services to talk to each other. Google sees that wealth of data as a key strength of its AI efforts. Some apps like Gmail are still given protected status in Google’s data handling, but there are now more avenues for that data to get out. The more data you share across Google’s ecosystem, the greater the risk to your privacy. We’ve seen a similar exception in the way Google uses your AI interactions for model training. While Google says it doesn’t use personal data for AI training, it does use your inputs and the model outputs for that purpose. With Gemini integrated with so many services and connected to them via Personal Intelligence, those outputs may very well contain elements of your personal information. Google has said it tries to filter that content from the user activity controls, therefore keeping it out of training sets. However, it doesn’t promise it will always be successful. Google is correct that Personal Intelligence is opt-in, but the company uses various prompts and pop-ups to entice users to turn it on without fully explaining the consequences. You may have turned it on without realizing. If you want to ensure ads never use your personal data in this way, you’ll need to make sure Personal Intelligence is turned off. The easiest way is to access the Gemini settings for Personal Intelligence and disable the connected apps. These settings are also a few levels deep in your main Google account settings under Data & privacy > Personalization settings > Manage Personalized Recommendations in Search Services > Connected Content Apps. From there, you can disable connections to Workspace and Google Photos.

Leaders in the US Space Force would like to have a new spaceport up and running in four years, adding a third heavy-lift launch site to relieve pressure from bases in Florida and California that have shot rockets into orbit for nearly 70 years. The obvious problem with that ambition is 2030 is four years away, and Pentagon officials have not determined where the new spaceport should go or how to acquire land for it. Air Force Secretary Troy Meink first mentioned the service’s search for a third launch base in May. A congressionally mandated study of the Space Force’s launch infrastructure identified the need for “another site that’s capable of heavy and super heavy launch capability,” Meink said in a House Armed Services Committee hearing. The Trump administration released a space transportation policy in August that endorsed the construction of more launch and reentry sites for commercial and military missions. This week, the Space Force general responsible for procuring launch services for the military laid out an “aspirational” timeline for a new launch site. “We need this capability by 2030, but I’m also a pragmatist,” said Lt. Gen. Philip Garrant, head of Space Systems Command. “That is not an inconsequential demand to build a spaceport when you factor in orbital regimes, safety, access to commodities, the Americans that live near it. In many ways, it’s much like an airport, but even louder. So there’s a lot of consideration.” But there’s no firm plan yet for when the military might get moving on actually setting up a new launch base. “It’s going to take a strong partnership with local and state governments,” Garrant said. “As far as decision timelines, we don’t have anything yet.” There were 175 orbital launch attempts last year from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station and Kennedy Space Center in Florida and Vandenberg Space Force Base in California. Space Force officials project the country’s established spaceports will need to support 700 launches per year by 2036. A third launch site would offload some of the launch demand from limited real estate at Cape Canaveral and Vandenberg. It would also provide what Space Force leaders call “geographic resiliency” to mitigate the risk of a natural disaster or military attack knocking the Cape or Vandenberg offline. The federal government already owns a third spaceport at NASA’s Wallops Flight Facility in Virginia, used for suborbital rocket tests and small- and medium-class satellite launches. Small rockets can also launch from Pacific Spaceport Complex, owned by the state of Alaska. There are also commercial options that might be available for a new military spaceport. Ars asked Garrant if the Space Force might consider leasing property from a privately owned launch base, such as the expansive spaceport SpaceX plans to build in southern Louisiana. “We’ve discussed that we need to consider all of those opportunities: public-private partnership, new federal installation, state land, leveraging commercial land—all of those are possibilities. They all have pros and cons. We haven’t made that decision yet, but we’re not discounting any of those options.”

Rakuten Medical, the biotech offshoot of Japanese tech conglomerate Rakuten, is putting about 95% of the company’s resources into cracking the U.S. market. The San Diego-headquartered firm is already using its signature photoimmunotherapy treatment to treat head and neck cancers in Japan; it’s now running Phase 3 clinical trials in the U.S. and gunning for a 2028 FDA submission. “The U.S. is the world’s biggest market, and it’s also where a lot of capital is deployed,” Maeda tells Fortune. “We want to be able to treat all solid tumors, rather than stop at just head and neck cancers—that means we need significant capital to be invested into R&D.” Rakuten Medical’s core technology has both Japanese and U.S. ties. In 2011, Japanese scientist Hisataka Kobayashi and his team at the U.S. National Cancer Institute accidentally discovered the therapeutic potential of the new technology. “Kobayashi was trying to use photoimmunotherapy to visualize cancer cells… it wasn’t for treatment,” Maeda explains. “But when they were doing an experiment, they realized that cancers were disappearing, and eventually attempted to use it for therapeutic reasons.” Today, Rakuten Medical produces both a proprietary cancer drug (Akalux) and a technology platform (Alluminox) used to administer photoimmunotherapy. Their clinical pitch is specificity: the Akalux drug binds to the epidermal growth factor receptor on cancer cells, and tags it with light-activated dye. Red light is then applied to selectively exterminate the tagged cancer cells. In Maeda’s telling, this helps to treat the disease in a minimally invasive way, while preserving patients’ quality of life. “In 10 years’ time, I hope everybody believes that this technology could be the fifth pillar of cancer treatment, aside from surgery, radiation, chemotherapy and immunotherapy.” The razors-and-blades model, but for drugs Doctors didn’t embrace the drug-device combination right away. “It’s not a refusal; it’s a challenge for the doctors to understand: why am I not going to do surgery?” Maeda says. The firm’s profits, Maeda explains, come mostly from sales of the Akalux drug. It’s a common business strategy: Offer the platform for free or at low margins, then sell consumables at a higher margin. Think razor blades or printer ink. “If your company sells both printers and ink, you can’t sell both at very high prices, since the printer is an enabler,” Maeda says. Maeda said that Rakuten Medical’s Japan branch is profitable, though the global entity operates at a loss due to high research spending. In the short-term, Maeda is looking to enter markets close to home—including Taiwan, Macau, Singapore, Hong Kong and Saudi Arabia—which acknowledge Japanese regulatory approval of the Akalux drug. From discovering new books and organizing your reading list to tracking your reading goal and sharing recommendations, book apps make it easier to maintain reading as a hobby. There are numerous apps on the market that promise to do just that, so we’ve compiled a list of some of the best ones to help you pick the right one for you. Whether you’re an occasional reader or a dedicated bookworm, these apps can help you make the most of your reading journey. Some of the apps on this list are somewhat new, while others have been around for a few years. Amazon-owned Goodreads, which is arguably the most popular book-related app, isn’t included in this list because we’re focusing on newer, lesser-known platforms. Fable is a reading app that combines book tracking with a social platform. It lets you organize reading lists, set reading goals, track streaks, write reviews, and join virtual book clubs. You also get personalized book recommendations and the chance to discuss what you’re reading with other readers. The app differs from others on this list because it puts reading and community at the forefront of the platform, which makes it a great option for people who like staying motivated to read by being part of a community and discussing their reads. Fable is free to download on iOS and Android, but also offers a Fable Plus subscription tier that unlocks additional features like custom goals and advanced analytics for a monthly fee of $5.99. Bookshelf is a reading app designed to help people organize their libraries, discover new books, and remember more of what they read. The app positions itself as an alternative to Goodreads, and combines traditional reading logs with AI-powered recommendations and tools. You can create custom shelves, set reading goals, receive reminders, save favorite quotes, and access AI-generated book summaries, key insights, and thematic analyses to recall the books you’ve read and get a deeper understanding of them. Bookshelf is free to download on iOS, but offers a $6.99 monthly fee to unlock premium features like unlimited collections and enhanced analytics. Pagebound is a book app that lets readers track their books, create lists, set goals, and discuss individual titles in dedicated forums. Instead of having to join an actual book club group, users can post reactions and thoughts to connect with others reading the same title. You don’t have to worry about spoilers for the end of the book, as conversations are organized based on reading progress, allowing readers to share theories and thoughts as they read. The app is a great option for people who want to discuss their reads with others without having to commit to a scheduled book club. Pagebound is free to download on iOS and Android and offers a $9.99 monthly subscription for added features like profile customization. StoryGraph is a reading app that lets users track their reading, discover new books, and access detailed reading statistics and personalized recommendations. Users can set reading goals, join reading challenges, and find new titles based on their preferred moods, themes, genres, and pacing. The app is ideal for readers who want to better understand their reading habits and discover books that match their current interests. Its recommendation system doesn’t solely rely on what’s popular or trending, and instead caters to exactly what readers are looking for. StoryGraph is free to use on iOS and Android, but also offers a paid subscription called StoryGraph Plus that costs $4.99 per month and unlocks custom charts, additional statistics, and more. Margins is a great option for mood readers, as it offers mood-based discovery alongside a more focused reading experience. It helps users find their next read based on how they feel in the moment by searching for specific vibes, such as “books like 1920s Paris.” Users can also track their reading goals. The app also lets users customize their virtual library by choosing book covers that match their aesthetic and organizing their shelves in different ways, including by color. For users who enjoy a more immersive experience, Margins offers a built-in “Reading Time” meditation timer that blocks social media apps. Margins is free to download on iOS and Android. It’s hard for books about technology to age well. They always risk becoming outdated as companies rise and fall, and as one hot new product is replaced by the next. But last year, “Nerd Reich” author Gil Durán declared that it was time to revive the career of Paulina Borsook, who he said “saw it all very clearly and tried to warn us 25 years ago.” Later that fall, The New York Times reported that Borsook’s “Cyberselfish” — first published in 2000 — was finding new fans who want to understand why the tech industry “lost its way.” Now, long after going out of print, “Cyberselfish” is returning in a new edition from Tin House. Borsook told me in a Zoom interview that the book is meant to be “almost a travelogue” of her attempts to understand what she calls the techno-libertarian influence on Silicon Valley culture. “That’s a 12-year-old view of the world,” Borsook said, describing it as “a kind of basket of beliefs” that’s “violently anti-regulation,” despite the fact that “no sector of society had benefited more and suffered less from the government than these guys.” In her telling, “even after the dot-com crash, that attitude was just there, percolating around, contaminating the aquifers. It was uncorrected, and it just spread out more and more.” Borsook has yet to publish a second book, and she said that after “Cyberselfish” was first released, “no one was interested in anything I might have to say about anything, tech or not tech, forever after.” But last year, she began to notice that prices for used copies were beginning to creep up, and she was starting to see “very out-of-the-blue, I-did-not-know-these-people posts on social media about how important this book was,” as well as inquiries from “European academics,” eventually leading to more press coverage and the new Tin House edition, with a new introduction by Durán. Keep reading for our full conversation, edited for length and clarity, in which Borsook discusses AI, how Silicon Valley has transformed in the past 25 years, and what advice she has for young people seeking a career in tech. Do you want to talk a little bit about how your interest in techno-libertarianism started, and how you decided that there was a book in this? Everything with me is always inadvertent. I started knocking around tech circles, however we define it, in the early-middle ‘80s, in the Bay Area. And then I would say, starting around maybe the early ‘90s, I would hear these — to me — very weird, offhand remarks that some people would make that I couldn’t make any sense out of. It’s not my nature to argue with people. It’s my nature to listen, and so I didn’t have the term for it except libertarian. But it seemed very screwy because initially, I grew up in Pasadena, which was [the home of the] Caltech-JPL space program, and so the parents of the kids I knew were another STEM culture. When I first got into Silicon Valley, I thought, “Oh, this is just another STEM culture like I’m familiar with.” And those guys [at Caltech and JPL]  had a very clear sense of what the government can do for you, were not into enlightened self-interest. It was a different personality type, and they certainly didn’t self-anoint with how glorious and grand and magisterial they were, even though they were doing great things. I was just listening, I guess, and being confused. Even then, I knew no sector of society had benefited more and suffered less from the government than these guys. So why are they acting like this? So, backstory: My agent picked me up right after my MFA in Columbia, and he said to me, “You know, it’s really hard to get fiction published. Do you have a nonfiction book in you?” And I said, “Well, there’s this weird subculture that I’ve been observing that I don’t understand, and any time I don’t understand something, I want to explore it.” That got me down the rabbit hole of looking at this stuff and writing about it, and I consider it really almost a travelogue, in the sense of “Black Lamb and Gray Falcon” or that kind of stuff, almost more than, “This is a polemic.” It’s more like: snapshot, snapshot, snapshot. I just fell into it, and also I’m sufficiently narcissistic that I felt I was uniquely qualified to write about this, and no one else was paying any attention, and that was very important to me. I haven’t done a backread of all the other books written about the tech industry before “Cyberselfish.” My sense is that you do recognize, say, reading Steven Levy’s work that there is this alternative philosophy, libertarian streak running through Silicon Valley. But no one really pulled it out and put it under the microscope in that way. That’s really true. And then most of the writing — certainly in the ’90s, but maybe later — was very celebratory. It wasn’t, “Wait a minute, what’s going on here?” I heard [New York Times reporter] John Markoff give a talk about six months ago, and he said something I really liked. He said, “Silicon Valley is a multicultural place.” By which he didn’t mean your country of origin. What he meant is there’s all these different kinds of cultures in there, and he was always more focused on these countercultural [elements,] that kind of thing. I’m never saying that wasn’t there. I’m just saying there was this other thing that I was more interested in. So they’re running in parallel. I don’t even think they’re in conflict. It sounds like you don’t see the techno-libertarian side of it emerging from the counterculture, but rather [as] distinct cultures within Silicon Valley. One of the distinctions I made in — I call it TDB, that damn book — is that distinction I made between Ravers and Gilders, and the Gilders being the social conservatives. I named them after George Gilder, who was this repellent thought leader of the early ’90s, very conservative, very reactionary, very backward-looking. And then the Ravers, in homage to John Perry Barlow, drugs and sex and rock and roll, “we’re going to Burning Man,” much more culturally liberal. And that distinction has held up, because you have that in contemporary Silicon Valley. You have these Christian nationalists and conservative Catholics in positions of leadership, and then you also have the Burners and people having interesting experimentations with sexuality and drugs and the [effective altruist] and AI worlds. And so, what’s the counterculture bit of it? Maybe the personal freedom aspect of it is countercultural. Another weird thing we always have to point out is that the [San Francisco] peninsula votes blue, still. Ro Khanna is the congressman from there. So I’m really talking about culture rather than electoral politics. [And I’m talking about] the leadership class here, the tech overlords. It’s not so clear to me how much the rank and file are like this, though probably more than when I was more familiar with what was going on there. Because I think a lot of people have been attracted to work in crypto or AI, and they’re not thinking about the greater political surround. But they’ll drink whatever they’re handed, and they don’t much care. We can point to people who are in charge, who have these [right-wing] beliefs or are allied with the Trump administration. There’s another executive class that maybe doesn’t have exactly the same beliefs, but is able to make themselves very comfortable with it. And then you have that same diversity that you were talking about. There’s different communities, different cultures underneath. I’ve certainly spoken to people who work for someone like Elon Musk, and they’re like, “Yeah, I don’t love this guy, but right now, I can’t afford to lose this job.” Right, I think there’s a lot more of that going on than in times past, because there’s greater precarity in terms of employment, and so people [say,] “I’ve got a job, I’m going to hold on to it.” […] Or, “I don’t espouse the beliefs that they’re giving out, but my project is okay.” Those kinds of things always go on. For you folks who aren’t familiar with the book and maybe have a general sense that there is a libertarian strain in Silicon Valley, can you say more why — if somebody thinks, “I believe in technology being liberatory, and maybe it can free us from the shackles of government bureaucracy and things like that,” why is techno-libertarianism a bad thing? First of all, that’s a 12-year-old view of the world. I didn’t really have a good word, but it was a kind of basket of beliefs, and now we’d say maybe a cloud, and it was violently anti-regulation. It was bad borrowing from biology. “Let’s just let things evolve and they will be fine.” They have no idea how much government has subsidized and made possible their life, their world, all the R&D. Government can do bad things. I’m an old hippie. I know about the war in Vietnam. I know what the CIA did with Allende. So I don’t want to come across [as if] I think it’s all okay. I don’t. But this sector of society has less to complain about. And one thing that just makes me grit my teeth right now is a lot of these guys are moving to Nevada to live on the Nevada side of Tahoe, because they want to escape California taxes. Sergey [Brin] saying, “California is turning into Soviet Russia.” Are you nuts? Because the thing is, they all love Tahoe. Why do they think the water of Tahoe is so pure and blue? That is decades of government intervention and interference, environmental scientists, regulation. I mean, there’s this inability to connect the dots and lack of history. To what extent is what we’re seeing now with the tech industry, with their alignment with the Trump administration, with all these other dystopian things going on, how much of that is a shift? And to what extent is this all one story, continuous with what you were seeing before? Unfortunately, I see more the continuity. Excuse me for [my own] bad borrowing from biology, but […] I really see the Netscape IPO is when things really began to change, and Silicon Valley was invaded by the finance bros, and a real change in attitude. You got the culture of flip and flee, all that kind of stuff. Even after the dot-com crash, that attitude was just there, percolating around, contaminating the aquifers. It was uncorrected, and it just spread out more and more. When you have things like Airbnb or Uber, which are strictly financial plays, they’re not technology innovation by my definition, but there was too much investment money in the world that didn’t know where to go. It would invest in these things, and these people would parrot this language. So I thought, “Ah, this is left over from 15 years ago,” in terms of their self-justification. And the way that something like Airbnb fought so hard against any kind of regulation and anything that would ask them to be any kind of a good community actor in the communities they basically destroyed, in a lot of ways, in terms of the housing market. It’s like, “Yeah, that’s that attitude I was talking about way back then.” It’s like Giardia in the water system. You can say, with good reason, that there was a lot of wonderful stuff that came out, and all the children of General Magic and all the great things that they created. I would never say that’s not true, but I’m not talking about that. I’m talking about the business culture of these people, how they see themselves. Sociopathy is a business virtue in a way that was never there before. How do you see the rise of AI through that lens? It’s perfect. “We don’t need to be regulated. We’re the most brilliant, genius, wonderful people in the world.” I see AI right now as a big Ponzi scheme. You probably saw that really horrible ad that Apple did a couple of years ago, for one of their new computers, and they had all the musical instruments and all writing and [it all went into] the hydraulic press. How did Apple become so tone deaf? This is not the Apple that all the quote “creative class” loved for so many years. It’s not the Apple of the 1984 ad. But they still see themselves in this liberatory way. If you buy the stockholder theory of value, it justifies whatever the AI companies are doing because of the stock play, not because it’s intrinsically valuable. In the last couple of years, [“Cyberselfish”] has had this renaissance, leading up to the current reissue. When was that something that you started to notice? This was maybe an exercise in masochism, but a couple of times a year, I’d look at the used book platforms, and it was selling for three bucks, which means that the seller is making more money on the shipping and handling than on the book, which is really humiliating. I would say starting maybe a year and a half ago, I began to notice the prices are creeping up — not hugely, initially, but they were creeping up. And also, I was beginning to be contacted by European academics who wanted to talk to me. And I thought, “How did you even hear about this?” And then there were some very out-of-the-blue, I-did-not-know-these-people posts on social media about how important this book was, it shouldn’t have been forgotten, this is wrong. Long story short, I got led to Gil Durán, who wrote “The Nerd Reich,” and then we went for lunch, and we realized we are kindred spirits. Around that same time, Steven Levy, who was working on a piece for Wired about “How did I get it so wrong?” — and of course, I’m sitting here thinking, “Yeah, Steven, how did you?” —  Steven contacted Gil and talked to him for 45 minutes, and Gil said, “You should really talk to Paulina, she’s been tracking this stuff forever.” So Steven never quoted Gil. He never contacted me. And when Steven’s piece came Gil put this incredible rant on Bluesky saying I got everything right. “We really need to support her. Let’s try to bring this book back into print.” And between that and the podcast I did with him, all of a sudden, you could not get a copy of TDB for love or money. [In the new edition,] there’s the new introduction by Gil, but otherwise it’s the book as it appeared [in 2000.] Absolutely, and then I wrote a little introduction, going into more, “This is how this came back from the dead.” The acquiring editor at Tin House, she said to me, “Don’t change a word,” which is exactly what I wanted to hear. Because I think it’s an artifact of a time and place. And with a book like this, if you start thinking, “Well, should we update this? Should we add to that?” it turns into a different book. People were glomming on to the book as it was. They weren’t saying, “Oh, it would be great if we updated this.” So, no, it’s not updated. Hopefully, it’s not naive of me to imagine that there are people who might read this interview, read your book, and they are mostly on-board with what you’re saying about the tech industry and techno-libertarianism. Maybe they work at some big tech company [or] they have a startup themselves, and they’re like, “I don’t agree with the Peter Thiels and Elon Musks of the world.” Do you think that those people can reform or change the tech industry from within? I want it to be true, because one of the questions that I’m getting these days, and I wish I could answer it better, is running into young people, they’re in CS or engineering or something, and they want a career in that, and they don’t buy this toxic stuff. “What can we do?” And I go, “I’m not sure, because first of all, AI is sucking up all the investment money right now. So if you want to do almost anything, if it’s not AI-related, you can’t do it. Or a military-industrial thing. Government is so broke right now. Can you get a job in government? I’m not sure. Even the state governments, or I’d say the nonprofit sector, but that’s not doing well, either.” I have this frantic thing of, I adore these kids and I want to support them and I don’t know what to tell them to do. It used to be that you could start a small company, a small consultancy, just you and a friend or two, and it didn’t have to elephantise as a grotesque growth, you could just bootstrap, but the revenue supported the growth, and you could do that. I’m not too sure how much of that world still exists. I think during the app era, you could also do that. I’m not sure now, but I never want to underestimate the both desperation and ingenuity of people, and so it may be possible. I think your larger question — which is, do I think these big companies can be reformed from within — I think all it takes is a change. They’re amoral enough that if it’s not in their interest to link arms with the MAGA thing, they’ll do something different. I’m very cynical about it. But I think the people who might be thinking about what used to be called appropriate technology, I’d love to see those people get funded or find a company that does that. It could be possible — whether they can do it within the venue of the hyperscalers, I don’t know, but I would hope that they could spin off and maybe do something else. U.S. federal safety regulators continue to tackle the sticky problem of autonomous vehicles, this time with a proposal that could signal to the public, quite literally, when a vehicle is operating autonomously. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration has proposed new lighting requirements for autonomous vehicles that include an option (dare I say a strong suggestion?) to use cyan-colored lights to signal when a vehicle is operating autonomously without a human occupant monitoring the road. The agency is not proposing a mandate for blue-green lights. Importantly, under the proposal, the lights would be used when an AV is operating in autonomous mode. Still, federal regulators are clearly seeking some uniform way of identifying autonomous vehicles. The proposal establishes equipment specifications, including where the lights are mounted, the color they emit, and how they’re activated. It would also remove requirements for manually operated controls for turn signals, high beams, and hazard lights. Under the proposal, the NHTSA suggests the lights be cyan (blue-green), remain steadily illuminated rather than flash, and be visible from the front, rear, and both sides. This latest proposal is one piece of a bigger effort to update regulations that help speed up the adoption of AVs while maintaining safety, according to NHTSA administrator Jonathan Morrison. The agency is already working on a federal automated vehicle framework and is trying to update federal motor vehicle safety standards, which already govern automakers today. For instance, FMVSS rules were not designed for vehicles like the Tesla Cybercab and Zoox robotaxi, which lack traditional, federally required equipment like pedals, steering wheels, and design features like side mirrors and rearview mirrors. In the eastern Indian state of Bihar, landlords used club-wielding enforcers known as lathaits to keep Dalits, members of the most oppressed caste, out of polling booths. “I remember watching members of my caste being dragged out of a polling booth,” an elderly Dalit man in Bihar’s Siwan district recalled in an interview with The Print, an Indian news site. During the state elections in 1967, Jagdish Master, a teacher turned activist, challenged the veto of the landlords over Dalit suffrage by escorting a group of Dalits to a polling booth in Ekwari, a village in Bihar’s Bhojpur district. The landlords’ henchmen brutally beat him and drove him out. He returned to the village as an armed guerrilla, recruiting Dalit laborers and other poor peasants in a Maoist-style rural insurgency that would flare across the region for decades. The power of the vote in Indian consciousness transformed radically after the “Emergency,” the period between June 1975 and March 1977 when Prime Minister Indira Gandhi suspended constitutional rights, assumed untrammeled powers, imprisoned opposition leaders and silenced the press. Much of India’s elite—bureaucrats, businessmen, newspaper editors and movie stars—bent the knee and welcomed Mrs. Gandhi’s promise of “order” and “discipline.” Wealth and connections offered protection from the state. India’s poor, stripped of their electoral power, suffered terrible abuses of an unaccountable bureaucracy and police from slum demolitions to forced sterilization of men. In the general elections held in March 1977 after the end of the Emergency, Mrs. Gandhi’s Congress Party was decisively defeated by the people and tossed out of power for the first time since Indian independence in 1947. The principal victims of abuses of the Emergency—Dalits, Muslims, and the poor of the populous northern Indian states—voted against the Congress party for the first time and contributed significantly to its removal from power The power of the vote has gradually forced India’s high-handed bureaucracy to recognize the human dignity and political citizenship of ordinary people. The electorate delivered its verdict on Mrs. Gandhi’s breach of the sacred covenant between the Indian state and its people: the people confer legitimacy on the state in exchange for their rights, including its protection of the sanctity of the vote. Having asserted their power, voters proved difficult to subdue again. As the political theorist Partha Chatterjee put it, it was the 1977 defeat of Mrs. Gandhi and the Congress party that established the “capacity of the vote.” India’s poor and marginalized took proper control of their voting choices three decades after independence. In the years after the Emergency, they leveraged the vote as a means to a secure livelihood or as a tool for ensuring justice. Politicians could no longer rely on local patrons to intimidate people into voting for them and had to compete on representing their interests. A new competitive politics emerged in the 1980s and led to an upsurge, as Chatterjee wrote, “of popular demands of a kind that had never before been allowed to disturb the order and tranquility of the proverbial corridors of power.” By the 1990s, political leaders from socially disenfranchised castes and Dalit communities were taking power in the populous, northern Indian states of Bihar and Uttar Pradesh. Political scientist Christophe Jaffrelot memorably described it as “the silent revolution.” This revolution, ushered in by the votes of the historically oppressed communities, did not quite transform the economic and social structures of the region. But it changed the everyday relations of power. Indrajit Roy, a scholar of development studies, recalled a refrain he heard frequently during his fieldwork among Dalits in Bihar in the late 2000s: “There has been development, change has come. Today, we can look the landlord in the eye and speak as equals.” Vote banks had thus turned into vehicles of collective political assertion. Marginalized communities had become adept at pooling their votes to secure political favors and advance their shared interests. They chose their own patrons, preferring their own caste leaders to represent their interests and negotiate with the state on their behalf. Over time, the clientelist politics of vote banks became associated with an entrenched culture of corruption. This helped pave the way for Modi’s successful national campaign in 2014, in which he cast himself as a populist strongman who embodied the aspirations for “development” of all Indians while portraying the Congress and regional parties as beholden to their respective vote banks. Modi’s attacks on vote bank politics also carried a more sinister charge: that successive governments had appeased India’s Muslims minority, which accounts for around 14% of the population, for their votes at the expense of the national interest. Bangladesh borders the Indian state of West Bengal and Modi reserved his sharpest, xenophobic barbs for Bengal’s Muslims, whom he tarred during his 2014 campaign as “Bangladeshi” and “illegal immigrants,” establishing a template for their vilification that persists to this day. In an inflammatory campaign speech, Modi issued an ominous warning: “After May 16 [the day of the results of the 2014 elections], these Bangladeshis better be prepared with their bags packed.” The Modi government invoked that very specter of the “illegal, Bangladeshi Muslim” to push through revisions to electoral rolls across most Indian states. India’s home minister, Amit Shah, who had earlier likened Bangladeshi immigrants to “termites,” responded to the ongoing protests by calling them an “action plan to protect infiltrators.” There are no reliable estimates of how many Bangladeshi immigrants live in India. In 2016, a minister of the Modi government told the parliament, without citing a source, that the country was home to some 20 million “illegal immigrants from Bangladesh.” The government later acknowledged that it had no accurate data on the number of illegal immigrants in India. Yet the Indian government has used unfounded speculation about “illegal” Bangladeshis to overturn the fundamental logic of the country’s electoral registration system, shifting the burden of ensuring registration from the Election Commission to the voter herself. The commission was responsible for ensuring that every citizen made it onto the electoral rolls. The exercise has already disproportionately disenfranchised the poor, migrants, religious minorities, women—and is bound to continue doing so. “The majority of voters in Bihar who were excluded from the list were in rural areas,” said retired Maj. Gen. Anil Verma, who heads the Association for Democratic Reforms, a nonprofit group that challenged the electoral rolls revisions in the Supreme Court. “Those worst affected were the poor and the marginalized—they had no documents.” Opposition parties have also alleged that the electoral roll revisions tilted the electoral field in the BJP’s favor in the West Bengal election held earlier this year, helping the party win power in the state for the first time. An analysis by the SABAR Institute, a public policy research institute, lent some credence to that charge: a disproportionate share of the 9.08 million voters removed from West Bengal’s electoral rolls came from constituencies held by the then-ruling Trinamool Congress or those with large Muslim populations. The Election Commission has denied the charge that the deletions were politically motivated, arguing that it applied uniform criteria to remove entries for voters who had died, were registered more than once or had permanently relocated. But that claim is difficult to reconcile with the disproportionate representation  of certain  groups among those deleted from supplementary lists issued for some constituencies after an adjudication process. For instance, although Muslims make up around 23% of the population of Nandigram, a constituency in West Bengal, the SABAR Institute study found that they accounted for 95.5% of the deletions across seven supplementary lists released by the Election Commission for the constituency. Voting carries an almost existential significance for Indians whose equal citizenship has not been fully recognized by the institutions of the state. Indians see the vote as an affirmation of their existence. If the vote is no longer a gift freely given or withdrawn by the people but a resource controlled by the state, it reduces ordinary voters once again to supplicants dependent on the favor of political elites. The electoral revisions and mass deletion of voters have provoked such widespread revulsion because many Indians see it as an assault on their identity as citizens and a threat to the social recognition they have won through a long, arduous struggle. Many Indians might have forgiven Modi for his numerous missteps but they are determined not to let him devalue their voting power. The young protesters moving through Delhi’s barricaded streets carry a palpable sense that they are living through a historical moment. References to the Quit India movement against the country’s erstwhile British colonizers and the protests around the Emergency have become commonplace. Memories of seminal events of the past offer a lens through which to consider the present. The future seems open to change through collective action. This sense of a turning point imbues individual actions with a larger purpose and drives protesters to confront a police force intent on clearing the streets by whatever means necessary. On Oct. 9, a day before Delhi was placed under a lockdown, Bora invoked India’s struggle against British imperialism to denounce the pre-emptive detention of protest organizers. “History is repeating itself. Just like in 1942, the arrest of leaders could not stop the Quit India movement; this movement will also not stop,” Bora said. “This is nothing but a state of open emergency in the country.” The convergence of young people’s anger over education and employment, fears of authoritarianism, and concern for the sanctity of the vote poses a serious challenge to the Modi government. It could forge a broad popular coalition of unemployed young people, urban poor, Dalits and religious minorities, united in challenging the legitimacy of the government. Unless the government retreats on electoral roll revisions, sustained protests could drain its political capital. One of Indian democracy’s great achievements is that even its poorest citizens have proved adept at recognizing when the power of their vote is being wrested away, gauging the consequences and organizing to resist. A government in India can retain its legitimacy despite failing to provide functioning schools or decent employment. It cannot do so if it also violates the sanctity of the vote. Under a recent settlement that Meta recently reached with states, Facebook and Instagram are about to look different for minors Among the new protections, one seemingly modest change caught my eye. By default, users under 18 will no longer be able to see likes and other reactions in response to their posts or those of others. The reforms are designed for children and teenagers, but touch on a psychological phenomenon just as relevant to adults. As a psychiatrist, I found myself wondering: Why does that little number beneath your post matter so much? The answer has less to do with social media and more to do with an ancient human tendency: social comparison. Social comparison is the psychological process by which people assess themselves, including their own abilities, opinions, and achievements, in relation to those of others. Likes can both reward us and rank us. They show us that people are validating our posts and also quantify that validation. We know how much approval we receive relative to what we might have expected, what we got the last time we posted, and what others receive. Feedback is scored. Likes are just one example. Follower counts, views, and shares are other metrics that make social information measurable. When social approval is numerically valued, comparison becomes harder to avoid. From an evolutionary perspective, social comparison may have served an adaptive purpose. Humans evolved in social groups. Knowing where you stood relative to others might have helped you identify what behaviors were approved, whether you belonged, and how to navigate hierarchy. But moving beyond prehistoric time to the last century, comparison still had constraints. Your 25-year high school reunion? It happened once in your lifetime, maybe in a city far away from you, with only your fellow classmates in attendance. Now, the high school reunion lives in the device in our hands, capable of giving anyone quantitative signals from a vast reference group at any moment on our personalized scoreboards of social approval. If the subjective question is whether we belong, what can feel like an objective answer is the 100 likes our post gets from 2,500 followers. The scale, frequency, quantification, and accessibility of social comparison have all changed. Our brains weren’t built for this much comparison. Neuroimaging studies show that our brains allocate value to how we believe we stack up next to others. Downward social comparison, in which we learn someone performs worse than us, can engage neural circuits associated with reward and valuation. Upward comparison, in which we learn someone performs better than us, can engage neural circuits associated with processing unpleasant emotions. These are ways in which our brains process social outcomes much like other kinds of gains or losses. But our brains don’t just register numbers; we also tend to evaluate their meaning. In one study, likes functioned as a type of currency that adult users compared themselves on, shaping how they felt in that moment. In another set of studies, adults who made more frequent and extreme upward comparisons reported an immediate decline in self-evaluation and cumulative declines in self-esteem and life satisfaction over a browsing session. Research has also found that across generational groups, greater user tendency to engage in social comparison, together with greater maladaptive ways of coping with emotions, is associated with more symptoms of anger, anxiety, and depression. Social comparison isn’t inherently harmful. But as repeated opportunities to compare mount, the mental health effects can add up. While hiding likes may not eliminate social comparison, it does remove a metric from the scoreboard. The instinct to compare or desire to belong won’t just vanish. But even if we might wonder where we stand next to who’s performing better, there’s a difference between wondering and a number answering that question for us. Social media didn’t invent our instinct to compare ourselves to others. We’ve had that for millennia. What it did was shift the environment surrounding our instincts, encoding one form of social feedback into numbers we can monitor. That's why changing our relationship with social comparison will mean more than adapting digital platforms. What do we value? What gives us purpose? What makes our lives matter? The cultural shift that lies ahead is to ask the questions about our social worth that don’t need a scoreboard to answer. With just days to go before November’s midterms, the Department of Justice ended this week by launching its most desperate and unprofessional assaults on the American election system yet. On Thursday, the DOJ filed an absurd lawsuit seeking to block a declaration of a winner in Alaska’s U.S. Senate race until the courts relitigate the eligibility of one of its candidates. This comes the same day as an audacious DOJ effort to preemptively push a California election case to higher courts before lower courts have even had a chance to hold a hearing. The executive branch’s continued election interference has not been deterred by going zero for 26 in cases seeking unredacted state voter lists and losing badly at the Supreme Court in a case that would have shut down mail balloting this election. From Alaska to California and who knows where else, DOJ has been filing cases to interfere with how states count and certify votes. The agency’s latest moves—as factually unsupported as they are legally vacuous—demonstrate that for all the fear about DOJ coming in with guns blazing to try to overturn the results of the elections, it is most likely to be shooting blanks. The main reason that DOJ has been losing election-related lawsuits is that the Constitution does not give the president or the executive branch a role to play in running elections. That matter is left to the states, subject to congressional legislation that can alter the rules in federal elections. So when President Donald Trump issued a March 2025 executive order soon after coming back into office that would have changed the federal voter registration form (mandated by Congress) to require it be accompanied by documentary proof of citizenship like a birth certificate, a federal court shut it down. It ruled that, “Put simply, our Constitution does not allow the President to impose unilateral changes to federal election procedures.” And when the USPS followed Trump’s second executive order by announcing a rule that would have made the agency a federal gatekeeper over state mail ballots, the Supreme Court ruled that USPS was likely to lose on the merits because its muscling into state election processes exceeds its power. DOJ’s latest moves in California and Alaska are even weaker than DOJ’s earlier attempts to assert power over elections. In California, DOJ demanded that California and Los Angeles County go through a 20,000-person list of what it termed “likely” or “suspected” noncitizens (it vacillated on what to call them), take their mail-in ballots out of the pile, and segregate them so they won’t be counted. It first provided the information on a thumb drive with the password on a Post-it note. After California officials complained that using such a drive would breach security protocols—would you put a Trump-issued thumb drive into your PC?—they provided a spreadsheet. The spreadsheet appears to be wildly inaccurate; more than half from a sample of 200 looked at by the county were people not registered, and many were U.S.-born—a great indicator they are citizens. This is no surprise. As I recently recounted in Slate: When the Department of Homeland Security made unsupported claims that states were allowing thousands of noncitizens to remain on their voting rolls, state and local election administrators demanded evidence. After Nevada got the data and investigated, the DHS list of 16,000 potentially ineligible Nevada voters fell to zero. Even weaker than DOJ’s factual claims are its legal arguments. The sole basis that DOJ relied upon in seeking this extraordinary relief from California is a federal statute that makes it a crime for a noncitizen to vote in an election. That statute gives the federal government zero right to tell California what it can or cannot do in counting ballots, and indeed, another federal statute, the National Voter Registration Act of 1993, prohibits states from systematically removing ineligible voters in the 90 days before an election. That’s to prevent the inadvertent disenfranchisement of eligible voters. Undeterred by the legal and factual weakness of the case, DOJ asked a federal district court to issue a temporary restraining order to make Los Angeles County segregate out those ballots. TROs are not appealable, and the judge has not even ruled yet, so there is no order to appeal from.
