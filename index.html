Microsoft is beginning the rollout of a Windows 11 preview update that finally addresses a persistent power user complaint with the operating system: the right-click menu that obliterated years of muscle memory on command locations and hid others behind an additional click. The timing is notable, as the announcement comes alongside Microsoft’s quarterly financial report, which showed a continued decline in the business of selling Windows licenses for new PCs. The preview update, KB5127936, is cumulative for Windows 11 versions 26H2 and 25H2. The update will be a gradual rollout before eventually moving on to a normal rollout for all Windows 11 devices once it reaches general availability. The highlight of the new update should calm annoyed users who have spent years with custom scripts and services in an attempt to shape Windows the way they would prefer it. Users will get a “customize menu” button that controls what commands appear when they right-click their mouse. Users can toggle built-in commands such as Send To or Create Shortcut on or off, rearrange what appears on the main menu, and control which third-party extensions show up in either the main or submenus. The gripe with the right-click menu had been that Microsoft hid familiar command locations behind a top row of icons while moving other familiar commands under the “Show more options” submenu. Now, Windows users can customize the whole thing around their habits. The new preview also highlights a new Windows feature called Cloud Rebuild, a recovery option that can reinstall Windows 11 directly from the cloud, even when an existing Windows 11 fails to boot. Cloud Rebuild will pull the Windows 11 image and needed system drivers directly from Windows Update. Users will not need a custom recovery image or external media to reinstall Windows. There is an important distinction to note, however. Microsoft classifies Cloud Rebuild as a recovery option, but it behaves more like a fresh Windows install than a repair, as existing applications and files will not be included. Those worried about data security can sanitize entire system drives prior to reinstalling. For a full list of improvements coming to Windows 11, including changes to Xbox mode and settings, you can view the official Microsoft release announcement. Microsoft’s latest quarterly report, released this week, paints a less encouraging picture of its Windows OEM business. “Windows OEM revenue was below expectations and decreased 27% and 28% in constant currency, driven by the Windows 10 end of support demand in the prior year, inventory drawdowns by OEM and channel partners and weaker PC market demand,” said CFO Amy Hood, according to a transcript of the call. The broader Windows OEM and devices category, which includes Microsoft’s own Surface lineup, fell 22 percent, per the earnings release. Hood went on to state that “we expect these trends to continue through the rest of the fiscal year.” Recent hardware efforts like the Surface Laptop Ultra and broader RTX Spark platform arrived too late in the quarter to register in the results, and Microsoft forecast further drops before any possible boost from new hardware can show in its numbers. The news for Microsoft isn’t negative all around, though, as its Azure platform saw a 48 percent jump, and Microsoft Cloud revenue was also up 30 percent, year over year—now roughly 70 percent of the company’s $92.3 billion total quarterly revenue.

President Donald Trump has declared the imports of foreign-made factory robots to be a national security threat—ordering new US negotiations with trading partners while holding out the possibility of imposing new tariffs on such robot imports in the future. Issued on October 28, Trump’s presidential proclamation represents heightened government scrutiny of foreign-made industrial robots that could complicate the manufacturing operations of major US corporations that rely on such robots. Those companies include Detroit automakers General Motors and Ford, electric vehicle manufacturer Tesla, and aerospace and defense giants like Boeing and Lockheed Martin. Trump’s proclamation describes industrial robots and machinery as being crucial for producing US military weapon systems along with representing a “driving force” behind all critical infrastructure sectors within the US economy. The proclamation cites an investigation under US Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick as having found that US imports of such robots “pose[] risks to the United States industrial base and threatens the long-term competitiveness of the United States in a sector that is increasingly vital to national security.” The Department of Commerce investigation also supposedly found “unique security and safety risks” that may introduce “significant cybersecurity vulnerabilities that can result in severe consequences to the United States.” Such wording echoes justifications used by Trump’s Federal Communications Commission when it banned new models of foreign-made robots, such as robotic vacuums, four-legged robot dogs, and humanoid robots, in July 2026. But that FCC action had specifically exempted industrial robot arms and other fixed or stationary robots commonly deployed in automotive factories and logistics warehouses. Now Trump’s directive for the United States and trading partners “to adjust imports of robotics, industrial machinery, and their components” aims to strike new agreements on the issue within 180 days of the proclamation’s date. If no agreements have been reached by then, the proclamation warns that the president may take additional actions, like imposing “tariffs, duties, quotas, and fees” on such imports. Any tariffs or other fees would, of course, be paid by the US companies importing such foreign-made robots. That would likely increase the cost of US manufacturing operations at a time when the United States has become the second largest market in using industrial robots, with the International Federation of Robotics reporting nearly 38,500 new US installations of industrial robots in 2025. Major US automotive and aerospace companies often buy large robotic arms for factory floor work from the “big four” robotics makers—FANUC, ABB, Yaskawa, and KUKA—that are headquartered in Japan, Switzerland, and Germany. No US company currently offers comparable heavy-duty hardware for large industrial robotic arms, although the foreign robotics makers have been expanding their US engineering and assembly facilities. Even Amazon, the company that has arguably deployed more robot workers than any other in the world, still uses some foreign-made robots, like Japan’s FANUC robotic arms, despite having significantly ramped up its own in-house robot production and developed certain specialized robotic arms. Trump’s own proclamation acknowledges that US robotics companies do not produce enough industrial robots to “meet national security needs,” and that the United States “relies on foreign imports for most types of robotics and industrial machinery and for their components.” If Trump eventually slaps tariffs on US imports of foreign-made industrial robots, that would at least fall short of restricting imports outright. Although as Intel recently showed, major US companies can still get waivers from the Trump administration to continue using foreign-made robots even when a nominal ban is in place.

Turning a solar power system into its own backup

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-29

Visit Original Website
Rather than directly converting photons into electricity, concentrated solar energy uses mirrors to turn sunlight into heat, which can then be used to drive traditional generating turbines. Up to about a decade ago, there was a case to be made that concentrated solar energy had a bright future, as improvements in heat storage had turned it into a way to generate power around the clock. It wasn’t to be. While concentrated solar costs fell enough to compete with nuclear power, the price of photovoltaics plunged, making solar panels the cheapest source of power in most locations. Currently, if you have a good site to generate solar power, then the economics of PV panels are hard to beat. That has left us needing to build a mixture of other facilities—typically batteries and natural gas facilities—to handle overnight needs or weather-driven shortfalls. But a small company is promising that it can help avoid the need to build a separate facility. Its version of concentrated solar is designed so that a fossil-fueled backup system shares most of the generating hardware with the solar side. Concentrated solar, also called solar thermal, is pretty simple in principle. Most of the site is covered with heliostats, devices that incorporate mirrors and a motorized tracking system that can keep their focus on a single point as the Sun moves across the sky. The point of focus is in a tower, where sunlight heats a working fluid. The hot fluid can then be used to power a traditional steam turbine, or its heat can be stored and used to generate power overnight. There are many potential variations, however: different working fluids and methods of heat storage, trough-shaped mirrors that eliminate the need for a tower, and so on. All of those variants are meant to reduce some of the issues inherent to solar thermal: lots of moving parts, potentially corrosive fluids, and so on. Those issues and the lack of standardization have helped ensure that the costs of these plants haven’t come close to keeping pace with photovoltaics. Without any incentive to generate renewable power overnight, there hasn’t been any way for concentrated solar to compete. Construction of new plants has largely stopped, and a number of sources have simply stopped tracking the economics of it. So it was a bit of a surprise to hear that India had contracted with a US-based startup called 247Solar to build and operate a small concentrated solar plant. While the plant will be extremely small (1 megawatt) and mix in photovoltaics, the company had a unique angle: The same hardware that is handling the solar thermal output can simply be run using fossil fuels if there is a significant shortfall in renewable generation. “Our systems are their own backup,” said 247Solar’s Mark von Keszycki. “During times of low solar resource—long duration, bad weather, whatever the case may be—they’re able to burn a variety of fuels.” To understand how this works, it’s best to step back and understand the entire system being built, which includes details about how the 247Solar team hopes to avoid some of the problems faced by other solar thermal systems and provide a great deal of flexibility in terms of how its systems get put to use. Like other concentrated solar systems, 247Solar’s will be using a field of heliostats. But the receiver—the hardware that they focus the sunlight on—is distinctive, in that it works with ambient-pressure air. (It’s an updated version of hardware developed by a German company.) Air exits this hardware at approximately 970° C. That air can potentially be used directly for industrial process heat or sent on to the hardware that generates electricity. But in most cases, it will be directed to heat storage. Rather than dealing with molten salts and potential phase transition, 247Solar heats up ceramic pellets roughly the size of a golf ball. Depending on the details of the storage configuration, von Keszycki says that the heat from the storage could be used to generate power for anywhere from nine to 20 hours after the Sun sets. Hours will obviously be shorter if some of the heat is used to generate power during the day, but 247Solar plans to pair its system with photovoltaic installations for daytime generation. Wherever the hot air comes from, it will be sent to a heat exchanger. This is designed as a drop-in replacement for a combustion unit on a traditional turbine to heat up compressed air to drive generation. “The heat exchanger does the same job as the combustor did,” 247Solar’s Bruce Anderson told Ars. “It’s in the same place in the turbine assembly as the combustor was: after the compression stage and before the expander.” The initial installation will pair two 200 kW generators; aside from the heat exchanger, everything else is part of a standard off-the-shelf package. This is the point where the system’s flexibility comes in: It doesn’t care how the air in the exchanger gets heated. So, any of a variety of fuels can be burned to provide the equivalent heat. von Keszycki suggested that adding the capability to the system to burn fossil fuels tacks on less than 10 percent of its total cost. The air coming out of the heat exchanger is still quite hot, in the neighborhood of 250° C, and von Keszycki called it “a separate usable resource” that could be directed to a variety of applications, including industrial processes and absorption cooling. Again, if intermediate temperatures are needed, it could potentially be mixed with some hot air coming directly from the storage medium, albeit at the cost of generation. The first installation will be small, with a pair of 200 kW generators, and so will mostly serve to validate the system’s engineering—Anderson said the biggest worries are “unknown unknowns.” But the system is designed to be modular, and the company is already working through the engineering of a 3 megawatt facility that combines multiple units that share a single tower that holds multiple receivers and heat storage. The installation will also give the company a sense of how the system actually works out financially. On a typical grid, there are few incentives for providing around-the-clock renewable energy. And the cost of ensuring that there is enough generation to back up solar plants is often absorbed by other parties. The ability to generate fossil-fuel-based backup power without having to build a dedicated facility can definitely save someone money, but markets aren’t always set up so that it’s easy to extract those savings. So, it’s not surprising that 247Solar has other markets in mind. It’s especially excited about remote mining sites, which typically require diesel to be shipped in, often at considerable expense. The 247Solar system would avoid that expense, while potentially providing process heat that could be useful for some of the ore processing. And powering mining operations without emissions would work well for companies that are required to account for the carbon emissions of their supply chain. Still, there might be a space for this sort of facility on the grid, if it does successfully avoid the need for dedicated fossil fuel plants that only ever run for a small fraction of the night—provided the price and incentives line up.

The Trump administration is trying to stop Utah from enforcing its gambling ban against Kalshi, making Utah the 10th state sued by the US government agency that regulates prediction markets. The company and two co-founders each face 13 felony charges and one misdemeanor for violating gambling laws. The charges are based on sports bets offered on Kalshi between September 13 and September 24. “The state did not issue arrest warrants for Monsour and Lopes Lara. Instead, they have been issued summons to respond to the charges in Third District Court in Salt Lake County,” The Salt Lake Tribune wrote. For 130 years, Utah has been clear: gambling has no place here, and we will enforce our laws.” Utah already defeated Kalshi in federal court after the prediction market filed a preemptive lawsuit that sought to prevent the state from enforcing its gambling laws. Now that Utah is moving ahead in its attempt to shut down Kalshi, it must defend against a lawsuit filed by the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC). The Utah attorney general’s office declined to comment today on the CFTC lawsuit. The CFTC previously filed lawsuits against Arizona, Connecticut, Illinois, Kentucky, Minnesota, New Mexico, New York, Rhode Island, and Wisconsin. The federal agency also filed briefs supporting Kalshi in appeals court cases. “Utah seeks to shut down prediction markets across a wide variety of markets,” CFTC Chairman Michael Selig said. “Event contracts do not just serve hedging and investment purposes—they also provide the public with information about the outcome of future events. Congress entrusted the CFTC to be the federal regulator with sole responsibility to oversee these markets. As I’ve said before, the CFTC has the expertise and responsibility to defend its exclusive jurisdiction over commodity derivatives, and that’s exactly what we’ll do.” The US Commodity Exchange Act (CEA) gives the CFTC authority to regulate swaps. The key legal questions being grappled with by courts throughout the US are whether event contracts offered by prediction markets qualify as swaps, and whether the CFTC jurisdiction over swaps preempts states from applying their gambling laws. The CFTC is suing Utah in the same US district court that previously ruled in Utah’s favor. Kalshi sued Utah in February in an attempt to stop the state from bringing an enforcement action against the prediction market. In August, US District Judge Robert Shelby granted Utah’s motion for summary judgment and denied Kalshi’s motion for a preliminary injunction. US law states that CFTC jurisdiction does not “supersede or limit the jurisdiction conferred on courts of the United States or any State,” and “explicitly provides for State regulation of its gaming laws in all but a few specified cases,” he wrote. “Congress is aware that some States permit gambling while others do not, and the CEA explicitly provides for State jurisdiction,” Shelby wrote. Kalshi is now appealing its loss to the US Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit but was unable to obtain an injunction preventing enforcement of the state gambling ban while that litigation continues. On September 8, Tenth Circuit judges denied Kalshi’s emergency motion for an injunction pending appeal. Utah filed its lawsuit seeking a shutdown of Kalshi about six weeks later. The CFTC lawsuit against Utah makes arguments similar to the ones already rejected by the district court, although some other courts have ruled otherwise. “Utah is not the first State that has attempted to invade the Commission’s exclusive jurisdiction over swaps,” the CFTC lawsuit said. “Already, several federal courts—including the Third Circuit—have swiftly responded by issuing or affirming temporary restraining orders and preliminary injunctions barring the States from enforcing their gambling laws against CFTC-regulated exchanges that offer sports-related or other event contracts.” The Third Circuit appeals court ruled that New Jersey cannot regulate sports bets on prediction markets. But Kalshi lost similar cases in the Sixth Circuit and Ninth Circuit in rulings that went in favor of Ohio, Tennessee, and Nevada. Because circuit appeals courts are divided on the issue, it is likely that the Supreme Court will take up the case and determine whether states can apply their gambling laws to prediction markets. New Jersey petitioned the Supreme Court to resolve the circuit split, and 39 other states and the District of Columbia filed a brief supporting New Jersey’s petition. A swarm of Silicon Valley billionaires, including Google co-founder Sergey Brin, has spent more than $333 million campaigning against the potential tax, warning it will backfire and push the wealthy to flee the sunshine state. Such reactions are not new. Even the income tax once looked like an assault on property. The Supreme Court struck one down as unconstitutional in 1895, and it took the 16th Amendment in 1913 to make it ordinary. Taxes on great fortunes may travel the same road and become unremarkable within a decade. Plus, research indicates that the wealthiest households often pay lower effective tax rates than the people who work for them. I support Proposition 40. But it is not sufficient in addressing the underlying economic issues at hand. A wealth tax like Proposition 40 tries to recover a share of value long after that value has been created, compounded, and defended. By then, the money has lawyers. Which is why the origins of this extreme wealth matter, now that the prospect of redistribution has so agitated the people holding it. To understand how these systems work, think of the iPhone. As I argued in my book The Entrepreneurial State, the iPhone's foundational technologies came from public research: the internet, GPS, the touchscreen, and the voice recognition behind Siri. Speech understanding research at Carnegie Mellon, MIT, and Stanford Research Institute was funded by the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) in the 1970s. Researchers then spent two more decades on language and learning systems no company would finance, because the payoff lay thirty years out. Or think of Google. The search algorithm that made Brin's fortune grew out of a National Science Foundation project at Stanford. And then there’s SpaceX, whose listing made Musk a trillionaire, took $396 million from NASA to develop the Falcon 9 and the Dragon capsule, then $1.6 billion to fly cargo to the space station and $2.6 billion to fly astronauts. The world’s richest man was also the biggest political donor this election cycle, spending $366 million supporting Republican causes. Other people see them as tech bros. I see them as a brologarchy. They surfed off publicly financed research to earn enormous rents, and continue to profit off of them through what I call “algorithmic rents”: returns from owning the platforms, data, and models the rest of us must pass through, and manipulating what our “attention” sees. None of this takes anything away from the entrepreneurs. Turning a research program into a reusable rocket is hard. The public carried the risk in the years when these technologies had no market and no buyer. The state carries the blame: for not setting out, in the contracts, the terms of that initial investment and a fair share of the rewards for the risk the public took. The Department of Energy made the asymmetry explicit in 2009, issuing two near-identical loan guarantees to two of Musk’s companies: $465 million to Tesla and $500 million to Solyndra. Solyndra, a now-bankrupt solar panel manufacturer, collapsed two years later, and the taxpayer absorbed the loss and the political damage. Tesla, by contrast, succeeded. Under its loan agreement, the government held warrants it could exercise only if Tesla failed to repay on schedule; Tesla repaid nine years early and the warrants lapsed. The public took the downside on one deal and wrote itself out of the upside on the other. Economists call this socializing risk and privatizing reward. It describes a contract drafted without the confidence to ask for anything. NASA once had that confidence. In the early 1960s, it moved Apollo contracts from cost-plus-fixed-fee to incentive contracts tying a contractor's fee to cost, schedule, and performance, setting terms rather than writing blank checks. More recently, the CHIPS and Science Act attached conditions to $52.7 billion of semiconductor funding, covering where plants get built and what jobs they create. I believe those conditions did not go far enough. Wage standards covered laborers and mechanics rather than every worker, and affected communities got no formal seat in the decisions. But the principle is American, recent, and bipartisan. Public money can come with terms. This is called “pre-distribution,” shaping how value is created and shared at the outset, rather than waiting for the tax code to repair the damage, and it is a crucial principle for making capitalism work for us all. But establishing this kind of capitalism requires that we commit to another principle: access for all and reward sharing. If the public carries the risk, it should hold a claim on the reward. In practice, that means equity stakes in companies built on public loans, royalties on patents from publicly funded research, and a public fund that recycles the returns into the next round of risk. The United States has started to embrace this philosophy in its semiconductor infrastructure funding. It could do the same for our public investments in artificial intelligence, and those contracts are still being written. These principles address our broader capitalist system and create a different framing from universal basic income, which many tech billionaires are quite fond of. A basic income leaves the origins of their fortunes untouched, treats wealth as given, and asks only for a transfer, which is why it can be endorsed without conceding anything. A citizens' dividend changes the logic, because it pays out on the grounds that the public helped create the value in the first place. That is a debt owed directly, not only through the tax code. And we know it is possible. Alaska has paid its residents a dividend from oil revenue since 1982. None of this is an argument for voting against Proposition 40. California's billionaires should pay, and the state's health system needs the money. But a one-time levy collected decades after the fact is a costly way to claim a return that could have been written into the contract at the start. Redistribution settles old accounts. Pre-distribution can redesign an economy from the outset for more inclusive, sustainable growth. It matters that we act now. The next fortune is being built right now, on public research, with public money; we should make sure it serves the common good. An economy is not weather, a natural phenomenon that cannot be designed differently or more fairly. It is a set of contracts, and we are the ones who write them. We can write them for an economy designed with people and for them, one that shares the knowledge and the rewards along the way. He’s spent the last decade baselessly questioning the integrity of U.S. elections—despite plenty of evidence finding they are safe and secure—with things coming to a violent head on Jan. 6, 2021. This year, as more and more polling is suggesting Democrats will sweep Republicans, even in solidly red districts, it raises the question: Will the president accept the results of critical House and Senate races, or won’t he? “I will, if they’re honest elections,” Trump ominously responded when asked this very question on Tuesday. But I have become particularly interested in New Hampshire’s Senate race, in large part because I live here—I moved a couple of weeks after Trump retook the presidency, soon enough that my town hall was still displaying the 2024 vote count, when 800 residents went for Kamala Harris and 792 went for Donald Trump. And I have come to think of this race not as a sleeper, but as the flip side of the coin to whatever happens with Abdul El-Sayed’s bid for Michigan’s seat or James Talarico’s impressive attempt to flip Texas. This is a race of establishment candidates in a moment when both the Republican establishment and the Democratic establishment are beleaguered. I wanted to find out what the establishment version of either political party even looks like these days, and whether the most boring Democrat in a midterms year of stars might have something to teach the rest of his party. I also needed to understand whether anyone actually votes a split ticket in our wildly polarized times. Given my own progressive tendencies, I came away surprised by how much close observation of the contest changed my mind. It’s not that this race feels like it’s happening specifically in 2012, it’s just that it has a retro feeling about it, particularly thanks to the candidates themselves. Jason Barabas, a government professor and director of the Rockefeller Center for Public Policy at Dartmouth College, put it succinctly: “You have the first open Senate race since 2010, but you have two incumbents squaring off.” Chris Pappas served in the House of Representatives for four terms before the Dems nominated him for Senate this year, and Sununu, a former congressman and senator, comes from the state’s biggest political dynasty. (It’s not just John Sununu’s brother Chris—the boys’ father, also John, also served as governor of the state, and as chief of staff for George H. W. Bush, with a memorable legacy.) “If his name wasn’t Sununu, I don’t think he’d be in the position he’s in,” Nicholas Germana, a government professor at Keene State University, told me. Germana knows what he’s talking about not only because he studies government (primarily German politics, not American ones), but because, like so many New Hampshirites, he serves in the state government himself. New Hampshire’s House of Representatives has 400 members (though apparently no one sits in seat No. 13), making it the second biggest legislative body in the country, just after the U.S. House, and one of the largest governing bodies in the world. This is particularly silly when you consider the small size of the state—with under 1.5 million residents, it’s split into just two congressional districts. But it’s also a huge source of pride and a cause of political engagement here. While reporting this story, multiple state reps reminded me that New Hampshire was the first state to declare independence from England (six months before the country did), and even if the state is losing its first-in-the-nation primary status (Iowa was always a caucus), being demoted from second to third (South Carolina and Nevada are moving up for Democrats) in this upcoming cycle, New Hampshire will still take its task extremely seriously. Is it people’s political involvement that has given the state its purple nature, with a push and pull back and forth between the parties? Not really. Independents outnumber registered Republicans and Democrats. For years now, the state government has been red—and currently, it’s a united Republican governorship, Senate, and House—while the state votes blue for president and sends a full blue delegation to D.C. Which means that people are splitting their tickets: red for the state, blue for the country. “That’s something we talk about all the time—marvel at, frankly—is how much ticket splitting there is,” Germana told me. That’s partly because you have a much higher likelihood of actually knowing your House representative, which might get you to vote across party lines. But it’s also because there’s some cultural state pride in “balancing things out,” even in these times. New Hampshire had one of the highest rates of ticket splitters in the 2022 midterms, Barabas told me, and this year, 13 percent of voters have already declared a preference for splitting their ticket. (The likely way this will lean is to send Pappas to Washington while the current governor, Kelly Ayotte, stays in power.) But the times are making it harder and harder to do that. These midterms are seeing an active effort from Democrats across the state as they respond to some of the more extreme moves made by the united Republican state government. “Republicans in the state had been able to maintain a brand identity that is different than the national Republican Party—rooted in that traditional view of what a Yankee Republican used to be,” Germana told me. But as the Republican agenda has lurched right, that distance has evaporated. In recent sessions, the GOP here has passed culture-war legislation like banning trans athletes from sports. They’ve also taken more aggressive steps to throttle public schools, including voting in a school voucher program that has activated plenty of independent voters. These days, there’s “no daylight between the Republican brand in the state and the Republican Party nationally,” Germana told me, though it’s worth keeping in mind that Germana is not just an observer—he’s technically a member of the opposition party. All of this makes Sununu’s bid to flip a Senate seat a tough effort, and that’s before you take into account his weaknesses as a candidate. While Sununu says he’s not at all trying to fool anyone into thinking he’s Chris, multiple people told me they only realized which brother was running when the primary came around in September. (Their reaction was not positive—Chris had a lot of cross-party appeal thanks to being “a phenomenal retail politician,” Germana said, which John lacks.) John Sununu’s been out of politics since he lost to Jeanne Shaheen in 2008, and it’s Shaheen he’s looking to replace now. While his absence from politics affords him a certain distance from Trump, when he lost his Senate seat, Sununu resorted to a classic move for the establishment and politicians of both parties: He went into lobbying, something the Pappas campaign has made hay of in both ads and debates. John Sununu was a Never-Trump Republican who supported John Kasich in 2016 and Nikki Haley in 2024. And yet, he finds himself in a difficult position this race, as D.C. Republicans personally recruited him to run, after failing to get his brother to do so. It feels a bit like he’s trying to run this race as if he could be New Hampshire’s very own Susan Collins, a Republican, sure, but a reasonable one who would hold his party to account. The problem is, Donald Trump endorsed him during his primary race, and here, like everywhere, Trump’s popularity is plummeting. Recent polling found that the number of Granite Staters who approve of the president was the lowest ever—just 19 percent said they approved of Trump in September of this year (compared to the 41 percent who approved of Trump in 2018, during his first in-office midterms). People are mad about tariffs and the Iran war just like they are everywhere—but New Hampshirites get to be extra mad because we share a border with Canada and are heading into winter, where fuel costs will matter more. All in all, Collins and Sununu seem to be the last individuals trying to claim that it’s possible to be something of a New England Republican—conservative on fiscal issues while being socially liberal—as Trump’s presence and his overwhelming grip on the Republican Party make the viability of that brand more and more difficult. But even Collins is struggling with this message this cycle, and Sununu is not campaigning in the relentless way she is in neighboring Maine. “Sununu is running a low-key campaign with almost no public rallies, few appearances from Republican surrogates, and limited engagement with national media,” the Dispatch reported recently, and he dropped out of what was supposed to be the final debate. I myself tried to engage with his campaign in multiple ways—attempting to RSVP to a meet-and-greet I was told was closed to the press, emailing the campaign, and even eventually texting his campaign spokesman—all to no avail. The main action seems to be in the form of the millions of dollars pouring into the state, where the GOP does have a clear advantage. (In early October, Republicans had spent $39 million compared to Dems’ $23 million, though both campaigns said they have about $14 million on hand for the final few weeks.) The latest ad features Sununu trying to distance himself from Trump, repeating his line that he’s “never met the man.” Pappas is doing the exact opposite of Sununu. He’s been crisscrossing the state (well, driving up and down—you can’t really drive across in New Hampshire) with bigwig Democrats who are happy to add a stop here in anticipation of 2028. Raphael Warnock, Pete Buttigieg, and Gavin Newsom all came through, and late this month, Pappas was joined by Kamala Harris herself. Which is part of what has gotten Pappas into trouble with members of the left flank of his party: Donald Trump may be toxic, but the Democrats are also dealing with their own intra-party feud. Manzur, his primary challenger, took aim at Pappas’ establishment connections and big money—her signs said “Powered by People, Not Billionaires.” And like Abdul El-Sayed, she tried to make her weak fundraising (in the low hundreds of thousands, not millions) an asset rather than a liability. It didn’t work—Pappas beat her by a ratio of just about 2-to-1—but it doesn’t mean her messaging didn’t land somewhere. I spoke with a group of students at Colby-Sawyer College in New London who were all required by a class to attend a political event on campus, and when I asked what they thought of Pappas, they all groaned and told me he was a “corporate Dem”—basically as bad as Sununu, who they didn’t want to vote for either, mind you. Several were still deciding whether they’d bother to change their state registration to vote here this fall (one knew she wouldn’t because she was “excited to vote against Susan Collins”). College students will be college students, but they weren’t the only ones I heard lamenting that Pappas might not have what it takes. An AARP poll that found Pappas has the edge also showed that both candidates have underwater favorability ratings. And I have to admit, for a long time, I felt the same way. On primary day, I was personally conflicted about who I’d vote for. (I’m a registered Democrat.) Ultimately, I watched about 30 minutes of the debate between Pappas and Manzur, and decided that Manzur wouldn’t have a chance of making it out of our purple state. I pulled the ballot for Pappas, but really it was a vote for pragmatism, driven by concerns over the ultimate balance of power in the Senate. Since then, though, I’ve been following him much more closely, and I’ve come to feel differently about my pragmatism vote. That’s largely thanks to the actual campaign that Pappas has knit together—one that has closed the initial gap he faced and looks solid going into November. At one stop I watched him tour a small manufacturing plant, nothing bright or flashy, but Pappas was the picture of politeness, gamely asking questions about how they’d gotten a competitive edge in the niche grips they made. The visit demonstrated what a layup Dems are getting this time around: Tariffs causing this small business problems—check. The war in Iran increasing expenses—check. Healthcare premiums going up—check. Pappas, whose family has long owned the Puritan, an event and conference space with a restaurant that claims to have invented chicken fingers, was able to engage in the minutiae of their business, from a place of true empathy. An on-floor engineer remarked that they sell their scrap metal, and the price has skyrocketed—something they take as a sure indication that the economy is going down the tubes. “Just like when peanut butter sales go up,” the chicken-finger heir nodded solemnly. At that stop, I spoke with an employee who told me she’s never been into politics herself, but that she’s estranged from both her parents on account of Trump. She stopped talking with her mother the day Roe v. Wade was overturned, she said—she couldn’t take it anymore, and she wants to set an example for her son about how people should behave. Her story was specific, but the distaste for Trump was second only to “affordability” on the trail, and luckily for Democrats this cycle, the two are intertwined now. You can’t go to a campaign event in New Hampshire without running into at least a few state reps (or candidates for state rep), and many of these people told me that they’re hearing from a lot of independents who are fed up with Trump and prices and are voting blue, and even a few registered Republicans here and there. Many offered praise for Pappas, who is skilled at listening to all sides of an issue and takes his representation of a mixed district seriously, they said—he’s made it easy for right-leaning folks to switch over. “He’s a guy who knows how to win a purple district,” Germana had told me, and he’s calibrated his public persona and strategy around that. One thing that’s stood out to me is the way Pappas seems to actually decenter himself in these settings, which you don’t need me to tell you is unusual for a politician. If elected, he will be the first openly gay senator in American history. This is not something he ever brings up himself, which, sure, maybe is a nod to the fact that identity politics is truly dead, but I think is more of a representation of his style as a legislator. Also: You’d think the man whose family claims they invented chicken fingers, of all things—what is more American and universally beloved than a chicken finger—would toot that at least a little bit on the campaign trail. But it’s all pretty straight “as a family business owner, I understand the struggle” type stuff. (On a recent visit to the Puritan Backroom, I was struck by two things: how quintessentially New Hampshire it was, which I’ve realized is my code for “caters to Trump voters,” and how delightfully large their Puritan Backroom–branded sauces are—Pappas should be running on his record of ranch portion sizes.) He’s a politician who has done things methodically, which in this day and age means mainly that he paid his dues in both the New Hampshire House and the U.S. House, along with a stint on the state’s executive council, before he went for a bigger office. In his mid-40s, he seems young for the Senate, but that’s just because he has a boyish look and our standards for how old lawmakers should reasonably be are broken. He has a stick-to-the-message-ness that can make him seem rehearsed—or can be interpreted as discipline from someone who is taking his race dead seriously. At a canvass kickoff event on Oct. 17, the one where Gavin Newsom joined the New Hampshire delegates running for office, I heard more practical praise for Pappas. A state House rep who described herself as far more progressive than he is explained why she voted for him in the primary over Manzur: Pappas pledged to endorse his opponent if he was bested, and Manzur did not. This was not the moment for such games, the rep had concluded. There was a couple who had come up from Massachusetts to canvass who constituted the first real Chris Pappas fans I met in the wild (“I’ve always liked him,” the woman told me, with an almost mothering quality). OK, fine, it wasn’t really the wild—these were people who were giving up their Saturday to knock on doors for Democrats, and would most likely end up at a “No Kings” rally later in the afternoon. Just two days before, the Pappas campaign had announced a “Country Before Party” steering committee of more than 50 Republicans and independents who were endorsing Pappas for Senate. The big coup on that list was Don Bolduc, the Republican who has been defeated for that exact Senate seat in the previous cycle. “I think that one was kind of surprising, to tell you the truth, and that can be quite powerful,” Barabas, the Dartmouth professor, told me. At the canvass launch, Pappas attributed Bolduc’s endorsement to his military experience, and his recognition of the nightmare the military is currently facing, led by Pete Hegseth into the Iran war. It set Pappas up perfectly for his line that “everyone is welcome on this team: progressives, moderates, independents, Republicans.” It was this plain appeal that made me start to realize what feels good—almost calming—about Chris Pappas’ campaign. There was something fundamentally decent going on here—a representative who truly seemed to understand that the job of an elected official is to represent all of his constituents, not just the ones who agree with him. One of the most memorable pieces of political journalism I’ve read this midterms cycle was a New York Times Magazine feature from Robert Draper that reported out, via interviews with legislators in both national houses, why Congress is so broken. It turned the entire idea of the midterms on its head for me: “On Nov. 3, American voters will decide which party controls the House and the Senate for the remainder of Trump’s presidency. In theory, at least, the outcome of their decision should be enormously consequential.” In practice, though, Draper is not sure how much any of it really matters, as he goes on to outline exactly how much power the legislative branch has ceded during the mind-bending times of President Donald Trump. There are many reasons for that, including Trump’s astonishing willingness to break norms (see: setting tariffs, something Congress is supposed to have control over). But Trump is also the avatar for how our broader politics have broken over the past decade-plus. Members from both parties explained that the quality of those who choose to join the government has declined, by which they mean that they’re all narcissists now. “What you see now are too many people who come in viewing Congress as their green screen,” David Joyce, a Republican from Ohio, told Draper. “And they’re going to make a name for themselves and don’t give a damn about anything else.” I’m not trying to suggest that the old Congress was all sunshine and daisies. Bogged down by our resolutely two-party system, we Americans can end up fetishizing Washington bipartisanship (that is ultimately out of step with what most regular people want—there’s a reason both sides are turning on the establishment this cycle). But even compromise is getting a bad name these days. Work too hard to actually get things done, and you might end up primaried by an inexperienced challenger like Manzur who may have plenty of good ideas, but no ability to even make it to D.C. to try them. It’s not that Congress should be populated by only normies, but it’s good to have a solid bloc in the mix. They aren’t exciting, but they’re a decent bulwark against the kind of “exciting” we’ve been living through, with brief interruptions, for a decade now. In the absence of a Congress that passes legislation, we have a Congress full of grandstanders. Forgive me for my Pollyannaishness, but as I’ve tried to figure out what is happening in my own state, why this tight race isn’t getting as much attention as I think it should, I came to a pretty simple conclusion. Chris Pappas is running to legislate. In 2026, that’s pretty boring. But I found myself convinced by his pitch—that he is actually going to Washington to attempt to solve our problems, even if it means reaching across the aisle or compromising in favor of getting something, anything, done. Rust guru rejiggers web app reactivity for potentially speedier page loading Popular for other uses, Rust isn't typically deployed for web development. That could change, however. With AI here to do the heavy lifting, coders may want to consider the programming language, given its runtime performance and safety guarantees, suggested Carl Lerche, an Amazon Web Services principal engineer, speaking at ScyllaDB’s 2026 P99 virtual conference last week. Lerche was there to demonstrate his latest project, Topcoat, a framework for running web applications written in Rust. Lerche is perhaps best known for creating Tokio, which is the most popular asynchronous Rust runtime in that ecosystem. “Three years ago, had [I] gone out and said that Rust could be the best language for building web apps, you probably would have called me crazy,” he told the virtual audience. Web developers gravitate towards the languages they find most familiar. These days, that means JavaScript, or possibly Ruby. A number of web frameworks already use Rust. Both Leptos and Dioxus compile Rust into WebAssembly, which most browsers run. This approach is great for heavy computational jobs, though the upfront loading time can be a real drag. Topcoat compiles your Rust code into more web-friendly JavaScript. For Lerche, JavaScript remains the best way to interact with the browser's Document Object Model (DOM). “Generally, the approach taken by any Rust framework can be traced back to another ecosystem, usually a very popular one. You can't really point to a single existing framework as [Topcoat’s] main source of inspiration,” noted frontend developer Ken Kohlman, in a summary video on his “Code to the Moon” YouTube channel. Lerche admitted that Topcoat isn’t for building complex applications, on the scale of, say, Figma, but it can easily be used for the run-of-the-mill, browser-based, database-driven applications, which is to say 90 percent of the web today. Unlike most present-day JavaScript frameworks, Topcoat eschews the current definition of being reactive, where the program logic is dumped onto the browser when the session begins. Instead, most of the logic executes on the server, with only the results delivered to the browser (Developers can still write client-side logic using a subset of Rust, which Topcoat automatically compiles into JavaScript for the browser). It’s an old idea recast with better tech. Server-side rendering predates reactive programming (see Apache’s server-side includes). With server-side rendering, Lerche admitted, “It might sound like we're going back to the old days of full page refreshes for every click [but] you can actually get almost all of what you think of as reactive with server-side rendering.” But server-side programming has been making a comeback lately, especially for those devs disgruntled with React and other frameworks. HTMX, Datastar, and Phoenix LiveView all render web pages on the server, sending them back to the browser nearly completed. Topcoat is just tweaking their ideas to fit well with Rust, Lerche said. When a user smashes a button on a web page, the whole page isn’t refreshed. Just the HTML snippet for that component is returned and refreshed by the server. Topcoat saves load times by rendering all the components at once, rather than one by one. Also, data loading does not block the loading of the page. The server can send back a web page with placeholders into which the data will stream. One big dollop of secret sauce that Topcoat has is that it provides a way for the component, which is already on the server, to speak directly to the database, thanks to an earlier project from Lerche called Toasty, a Rust-based asynchronous database object-relational mapper (which he spoke about at P99 2024). “You can do your rendering right with the data immediately, so you don't have to figure out how to serialize it, send it to the client, deserialize it,” he said. By pushing data loading deep down into the page render, the server can send the head of the HTML template to the client immediately, allowing the browser to start preloading assets (like CSS or images) while the server renders the rest of the page. “The simplicity is incredible, and it is something that we have been sorely missing in this space. I feel like someone who doesn't really know Rust could get off the ground pretty quickly with this thing,” Kohlman said. Snoops 'likely still' have access to US government-affiliated network Suspected Chinese attackers chained PHP2Shell with a Linux kernel privilege-escalation exploit to root 25 servers, including Palo Alto Networks firewalls linked to US government-affiliated organizations, according to Dream researchers. The snoops began exploiting these servers on October 23, targeting organizations across the US, Japan, Taiwan, and South Korea. The attackers also honed in on US government-affiliated firewalls. “Within hours of compromising a US government-affiliated firewall, they had tunneled into the internal network, compromised a third server using the same PHP2Shell exploit, and launched a full Active Directory attack campaign - all through a chain that started with a single unauthenticated HTTPS request to a management interface that should never have been exposed to the internet,” the Israeli AI cybersecurity startup said in a report. Plus, the attackers “likely still” have persistent, remote access to compromised organizations’ networks, Dream noted. The attackers rooted the first US government-affiliated firewall on October 23, but the device crashed shortly afterward, possibly due to around 900 MB of exploit traffic. According to Dream, it remained unresponsive despite 240 automated checks over two hours. They successfully exploited the second firewall a day later, on October 24. The compromised firewalls ran vulnerable versions of Palo Alto Networks' PAN-OS. Dream said it had been working with the vendor to investigate the compromises, and Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 researchers independently verified the attacker's tooling and confirmed Dream's reported results. To break into the firewalls, the attackers built a custom exploit chain that includes PHP2Shell and a Fragnesia kernel flaw, and ran about 40 custom post-exploitation scripts once they were inside the targeted organizations. PHP2Shell, comprised of CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, came to light early last week, along with fears that attackers would abuse the bugs to take down most of the internet. Despite “hundreds” of PHP2Shell proof-of-concept repositories on GitHub, the first reports of successful attacks didn’t start until Saturday. Both bugs have CVSS scores of 9.2 and can be exploited without authentication to run code on vulnerable PHP servers. The PHP2Shell exploit recovered by Dream was not a copy of any known public exploit. This summer, hundreds of OpenAI agents tried to cheat on a cybersecurity test. They broke out of their test environment and successfully hacked Hugging Face, a site that they believed held clues about how they’d be graded. Around the same time, another OpenAI agent accessed private files from an Australian government website. It’s a problem that’s by no means confined to OpenAI. In the months since, Anthropic, Google, and Meta have also disclosed incidents in which their agents have gone off track. And worryingly, in many cases the agents eluded the very researchers and safety teams tasked with monitoring them. AI agents are taking unauthorized actions, and the companies building and running them are mostly relying on luck and hindsight to mitigate any harmful impacts. They must do more to ensure safety. That’s why EU regulators and US agencies are rightfully calling for agent developers and deployers to monitor agents in real time. But these rules assume that companies can see what their agents are doing and why—when in reality, they lack sufficient visibility. As agents roll out across critical sectors, from finance to government to medicine, we must be able to monitor them in real time to prevent wide-ranging and potentially devastating consequences.  And while safety depends on monitoring, monitoring requires observability: the capacity to capture what the system did, process it into signals, and present those to humans or automated oversight systems. In other words, we need to know what the agent did and why it did it. And as our recent research shows, current agent frameworks—the software that runs agents— do not reliably generate the crucial observability records we need to ensure that agents are deployed safely. Agents present new kinds of problems that generative AI alone does not. They inherit the unpredictability of the LLMs they are built on, but where a generative AI system produces a single output for a human to act on, an agent performs longer tasks—it plans, picks tools, spawns sub-agents, and takes actions. So an agent’s course can take shape in a multitude of different ways, and no tests before launch can fully anticipate the risks. This makes observing the agents while they work even more challenging, and even more important. With colleagues from academia, civil society, and industry, we studied the four most widely used agent frameworks to see what signals they actually emit. We found major gaps that made it hard to achieve key safety goals, like noticing policy violations, unauthorized actions, and even human oversight failures. Key observability requirements were missing—like Agent IDs that persist over time, and records about which humans approved agent tasks. This made it hard to tell which agents did what or assess whether they were taking unauthorized actions. The good news is that these gaps, once identified, can be closed. Doing so starts with capturing two types of records: those that reveal what happened in an agent’s run and how the agent made its decisions. The “what happened” records identify the agent (while preserving privacy) and log how humans intervened and whether agent permissions or “memory” shifted, while the “decision” records show an agent’s reasoning and levels of confidence in the actions it’s taken. Take, for example, agents working to process a bank’s mortgage loans. The system examines an application and recommends denying it, and then a human loan officer signs off on the decision. In a later audit, or perhaps even in real time, a bank or regulator needs to be able to reconstruct which agent made the call, which officer approved it, and how long they took. And the bank must also analyze records of how the agent arrived at its refusal decision in order to ensure it did not use off-limits information, like details about the neighborhood the applicant is from. Recording “what happened” and “how the agent decided” require different approaches, but both are feasible. The current lack of “what happened” records is mostly a software plumbing problem that can be addressed through OpenTelemetry, an open observability standard maintained by engineers across the industry and by the agent frameworks adopting it. OpenTelemetry has launched a working group drafting shared formats for tracing agents, but they have yet to be finalized. The group should urgently incorporate standards for maintaining persistent agent identity and for recording human interventions and changes to agents’ permission or memory. And it should be done before these systems can scale, all the while attending to the privacy implications of agent ID that links to real people. Preserving “how agents decided” records is more challenging. The issue raises open research questions for model providers and researchers: Does the step-by-step reasoning a model displays, known as chain of thought, reflect how the model actually made a decision? And do we have reliable ways to measure how confident the agent is in that decision? These questions need answers before anyone sets standards for how such observability records are used. No single organization or sector can close the observability gaps on their own, but together, they can support the infrastructure required to prevent dangerous incidents and support safety. AI developers building agent frameworks, like OpenAI, Anthropic, CrewAI, and LangChain, must integrate telemetry signals recording an agent’s persistent identity, any human interventions, and any changes to an agent’s permissions and memory. Meanwhile, enterprises adopting these agent frameworks have an influential role to play; enterprises in regulated industries, like banks and hospitals, can put pressure on the agent developers to supply these missing observability records where standards do not yet exist. Enterprises must also define policies, permissions, and roles that set out what agents can safely do, since telemetry alone cannot assess whether agents broke specific rules. Finally, regulators and AI standards institutes must specify the observability records required for high-stakes AI deployments while leaving the technical details to industry. We have already seen initial momentum here: NIST’s agent standards initiative in the US is working on agent identity, one of the most salient gaps we found in our research. No bank would let a thousand unbadged employees loose on customer accounts and hope to piece together what happened when something goes wrong. Yet that’s how we’re deploying AI agents. Making their behavior observable is the first step toward fixing this. Climate change itself is increasingly fueling climate change. Rising concentrations of greenhouse gases are driving up global temperatures, setting off dangerous feedback loops that we have not fully accounted for in our climate models—or our emissions-reduction goals. Scientists have long warned that warming would propel further warming in various ways, but a growing number of observations and studies are at last providing a sharper sense of just how powerful these cascading effects could be. And, by extension, they’re showing how much faster the planet may warm in this century. A study in Environmental Research Letters in September, synthesizing the results of previous papers, found that the additional carbon dioxide and methane released from thawing permafrost, intensifying wildfires, hotter lakes and reservoirs, and wetter, warmer wetlands alone could drive global average temperatures up 0.2 °C to 0.4 °C this century. That would come on top of the 2.6°C of warming above preindustrial levels that modelers are already expecting through 2100. The additional heat could subject many of the world’s cities to monthslong heat waves and expose hundreds of millions of people to flooding. None of the models that inform the major UN climate reports, which assess how much the planet will likely warm under various emissions scenarios, account for all these feedback effects. Many of them don’t incorporate any. That leaves a huge blind spot in our understanding of how much the world could actually warm in the coming decades—and how rapidly nations will need to slash emissions to stay within critical boundaries. A separate analysis published in One Earth in March, which examined what the feedback problem means for our international climate goals, concluded that the world may be much further off track than is widely believed. It found that just three regional sources of warming-driven emissions—thawing permafrost, increasing fires in boreal forests, and warming tropical wetlands—may shorten by as much as 25% the time before the planet consistently breaches the 1.5 °C and 2 °C warming thresholds. Our climate models and the UN’s reports clearly need to be updated to better represent these effects. Nations must also step up their efforts to reduce emissions to reflect this improving understanding of the dangers we face, says Sam Abernethy, the lead author of the paper and a climate scientist at Spark Climate Solutions, a San Francisco–based nonprofit research group. At least some of this is starting to happen. Spark kick-started an initiative, called the Warming-Induced Greenhouse Gas Emissions Model Intercomparison Project, through which more than 20 international teams are running the same set of analyses on different models to develop greater understanding of feedback effects’ potential power. Researchers involved hope that the UN will incorporate those results into its next major climate assessment, due out in 2029. That report will update estimates of the remaining emissions that can be released before the world crosses key temperature thresholds. But what is already clear is that the planet is rapidly warming and that nations haven’t done nearly enough to cut fossil-fuel pollution. And even if we did manage to halt it, warming-driven emissions could continue. So a number of the research groups involved in the recent studies have started to explore what nations could do to get feedback emissions under control. Scientists at the Woodwell Climate Research Center in Massachusetts are, for example, investigating the possibility of preventing fires or rapidly detecting them and putting them out when they threaten to burn the vegetation and soil that help insulate particularly carbon-rich wedges of permafrost (a subject I explored at length in a recent feature story). In addition, the Environmental Defense Fund’s Climate Innovation Initiative has backed lab research on the potential of adding sulfate, iron, or other compounds to wetlands to counteract fast-rising methane emissions. Several startups are also exploring possibilities. They’re looking into suppressing the lightning strikes that are igniting more and more fires in high latitudes, placing synthetic mats above permafrost to prevent it from melting, and deploying autonomous drones to quickly suppress potentially high-emissions fires before they burn out of control. Investigating all these ideas is worthwhile. But researchers are only in the earliest stages of figuring out how big a dent in emissions any such efforts could achieve across sprawling global ecosystems undergoing massive transformations. The surest path to reducing the effect of warming-driven warming remains the one we’ve known all along: breaking society’s addiction to fossil fuels. Living digital twins are helping industrial plants run more like living systems, and setting the stage for industries that work in sync. Most people associate industrial environments with cold steel and clockwork machinery. Functionally, though, a plant or factory is more like a living thing: It has a pulse, even a metabolism. It goes through wear and tear as it ages, and signs of deteriorating health often show up as barely perceptible irregularities. Digital twin technology has long aimed to mirror that dynamism and model anomalies that may be symptomatic of larger problems. But earlier implementations were a few beats behind physical reality; they were incapable of real-time analysis and unable to proactively address any issues detected. They focused more on simulation, observation and what-if scenario planning, rather than closed-loop execution. Rather than serving as a digital mirror of reality that can spot anomalies or suggest optimizations, a living digital twin combines a dynamic, high-fidelity digital representation of complex operations with the capacity to intervene in the physical world through operations and control systems. AI is at the center of this change. Rob McGreevy, chief product officer at AVEVA, likens newer, AI-infused digital twins to organisms that grow and evolve. These “living” versions are adaptive, evergreen models of a plant’s assets and processes. Because they keep pace with live conditions and connect systems that once ran in isolation, they provide a previously missing layer of visibility and orchestration that better reflects the organic rhythms of the facility itself. Now, agentic AI is also enabling industrial systems to move beyond diagnostics or suggestions and act of their own accord. As the technology progresses toward greater autonomy, it’s starting to develop the reflexes for a kind of industrial homeostasis, in which small, constant adjustments keep a plant or factory healthy and humming. All that said, human judgment and operator oversight still play an instrumental role by defining what healthy looks like to begin with—and flagging when AI systems are falling out of alignment. Living digital twins provide the means for human engineers to monitor what AI is doing and ensure execution is on track. Living digital twins link engineering and design information with real-time operational data through a digital thread, or a connected record of an asset that follows it through its lifecycle. A couple of notable technological shifts have helped string this thread together: Sensors (which have gotten more affordable in recent years) now stream telemetry from IoT devices and industrial control equipment, and software platforms bring that information into user-friendly interfaces. Predictive and physics-based models can also provide AI-enriched insights into how a machine or process might behave. The lenses through which operators view digital twins have evolved in turn. “You can look at the digital twin through the lens of sustainability: What are my emissions?” says McGreevy. “Or, through the lens of maintenance: Is this asset performing in a healthy way?
