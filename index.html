This week, Ars hosted a live discussion with Ross Scott about the Stop Killing Games movement and its ongoing efforts to ensure that games remain playable even after the original publisher shuts down the online servers undergirding them. Scott talked with Ars Technica Senior Gaming Editor Kyle Orland, saying said he felt the situation around long-term game access was already “intolerable a decade and a half ago” and that he “kept hoping someone else would kick this off, [someone] more experienced or knowledgeable, but it felt like nothing was happening.” Scott said he felt making the effort to organize a movement against the practice was like “[drawing] the short straw on this, essentially.” During the conversation, Scott noted how the standard practice of shutting down game access for any reason, at any time, with minimal notice, would seem ludicrous in other areas of life: Let’s say… the plan was to shut down [a game] in five years if it didn’t do well. That needs to be communicated then, if you want to go down that road, because this manner of things doesn’t really apply to other goods and services. Compared to, let’s say you got… a warranty for a product, and it doesn’t tell you a year or two; it just tells you, ‘Well, we’ll tell you when it expires.’ Or an insurance policy: ‘Yeah, we’ll tell you when your insurance policy expires.’ Like that would trip up other laws than that. From the technical side, Scott said that when a publisher plans ahead for the end of an online game, the costs to transition to third-party servers can often be minimal: I think many developers will back me up on this, that if you’re planning for this from the beginning, where that’s an established requirement, we need to have some way to run this game at end of life, while you’re designing it. As I understand it, the cost can be extremely low then because it comes down to developing the game differently rather than having to have all these additional costs. Because even online, most online-only games, as I understand it, have what are called local test environments. Because let’s say it does depend on Amazon servers to host their game, but there’s an interruption, or it’s down for a while, or there’s incompatibility that they missed. Well, then you don’t want all your development to come to a standstill because of that. So you usually have to have some version of the server that’s operational that you can kind of keep testing. If they already have that, that’s half the battle right there. Scott also said he has been disappointed by the way the EU Parliament has handled the movement’s concerns so far, but he remains hopeful for action in the near future: One of the biggest problems we faced on this is so many governments and ruling bodies aren’t sure how the law applies to this. So I figured, OK, if we can have a million people coming to the EU commission saying, ‘Hey, please resolve this.’ Even if they don’t agree with it, clarify the law on this. Like, do we have protections on this? I thought, OK, they’ll look at that and they’ll settle it one way or the other. Nope. Instead, they managed to just dodge it in every way and say, “Oh, yeah, we’ll let the courts in each country handle this, even though our part of our job is to harmonize policy across the member states.” In my opinion, they’re not doing their job. Not because they disagree with us but because of their charter for this. But during that time, all the attention from that helped us to gather a lot of support and attention from EU Parliament. And they’ve been very favorable to what we’re trying to do. And right now, we’re scheduled, so far so good, to get included into the Digital Fairness Act, which, it’s a complete coincidence, but it happens to cover many issues that we’re practically a perfect fit for.

Google began offering third-party app store downloads in the Play Store earlier this week, but US District Judge James Donato says the company isn’t doing enough. After reviewing the process for accessing the first non-Google store, Donato ruled that Google has added unnecessary “anticompetitive friction,” ordering the company to rectify the issues. While Epic and Google are patching things up with a global partnership agreement, the two are still on opposite sides of the antitrust case in the US. Google lost the case in 2023, and the intervening years have been about determining how to implement remedies to address Google’s anticompetitive behavior. One of the court’s key elements is the requirement that Google include third-party app stores in the Play Store, giving users an easy way to get apps from non-Google sources. Aptoide Games became the first such app store recently. However, Google worked hard to avoid this outcome, attempting to settle with Epic to keep third-party app stores out. When it was forced to admit the Aptoide store under the terms of the ruling, Google didn’t exactly put up a banner welcoming third-party app stores. In fact, Judge Donato found in a Thursday hearing that Google’s implementation was not good enough, according to The Verge. While there is a link to the Play Store’s alternative app store page, it’s buried several levels deep in its confusing menus. Epic lawyer Yonatan Even showed the court that even searching for “app store” and “aptoide” didn’t show the intended results like any other search. Instead, the Play Store shows an “are you looking for” banner that links to the third-party app store page. Google’s lawyers contended in the hearing that Epic had already agreed that app stores are in a special category due to their expanded permissions, so the extra step was appropriate. The court didn’t buy that explanation, though. “That is not acceptable,” said Donato. “That has to be fixed. I want every possible variation that’s even only 70 percent properly phrased.” Another problem is the process of actually installing the store. When you finally get to the page with Aptoide (and eventually other stores), there is no install button, as you’d get with an app or game in the store. A “view” button opens another dialog to actually install. Again, Donato said this additional step was unnecessary and seemed geared toward discouraging users from downloading alternative stores. He said the button must change to “install” instead of “view.” Google’s legal team agreed to make these changes, and Donato gave the company one week, noting they should contact him if there is any problem with that timeline. So we can expect a smoother install experience for alternative app stores very soon. It’s possible that once this process is complete, Epic will finally take advantage of the ruling it worked toward for years and release its own storefront in Google Play. For now, Aptoide stands alone.

There is a lot of excitement and some trepidation among The X-Files fans about Ryan Coogler’s reboot for Hulu, currently in production. While we’re waiting, Hulu just released Chris Carter’s revamped (and R-rated) director’s cut of the 2008 standalone spinoff film The X-Files: I Want to Believe, distinguishable from its predecessor with a slightly different title: The X-Files: I Want to Believe–Vrach Frankenshteyn. (Minor spoilers below but no major reveals.) I Want to Believe received mixed reviews at its release and didn’t exactly light up the box office, but it still grossed $68 million against its $30 million budget. By 2015, the project led to the return of The X-Files as a limited-run TV series, with the six-episode season 10 debuting in 2016. Mulder and Scully were reunited 14 years after the events of season 9 as the FBI reopened the X-Files under Assistant Director Walter Skinner (Mitch Pileggi). The response was strong enough to warrant a follow-up ten-episode season in 2018, focusing on Mulder and Scully’s search for their missing son, William. So one could hardly call I Want to Believe a failure. I quite enjoyed the film, but I always had a niggling sense that something was missing. That suspicion was confirmed when Carter made an appearance on David Duchovny’s podcast last summer and revealed that the studio had insisted he cut numerous scenes to get the desired PG-13 rating. Carter also hinted that a director’s cut was in the offing. “I made it too scary,” Carter told Duchovny of his original vision. “Now I have a chance to go back and make the scary movie that I always intended.” Originally slated for a June release on Disney+, I Want to Believe–Vrach Frankenshteyn eventually moved to Hulu with an August 14 release. It’s largely the same film as in 2008, just rejiggered to emphasize the stronger horror aspects that Carter originally envisioned. Per the official premise: When a group of women is mysteriously abducted, it becomes a case right out of The X-Files. Years after walking away from the FBI, Fox Mulder and Dana Scully are pulled back from the shadows when a federal agent vanishes without a trace. In a case you’d never see on TV, their only lead is a disgraced, defrocked priest claiming to have horrific psychic visions of the crime. Forced to confront the ghosts of their past, the partners must navigate a chilling winter landscape and an even darker human monstrosity. The truth of these crimes is out there somewhere… and it will take Mulder and Scully to find it. Ars Technica caught up with Carter to learn more. Ars Technica: The original series always had some elements of horror. I’m curious about why you wanted to make a feature film that was more overtly horror with I Want to Believe. Chris Carter: We certainly mined the horror genre over and over. The show always works at the intersection of science and science fiction. The original inspiration for this movie was that I read about a doctor who had done a head transplant from one monkey to another monkey’s body, and he’d done it successfully. Immediately I said, “That’s an X-File. I’ve got to tell that story.” Of course, I wanted to tell it with human beings. So I flew to Cleveland, Ohio, where this doctor was, and he told me how he did it. It was successful, but ultimately the monkey died. But then I started thinking, “Well, maybe this is a way to live forever.” If you could transplant your head onto a younger person’s body, you could possibly keep doing that until something went wrong. There’s this tech-bro fascination with living forever right now. So this story is as relevant now as it was back then. It’s a great time to tell this story. And I got to go back and revisit something that I was unable to make the way I wanted to make it, due to studio executives who really didn’t know the show. I had promised them I’d make a PG-13 movie because I thought, “That’s what I make on TV every week.” But it turns out that both the executives and the MPA, the people who do the ratings, are less permissive with a PG-13 movie than network television was with horror. Ars Technica: There’s more to your director’s cut than just restoring a few deleted scenes. You’ve done a full re-edit. Chris Carter: I worked with two different editors, people I respect and I’ve worked with before who weren’t involved with it all originally. I asked them to watch the movie and help me to refine the cut and make it the scary movie that I originally intended to make. It was heroic on their part. The movie was originally edited by an Academy Award winner, and so coming in and basically re-editing his vision took some courage. That said, I think the editors alone, just in the way they re-approached the movie, really helped it. I gave them the reins early on, and then when they gave those cuts back to me, that’s when I started to work on it. They added material, they took out material, they restructured material. I wanted to make a good, scary movie. The [2008] movie was a relationship movie for Mulder and Scully that happened to have an X-Files case. The weighting was wrong, and I wanted to remaster that. Ars Technica: Vrach Frankenshteyn is Russian for Victor Frankenstein. You dabbled with that theme in the series occasionally as well. What keeps drawing you back to that particular well? Chris Carter: Creating life anew, breathing life into something that is inert, is fascinating to me. That story just fascinates me. The Mary Shelley story is much different than the original Frankenstein movie. They didn’t use lightning bolts to bring the creature to life. It was much more like an X-Files story. I’ve always drawn on my love of that story. Even if it’s not as pointedly like The Postmodern Prometheus, that science lives and breathes in several places in The X-Files. Ars Technica: For many fans, Mulder and Scully will always be the beating heart of The X-Files. What makes them such an enduring couple, even if they are not always technically “together”? Chris Carter: Scully being a scientist and Mulder being a believer in the paranormal, and that they agree to disagree wonderfully—it’s part of the nature of their relationship. I think it’s their mutual respect for one another. That, for me, is the ideal man-woman relationship. It feels inevitable. It feels destined. I have that relationship with my wife. I believe in those connections, and I believe that everyone has a path. It’s just figuring out what path to take. Sometimes the path presents itself and you can’t see it. But with Mulder and Scully, they found their paths, and they’re traveling them together for better or worse. Ars Technica: Back in 2008, you said that if I Want to Believe did well, there was a third X-Files film you were eager to make. Is that still ticking away in the back of your mind? Chris Carter: It is ticking away in the back of my mind. If you watch the two reboots, there are four episodes [collectively] called “My Struggle.” They are the bookends for each of those series. They ended up in a place where people either loved me or hated me—when Scully revealed that she was pregnant. That is part of a bigger story, and I want to tell that story sometime. Right now, I think everyone’s focus is more on the Ryan Coogler reboot, and I don’t want to do anything to get in the way of that. I respect him, admire him, and want to see his vision. The story that I want to tell, if it is told—and I sure hope it will be—will be apart from that, but we certainly can’t ignore it. Ars Technica: Did you envision when you created The X-Files that the series would become such a cultural touchstone for so many people? Chris Carter: No, I didn’t. I was sitting alone in my mid-30s. I’m working in Hollywood. I’ve been hired to create TV shows. I love movies like the Sherlock Holmes movie Murder by Decree. I love Kolchak: The Night Stalker. I have all these things that are inspiring me. I put them all together into a—call it a kiln. And I fired the kiln, and I opened it up and it’s The X-Files. I had never in my wildest dreams believed that I’d be sitting here talking to you today about The X-Files, that there’d be a Lego set coming out, that it would’ve changed people’s lives. At the 30th reunion anniversary in Minneapolis, I had 12-year-old, 10-year-old kids come up and say they love the show. And I have to think to myself, “Your parents probably didn’t even know each other when the show first aired.” I’ll tell you another story that is near and dear to me. My wife and I were running on the beach not long ago; we run for 10 minutes, and we walk for a minute. When we started walking in that minute, I look up and I see an image that I recognize immediately. I start walking toward this young woman in her early 20s who’s wearing a graduation gown and holding her mortarboard in her hand. On the top of the cap, it’s got an image of Mulder and Scully. And it says, “The future is out there.” I say, “I know that image.” She said, “Oh yeah, it’s Mulder and Scully from The X-Files.” And I said, “I know, I know. I wrote that show.” It was a wonderful moment. She didn’t grow up with the show, she found the show. That’s the mind-blowing thing to me: it inspired her enough to put it on her graduation gown and cap. That kind of thing you can never imagine when you sit down to create something. Leading US AI labs such as OpenAI and Anthropic are releasing cheaper models as they fight to retain cost-conscious customers who are switching to cut-price alternatives from Chinese rivals. The price war comes as rising AI bills push companies to curb usage and seek cheaper models, helping Chinese developers including Moonshot and DeepSeek make inroads with users from Silicon Valley to Europe. OpenAI recently said that it was slashing prices for GPT-5.6 Luna, its “fastest and most affordable model”, by 80 percent. Anthropic has launched Claude Opus 5, touting the system’s “frontier intelligence… at half the price” of Fable 5, the company’s most capable model. The moves have helped decrease prices that customers are paying for models from leading US labs by almost a quarter since mid-July, according to Silicon Data’s token price index. Tokens are the units of data processed by language models and are used to calculate many customers’ bills. The cuts mark a shift for US AI groups that make proprietary “closed” models that have, until now, competed heavily on performance. Increasingly capable “open” Chinese models—which can be freely downloaded and tweaked by developers—have contributed to pressure on prices. The moves also come as OpenAI and Anthropic plot initial public offerings at trillion-dollar valuations while investors seek evidence that the industry’s vast spending on AI can generate returns. Corporate AI users face cost pressures as Anthropic and OpenAI shift some enterprise customers away from flat subscriptions and toward usage-based billing, under which companies pay according to the computational resources they consume. Some businesses have responded to a rise in bills by imposing caps on AI usage or testing cheaper alternatives. Companies such as DoorDash and Airbnb have said they have started to use Chinese-made models in an effort to rein in bills. That shift has coincided with a flurry of releases from Chinese labs that have narrowed the performance gap with leading US models, raising concerns in the US tech industry that American developers could lose customers even as they spend heavily to maintain their technological edge. AI labs offer a range of models with different capabilities and prices, with costs varying further according to the version of a model and the “effort” settings used. Customers are typically charged for input tokens, used to measure data fed into a model, and output tokens, which measure what it generates in response. The latest price cuts from US labs apply to mid-tier products and make them more competitive with Chinese offerings. OpenAI, for example, cut the price of GPT-5.6 Luna from $1 to $0.20 per million input tokens and from $6 to $1.20 per million output tokens. Anthropic launched Opus 5 at $5 per million input tokens and $25 per million output tokens—half the price of its Fable 5 model. This week, the company called off a planned rise in prices for its Sonnet 5 model, which had been due to take effect from September. Headline token prices do not provide a straightforward comparison between AI models, however. More capable models can sometimes complete a task using fewer tokens or with fewer attempts, meaning a model that appears more expensive based on the headline price of tokens can ultimately cost less. Additionally, most can operate at different “effort” settings, which vary the computing power used to answer a question and can affect both performance and the ultimate cost of completing a task. Artificial Analysis, which benchmarks models across areas including math, science, coding, and reasoning, found Anthropic’s Opus 5 at “medium” effort delivered similar performance and cost per task to Moonshot’s Kimi K3 at “max” effort. OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Luna at “max” effort performed similarly to DeepSeek’s V4 Flash at “max,” but cost just under twice as much per task. Anthropic and OpenAI declined to comment. A Cold War Movie That Gives Too Much Credit to Ronald Reagan

The Atlantic | 2026-08-14

Visit Original Website
Americans do not know enough about the Cold War, and much of what they think they know is wrong. After the fall of the Soviet Union in 1991, Republicans took a victory lap, rightly noting that the Soviet collapse was unimaginable when President Ronald Reagan first took office. Liberal critics, however, argued—also rightly—that the Soviet Union was a decaying, economically crippled giant by the time Reagan was inaugurated. In the years since, explanations of the ending of that great conflict have been reduced to the equivalent of partisan bumper stickers: We crushed the Kremlin in the arms race, or The U.S.S.R. imploded without any pressure from the West. In October 1986, Reagan and Soviet General Secretary Mikhail Gorbachev met to discuss nuclear-arms control in Reykjavik, Iceland. At the time, I was a young scholar in Washington, training in Soviet affairs and international relations at Georgetown University and working at the Center for Strategic and International Studies. (I was also a two-time Reagan voter.) When the Reykjavik meeting took place, no one was looking ahead to the collapse of the Soviet Union; Kremlin watchers were, mostly, relieved to see a Soviet general secretary and an American president talking with each other. The Brink of War, a new film released nationwide today, tries to bring texture and detail to those two days in Iceland. The movie relies on transcripts and other documents from the time, along with interviews with a former secretary of state, the late George Shultz (to whom the movie is dedicated). The film is a welcome corrective to the liberal orthodoxy about Reagan—specifically, that he was a disconnected simpleton who didn’t understand the complexities of the arms race. But it leans too much in the opposite direction: It is too kind to the Gipper, and takes too many liberties with the historical record. As a viewing experience, The Brink of War is a better film than the only other movie about the summit, a 1987 British production titled Breakthrough at Reykjavik. But that’s because the earlier movie (now no longer available, as far as I can tell) was more accurate—and perhaps, for some viewers, more boring. The diplomats who met in the middle of the night thought they were ridding the world of nuclear arms, and that story has plenty of drama, but The Brink of War forgoes those details in favor of needless melodrama and a focus on fewer personalities. Read: The thin line between biopic and propaganda The title itself is misleading. The world was not on the brink of war in late 1986. Soviet-American tensions were much higher, and the risk of war much greater, during Reagan’s first term, which were perhaps the most dangerous years of the Cold War since the Cuban missile crisis. In the early ’80s, the Soviet Union was led by Yuri Andropov, a former KGB chief. In 1983 alone, Reagan gave his “Evil Empire” speech and announced the start of his missile-defense plan, the Strategic Defense Initiative; the Soviets shot down a civilian Korean airliner (killing a U.S. congressman); and the Americans overthrew a Marxist regime in Grenada. Andropov died in 1984, and Gorbachev took more than a year to gain power while the Kremlin stumbled along under the caretaker regime of the grievously ill Konstantin Chernenko. Gorbachev’s arrival cooled some of these tensions. Almost a year before Reykjavik, Reagan and Gorbachev met in Geneva, where they issued a joint announcement that “a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought.” This was an important moment: Gorbachev, still consolidating power, was taking a risk by bucking anti-American hawks back in Moscow and standing with Reagan to join him in officially ruling out nuclear war as a rational instrument of policy. By the time the two men met in Iceland, they were both determined to reduce their nations’ stockpiles of nuclear weapons. For Gorbachev, this reflected both a sincere belief and an economic imperative. The Soviet Union was struggling with the costs of boneheaded decisions such as the invasion of Afghanistan and the deployment of new and extremely threatening Soviet nuclear weapons in Europe. Reagan, despite his reputation among his critics as a warmonger, was actually an abolitionist when it came to nuclear weapons, and on this, he and Gorbachev were of one mind. Both men had earlier made dramatic proposals to rid the world of nuclear arms. These were roundly dismissed in each other’s capitals as propaganda, but as it turned out, Reagan and Gorbachev were completely serious. The meeting foundered because of Reagan’s commitment to SDI, but the Reykjavik summit, despite its failure to reach any agreements, nevertheless suggested to the two leaders that they agreed about important things. The Brink of War gets much of this right, but it portrays Reagan as highly prepared and Gorbachev as somewhat nervous. In reality, Reagan was concerned about the meeting, and at the start he relied on notes and cards he had in front of him. One of Gorbachev’s first observations when they met was about all the paper in front of his American counterpart; Reagan at one point dropped all of his notes and had to shuffle through them to get back on track. The film also shows Reagan confidently teasing his negotiating team about being Democrats, which was probably done so that the movie could make the summit seem like a bipartisan effort. It’s true that a few Reaganites were once Democrats, such as the negotiator Richard Perle, but this was not a team of liberals and conservatives. Perle was so distrustful of arms control with the Soviets that his nickname in Washington in the ’80s was the “Prince of Darkness.” Soon the two leaders took up the proposal first discussed in Geneva for a 50 percent cut in Soviet- and American-weapons stockpiles. Reagan and Gorbachev, in the course of their discussion, agreed to eliminate all nuclear weapons, a move that surprised both delegations. The idea of full abolition—beloved by both leaders but not part of the Reykjavik agenda—was an improvised moment, almost an accident, and not some premeditated masterstroke (as the movie implies) by Reagan. This sudden change drove hours of unexpected negotiations. Both sides, in a very short time, got remarkably close to a major nuclear deal, because their bosses, both Soviet and American, wanted one. These American negotiators do not get much attention in the film; likewise, the Soviets, starting with Gorbachev himself, get unfair treatment in The Brink of War. The Soviet boss huddles in the bathroom with his wife, hiding from the KGB, as if he were doing something illegal and not presenting positions he’d already raised among Soviet leaders. The film also depicts Gorbachev as something of a drama queen during the meetings, slamming the table and raising his voice. At one point, when Reagan suggests sharing missile-defense technology, Gorbachev stands up and whines about how America will not even share dairy technology so that the Soviets can provide milk to their children. Reagan did make that missile-defense technology offer, and Gorbachev did in fact dismiss it. What Gorbachev actually said, however, according to records from the meeting, was more restrained: “Excuse me, Mr. President, but I do not take your idea of sharing SDI seriously. You don’t want to share even petroleum equipment, automatic machine tools or equipment for dairies.” This was a kind of sarcastic zinger from the real Gorbachev. But in any case, no Soviet leader would complain to an American president about hungry Soviet kids. It would have been especially out of character for Gorbachev, who Shultz later recalled was not whiny but rather “brisk, impatient, and confident, with the air of a man who is setting the agenda and taking charge of the meeting.” Other Soviets appear as central-casting scary Communists. The Soviet Foreign Minister Eduard Shevardnadze is played as a glowering, skeptical presence by a darkly handsome English actor. The real Shevardnadze looked more like a white-haired professor, and he supported Gorbachev’s proposals. Likewise, Marshal Sergei Akhromeyev, the senior military officer who led the Soviet negotiators, is portrayed as a difficult, stiff military man, but American negotiators, especially Paul Nitze, later spoke of him with great admiration as smart, flexible, and someone the Americans could trust. The Brink of War also presents Reykjavik as something of a guy’s poker party. Men conduct the work in the movie, but in real life, some of the important negotiations with the Soviets were led by the veteran diplomat Rozanne Ridgway, who does not appear in the film. The two most important women in the film are the wives, Raisa Gorbacheva and Nancy Reagan. Raisa is presented as a chic, smart adviser to her husband. Nancy, who stayed in Washington, is Ronnie’s faithful booster, but she also unfairly comes across as a catty flake, envying Raisa and telling the president that the stars are aligned for a successful summit. Nancy Reagan, fearful of losing her husband, did indeed consult an astrologer after Reagan was shot. But Karen Tumulty, The Washington Post’s chief political correspondent and the author of a definitive biography of Nancy, told me that Nancy was angry in October 1986 not because she felt upstaged, but because her husband’s advisers had told her that Iceland was not a “summit” and therefore that Raisa would not be there. A scene where Nancy tells Ron about the position of Mars, Tumulty added, “strikes me as preposterous.” The supreme error in The Brink of War, however, is that it wanders too far from the history of the event in order to depict a successful summit. In life, the two leaders agreed about ridding the world of nuclear weapons, but they both left Iceland full of gloom and bitterness. A deal to eliminate nuclear arms dissolved when Reagan insisted that testing for SDI had to continue. Gorbachev, hamstrung by demands from hard-liners back in Moscow, was equally intransigent that no deal could be made so long as SDI was in the works. The summit-that-was-not-a-summit failed. As the Reagan biographer Max Boot told me, both Reagan and Gorbachev left “furious and disappointed.” Tumulty cites Nancy Reagan as saying later that it was clear to her, watching from afar, that her husband, “pale” and with his “teeth clenched,” was extremely angry as he said goodbye to Gorbachev. According to declassified Soviet transcripts released years later, as the two leaders stood outside waiting to leave, Reagan accused Gorbachev of not wanting a deal in the first place, adding that he didn’t know when they would meet again. (“I don’t either,” Gorbachev said.) Ken Adelman, one of Reagan’s aides at the meeting, later wrote that Reagan, during that final conversation, recalled Gorbachev saying, “I don’t know what else I could have done.” Reagan answered: “I do. You could have said yes.” None of this makes its way into The Brink of War. Instead, the film makes much of Reagan trying to get a visa for the sister of Mstislav Rostropovich, a Soviet musical maestro who had defected to the United States. Reagan did raise this issue, and the Soviets relented, but The Brink of War makes it seem as if reaching agreement on this opened the way to more deals later. The movie ends with Reagan doubling back to try one more time on the Rostropovich issue. Gorbachev agrees, the men shake hands, and the last word of the movie is deal. This is not remotely what happened. According to Adelman, it was Gorbachev, not Reagan, who tried one more time to engage—and not on Rostropovich’s sister but on a nuclear deal. “Can’t we do something about this?” the Soviet leader asked as Reagan got ready to leave. “It’s too late,” Reagan said, and walked out. In a private moment, he stares at the scar from the 1981 attempt on his life; that night he has a fit of PTSD about the shooting as he tosses and turns. The next day, Reagan emphasizes to Gorbachev that he felt that God had spared his life for a great purpose. Whether Reagan had PTSD from nearly being murdered is a matter of speculation, but he said no such thing to Gorbachev. The movie is full of lines that sound good from Reagan but that he never said. At one point, he invites Gorbachev to “tear down this wall between us,” as if he were already thinking of the line he would use in front of the Berlin Wall a year later. Nor did he say to Gorbachev that the Soviet people were “pissed off” at their government, a line that rings especially false from Reagan, who did not approve of any kind of swearing. As Boot noted to me, the president would not even spell out the word hell in his own diaries. All of this is unfortunate, because Reykjavik was a crucial step in ending the Cold War, and The Brink of War could have captured Reagan’s admirable toughness and confidence without exaggerating either the president’s abilities or what happened in Iceland. Reagan deserves great credit for making the end of the Cold War possible not only by holding fast to his belief in the superiority of the American system but also by having the vision to see that the Cold War and its endless nuclear tensions did not need to be accepted by either side as an immutable fact of life. He came into office excoriating the Soviets, and learned—almost too late—that he had succeeded too well in scaring the Kremlin and had brought the world closer to war. Encouraged by Shultz and Nancy, he changed course during his first term, and he found a courageous partner in Gorbachev. Yet Reagan, especially at Reykjavik, was hobbled at times by rigidity and tunnel vision. At the summit, he tried to lecture Gorbachev on the bankruptcy of Marxism-Leninism—this did not go over well—and the president’s obsession with SDI tanked what might have been the most dramatic arms agreement in history. Gorbachev, too, was intransigent, but by Soviet standards he was practically a radical. Fortunately, Gorbachev would drop his objections to SDI in time for both men to sign a landmark treaty in 1987 that abolished all intermediate range nuclear arms in Europe and made the entire world safer. (Donald Trump foolishly withdrew from this treaty in 2019.) Reagan did not win the Cold War alone, but he and Gorbachev made the end of the conflict and a more secure world possible. The Brink of War, whatever dramatic license it takes, underscores that Reykjavik was part of that story. The summit itself turned out to be a failure, but Washington and Moscow later climbed over its wreckage to reach new agreements. Reykjavik did not end the Cold War, but the Cold War could not have moved toward its peaceful ending without Reykjavik and the spirit of discussion it fostered.

Healing those wounds is no small challenge. Democratic divisions have been front and center this year. (Losing another presidential election to Donald Trump will do that to a party.) Some of the divides are over ideology and policy; in last week’s Michigan Senate primary, Abdul El-Sayed, a progressive supporter of Medicare for All, narrowly beat Rep. Others—including Tuesday’s Wisconsin governor primary, in which the moderate David Crowley even more narrowly defeated Francesca Hong, a democratic socialist—are about which candidate is better equipped against Republicans or is advocating exciting new ideas. Divisive primaries happen for a reason; they can help parties work out policy disagreements or stress-test candidates’ mettle. But bridging those divides isn’t easy, and this year’s primary season could haunt Democrats in key races this fall. Political science research suggests that longer and more competitive primaries can ultimately cost the party’s nominee votes in the general election. Only one candidate can win, after all, which leaves plenty of room for hurt feelings among the losing contender’s supporters—some of whom might choose to stay home rather than vote for someone who wasn’t their first choice. Several of this year’s bitterest primaries so far also involved more than two candidates; this can split the vote such that no one gets a majority. That’s what happened in Michigan (where El-Sayed won with 49 percent of the vote) and in Wisconsin (where Crowley emerged victorious despite earning less than 40 percent). A 2024 study that looked at both parties’ House, Senate, and gubernatorial primaries from 2010 to 2022 found that candidates who win only a plurality of the vote tend to underperform in general elections relative to expectations. For plurality winners in closely divided parts of the country (like, say, Michigan and Wisconsin), “this translates into a reduced likelihood of winning,” the paper concludes. Acrimonious primaries can hurt victorious candidates in other ways, too. Sometimes the positions they take to court their party’s base actively repel the wider pool of voters, whose support they may need in the fall. Contentious primaries often bring media scrutiny, which can expose damaging truths about a candidate’s past; Hong’s years-old criticisms of policing and Thanksgiving probably helped doom her in Wisconsin. Extended primaries also eat up resources. Dollars spent on attack ads against a fellow Democrat over the summer can’t be spent on attack ads against a Republican in the fall. But just because divisive primaries can be fatal in general elections doesn’t mean they have to be. Democrats have a choice about whether to let the wounds of primary season hamstring their nominees. We can’t talk about cloning without mentioning Dolly, the celebrity sheep born in 1996 and the first mammal successfully cloned from an adult cell. In that case, scientists took the DNA-containing nucleus of an adult mammary cell from one sheep and transferred it into an egg cell that had had its own nucleus removed. The resulting embryo was transferred to a surrogate sheep, which gave birth to Dolly—an animal genetically identical to the DNA donor. The scientists behind that work were interested in genetically modifying livestock. Farmers have essentially been doing this for thousands of years through selective breeding, but cloning allows scientists to create genetic replicas of animals with desirable traits. Cloning is also being used to replicate deceased pets, including, famously, those of Barbra Streisand and Tom Brady, among others. For a price somewhere in the tens of thousands of dollars, a company can take cells from your pet and turn them into a living, breathing clone. One bioethicist, Jessica Pierce, has described this aspect of dog cloning as “the exploitation of the canine underclass.” The case for cloning is stronger when it comes to conservation—where some argue there is environmental value. Quantum computing has alternated between breakthrough darling and overhyped promise in technology circles. Its powerful new capabilities come with a threat to break current cryptography, but for business leaders navigating the noise, the signal should be clear: post-quantum cryptography (PQC) is a manageable evolution, not a crisis. The mathematics behind today's encrypted digital transactions may yield to quantum computers one day, but the transition to quantum-resistant algorithms is neither sudden nor insurmountable. For executives concerned about disruption, cost, or complexity, a structured and phased approach exists with trusted technology partners like Intel that are already beginning to deliver the infrastructure to make it possible. The "quantum threat" narrative often swings between two extremes: imminent catastrophe or distant irrelevance. The reality occupies a more pragmatic middle ground. Quantum computers are highly specialized accelerators that exploit quantum physics to solve specific hard problems. They have the potential to crack modern encryption, but they will not replace classic servers overnight, nor will they instantly break every encryption protocol on the internet. What they will do is gradually shift the security landscape, much as previous cryptographic transitions have done over the past three decades. In late 2024, the Global Risk Institute, a Toronto-based financial services think tank, surveyed 32 quantum computing experts on when a quantum computer could break a 2048-bit RSA key within 24 hours. This timeline, uncertain but measurable, creates space for deliberate planning rather than emergency reaction. The near-term focus should be on "harvest now, decrypt later" scenarios, where adversaries collect encrypted data today and then hold it for future decryption later when that capability becomes possible. For commercial enterprises, these timelines are not mandates, but could be signposts. He argues the largest players survive a reset. OpenAI and Anthropic hold significant capital reserves, on his account, and should withstand turbulence even if expectations reset around them. So the position is narrow and unusual. The revolution is real, the prices are not all real, and the biggest names are not the fragile part. That leaves an obvious question he does not answer directly. If the labs survive a reset, somebody else absorbs it, and the circular deals are where that would land. Compare that with Masayoshi Son, who told SoftBank shareholders in June that calling AI a bubble is an insult. Son has also called such commentary blasphemy. Hauser is doing something different from either camp. He is separating the technology question from the pricing question, and answering them in opposite directions. That is harder to headline and more useful to read. Hauser co-created the architecture inside most of the world’s phones, so his view on the next one carries weight. He thinks AI is forcing a rethink of computing architecture itself. The pressures are practical. AI is expensive to run, chips are difficult to cool, memory is expensive, and bottlenecks run across the industry. He pointed at two responses. In-memory computing and photonic computing both aim to cut the energy spent moving data between processors and memory. His comparison is the striking bit. Those changes could matter as much as the architectural breakthroughs that let Arm challenge the established chipmakers. “I never thought that we’d have a very fundamental change in the computer architecture as a result of AI,” he said. The photonics half of that is not theoretical. Nvidia spent $6.5bn across photonics companies in three months to replace copper with light inside AI data centres. That is a bottleneck being bought out rather than engineered around. The problem Hauser describes has a price attached already. In-memory computing is further back. It moves the arithmetic into the memory rather than shuttling data to a processor and back. Both are attempts at the same target. The cost of moving data, rather than the cost of the maths. Arm itself is the precedent Hauser is reaching for. It won on power efficiency at a moment when the industry was optimising for raw speed, and the constraint created the opening. On the continent’s prospects Hauser is direct in both directions. European companies have the innovation and the skill to compete with the US and China. The failure is at the next stage. European companies struggle to grow from a small startup into a genuinely global competitor. That is not a new diagnosis, and coming from an investor who has funded the attempt it is a pointed one. Amadeus Capital exists to make that jump happen. He connects it to something larger he keeps returning to. Technological sovereignty. Hauser worries Europe depends on foreign suppliers for critical technologies. His range runs from AI models to semiconductor design software. That second item is the one most people skip. Chip design software is a small market with few suppliers, and it sits upstream of everything else. The desk has covered that layer moving. Synopsys pulled back from chip fab software in July to chase AI design margins. His caveat is as firm as his warning. Close cooperation with allies is essential, and dependence still carries risks in an era of geopolitical tension and export controls. Export controls are the mechanism that turns a supplier into a chokepoint. A tool you can buy today is a dependency, not a purchase. His conclusion was blunt, and it is the quotable one. Europe should preserve its partnership with the US, but it should not become “a technology colony of the U.S.” Brussels has been acting on that fear already. The Commission proposed a sovereignty package in June restricting US cloud providers from handling sensitive government data, alongside a Chips Act 2.0. Hauser is not arguing for a break. He is arguing that partnership and dependence are different arrangements, and Europe has been treating them as the same one. Three things, and the first is the architecture claim. In-memory and photonic computing are testable, and Nvidia has already priced part of the answer. The second is whether a European company makes the jump he says none of them make. That is a question about growth capital and management, not about research. The third is the rollercoaster.
