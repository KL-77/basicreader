Pennsylvania issues alert on congenital measles—something US hasn't seen for decades

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-19

Visit Original Website
Pennsylvania’s health department issued a health alert Monday as cases of measles in pregnancy and, in turn, cases of congenital measles have continued to increase. Before the current outbreak—which continues to rage across the state—congenital measles cases hadn’t been seen in decades, the department noted in its advisory. The alert included detailed clinical guidance for managing measles exposures and cases during pregnancy as well as cases in newborns. Many doctors have never seen such cases in their careers given the decades of successful use of the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine. But now, with vaccination rates slipping nationwide, the highly infectious virus is better able to get footholds in communities and spread rapidly through under-vaccinated pockets, as is happening in Pennsylvania. As of October 19, Pennsylvania had reported 1,286 measles cases this year across 42 counties. Of those, 247 have been hospitalized, and six people have reportedly died (though the latest reported death is still pending an investigation by the state health department before it is added to the state tally). The Pennsylvania health department did not immediately respond to a request for information from Ars Technica. But reporting from The 19th earlier this month put the number of measles cases during pregnancy at 53. At least one case of congenital measles led to a death; the first death reported in the current outbreak was in a newborn baby boy, who died immediately after birth while his mother was severely ill with measles. Pregnant people who have not been vaccinated against measles nor previously had the infection are susceptible. A case of measles during pregnancy increases the risk of hospitalization, pneumonia, the need for oxygen support or mechanical ventilation, and death. It also increases the risk of miscarriage, stillbirth, low birth weight, and preterm labor. The MMR vaccine is not recommended during pregnancy because it is a live attenuated vaccine and poses a theoretical risk of causing an infection in a fetus. If a pregnant person is exposed to the virus, they’re recommended to receive intravenous immunoglobulin to help protect against infection—but only if it is given within six days of exposure. If there’s a measles infection during pregnancy, the measles virus can pass to a fetus in utero or can transmit during or just after birth, leading to a congenital case. These cases can vary widely in symptoms and severity. Some babies will remain asymptomatic while others develop severe, multisystem disease and death. Some common symptoms of congenital measles include fever and jaundice. They can also develop the telltale measles rash, but it is sometimes delayed or atypical given a newborn’s underdeveloped immune system. Other symptoms can include low platelet counts, pneumonia, and neurologic complications. Even if a newborn survives the acute infection, they are at risk of long-term complications. These include immune amnesia, a type of immunosuppression in which the measles virus destroys immune cells and adaptive responses. They are also at risk of subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE), a progressive, fatal neurological disorder that can strike years after an infection. The alert warns that babies with congenital measles need long-term clinical follow-up and monitoring for these neurologic and immunologic complications, and parents need counseling on how to spot early signs of SSPE, which include seizures, regression in milestones, and academic difficulties in older children.

A Russian military satellite maneuvered in “extreme proximity” of a commercial Earth-imaging satellite in recent days in the first known example an unsanctioned rendezvous in low-Earth orbit, according to the orbital intelligence company LeoLabs. The Russian spacecraft, Kosmos 2612, “conducted a deliberate series of maneuvers toward ICEYE-X36, a US-operated commercial imaging satellite,” LeoLabs said. “Kosmos 2612 closed from hundreds of kilometers away to extreme proximity to ICEYE-X36. It then maneuvered around ICEYE-X36, remained nearby for an extended period, and then began moving away.” US military officials and independent analysts have tracked Kosmos 2612 since its April launch on a Soyuz rocket from a spaceport in northern Russia. Within weeks, it became apparent that at least four of the six satellites from that launch were stalking ICEYE-X36 after they adjusted orbits to line up with the orbit of the ICEYE spacecraft. The situation remained stable until last week, when Kosmos 2612 began rapidly moving toward ICEYE-X36. By Friday, Kosmos 2612 was holding station 2 to 3 kilometers away before shifting positions ahead of and behind the ICEYE satellite, LeoLabs said. The satellites were within 500 meters (1,640 feet) of one another over the weekend, and “likely quite a bit closer than that,” said Adam Marsh, LeoLabs’ vice president of technical solutions. LeoLabs operates a global network of radars to track objects as small as 2 centimeters, about the size of a marble, in low-Earth orbit. The proximity of Kosmos 2612 and ICEYE-X36 “occasionally made it difficult to distinguish the two spacecraft independently,” LeoLabs said. “This was not a routine conjunction or ‘fly by,’” LeoLabs said. “The sequence included a controlled approach, changes in relative position, close-proximity loitering, additional small maneuvers, and a controlled departure. The behavior is consistent with sophisticated rendezvous and proximity operations (RPO).” Satellites often fly near one another in orbit, usually by happenstance and at high closing velocities. Russia has previously deployed satellites in orbits close to the US government’s most sophisticated and secret spy satellites. The difference this time is that Russia’s target was a commercial satellite. Kosmos 2612 got much closer to ICEYE-X36, then stayed a while. The deliberate nature of the Russian maneuvers makes this weekend’s event “strategically important,” LeoLabs said. “To my knowledge, this is the first LEO rendezvous of two active satellites where the owner of one of the sats (satellites) did not give approval,” Jonathan McDowell, an astrophysicist who tracks global launch and satellite activity, wrote on X. “There are parallels that have happened in geosynchronous orbit, but they’re not even close to the same as this,” Marsh told Ars. “As far as this kind of event, rendezvousing in, staying there for a few days, and getting very, very close in low-Earth orbit, this is the first time we’ve ever seen this without consent. We’ve seen them test it with their own satellites before, but not like this.” As of Monday, Kosmos 2612 is moving away from the ICEYE satellite at a relative velocity of about 10 mph (17 kilometers per hour), according to Marsh. ICEYE is a Finnish company with a fleet of high-resolution radar imaging satellites capable of resolving features on Earth’s surface through cloud cover or darkness, which inhibit conventional optical reconnaissance satellites. The company has launched 80 satellites to date, and four more ICEYE satellites are set to launch this week on a SpaceX rideshare mission. The company’s US subsidiary, ICEYE US, owns the ICEYE-X36 satellite. ICEYE satellites provide imagery to numerous military users, including Ukraine, which has used ICEYE’s “near-real-time space-based intelligence” since the Russian invasion in 2022. A Russian government official in 2022 said civilian satellites supporting Ukraine’s military are a “legitimate target.” Taking out a single satellite in a constellation of dozens would have little effect on ICEYE’s capacity to support its customers, which also include the US military and several European defense ministries. “We are aware of the situation and tracking it closely,” an ICEYE US spokesperson told Ars. “Our constellation architecture is designed for continuity and resilience.” Short time-lapse of part of this morning’s first pass of ICEYE-X36 (59103) and COSMOS 2612 (68762) over South America under partly cloudy skies. https://t.co/t8RhwmKcV4 — s2a systems (@s2a_systems) October 18, 2026 Gen. Stephen Whiting, head of US Space Command, said in April that it was “evident” that Russia is operationalizing anti-satellite weapons. US government leaks have also suggested Russia is eyeing the placement of a nuclear weapon in orbit. The US military disclosed last month that it, too, has placed weapons in space, without elaborating. There were no reports of physical damage to the ICEYE-X36 satellite by the time Kosmos 2612 started its retreat Sunday. But getting so close to a US-owned satellite with military utility was a provocation, said retired Lt. Gen. John Shaw, a former deputy commander of US Space Command. “So far, the best interpretation of the Russian activity is clear messaging that they will intentionally conduct aggressive activity near platforms they perceive as actual or potential threats to their interests,” Shaw said. “A broader point is the acknowledgment this activity in the space domain is not at all different from what we often see Russia conduct in the other domains—close-in aggressive and provocative behavior.” Russian Air Force aircraft routinely fly in a buffer zone near US and Canadian airspace, and fighter jets downed a US military drone over the Black Sea in 2023. “If the Russians were willing to do this to an unmanned US military platform, it would seem their threshold for attacking an ‘unmanned’ commercial space platform cannot be too different,” Shaw said.

Batteries help reduce emissions, but how do we reduce their emissions?

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-19

Visit Original Website
The first order of business for the energy transition is electrifying applications like vehicles and heating and making sure that the electricity that powers them is made using renewables. But once the dominant slices of the emissions pie have shrunk, other categories with less potential to reduce emissions become important. A new study led by Haiwei Zhou at the University of Melbourne focused on the emissions associated with making lithium-ion batteries, which many parts of the energy transition rely on. Those emissions aren’t zero, so as we use more and more batteries, that number will grow. Right now, the researchers estimate that these emissions only account for 0.18 percent of annual greenhouse gas releases, but they could increase by a factor of 10 or more by 2050. There are a number of ways to reduce the emissions required to make a battery. Some elements used in various lithium-ion battery chemistries are more energy-intensive to mine and refine than others. The battery manufacturing process involves many steps that could be made more energy-efficient. And then there’s recycling, which can put battery materials back into the loop while requiring much less energy than mining. But the researchers wanted to compare options on the demand side of the equation. Rather than shaving the per-battery carbon footprint, this means reducing the number of batteries we need to make, avoiding their emissions entirely. The team used projections of emissions throughout the life cycle of batteries used for electric vehicles (EVs) and energy storage through 2050. This includes regional differences in demand and electricity generation, using International Energy Agency scenarios for the future. The estimates of emissions savings, then, aren’t just compared to the current state of industries, but to industries in a future world with more batteries, more end-of-life batteries to be recycled, and a cleaner electrical grid. In total, efforts to clean up battery production could reduce the carbon footprint of a given battery by 34 percent by 2050, they found. Between 2024 and 2050, that would avoid somewhere between 3.5 and 4.7 billion tons of CO2. Reducing demand isn’t a “just say no to batteries” story. There are simple strategies that limit the number of batteries you need to make. Ensuring that EV batteries last as long as the average vehicle reduces the need for replacements, cutting battery demand by 29 percent in this analysis. And limiting the ongoing growth in average EV battery capacity—driven by preferences for longer range and larger vehicles—could reduce demand by another 23 percent compared to the current trend. Additionally, adoption of services like ride-sharing could reduce demand by almost 10 percent, while repurposing end-of-life vehicle batteries for energy storage could contribute another 8 percent. Together, all of this would eliminate 6–7.2 billion tons of CO2 between 2024 and 2050—a fair bit more than the supply chain improvements. For context, burning fossil fuels produced about 38 billion tons in 2025. Battery production is a small slice of the emissions pie, but getting to net-zero emissions means tackling many small sources as well as the biggest ones. There is a lot we can do to produce cleaner electricity and to reduce the environmental footprint of batteries. But the battery you don’t make has zero footprint, so the benefits of any reduction in demand add up fast. One Earth, 2026. DOI: 10.1016/j.oneear.2026.101863 (About DOIs).

Recently fixed vulnerabilities in the Plex Media Server are under active exploitation in attacks that, when successful, install malware, including cryptominers and residential proxy apps, a researcher said. Plex fixed four vulnerabilities at the start of last month. Three weeks later, published CVE designations revealed five vulnerabilities, with one of them carrying a severity rating of 8.6 out of 10. A researcher last week revealed an exploit that chained four of them together to achieve remote code execution of the device running a vulnerable Plex version. The researcher named the chain “Plex2Shell.” The new details allowed a separate researcher to create a honeypot that could track attempts in the wild to exploit the vulnerability chain. Within a day, the researcher began seeing active scanning of the simulated Plex server and eventually successful attempts to infect devices. So far, successful attacks are installing two payloads. One is a cryptominer, which uses the device to mine cryptocurrency and deposit it into a wallet controlled by the attacker. The other payload is residential proxy software, which allows people to anonymize their Internet activity (much of it illegal) by connecting to the infected box and then using it to send the user’s traffic to a final destination. “Plex may be hosted on residential IP [sic] and I think threat actors would love to have residential IP proxy to access corporate VPNs and emails,” the second researcher wrote. The published exploit chain was part of a proof-of-concept attack that the first researcher included when submitting a bug report to Plex. The complex code completes a series of steps, including performing an unauthenticated SSRF, a loopback pivot, a security token theft, a payload delivery, and finally the execution of the payload. The bug finder said he used AI “heavily” to develop the exploit chain. Steps included: “This was not a matter of telling the LLM ‘go hack Plex,’” the researcher wrote. “It was hours of iterative work, dead ends, and reprioritization. All of the judgment calls on what to look for and what to prioritize were mine. The LLM speeds this process up significantly, allowing me to stay at a higher level and simply guide the process.” Plex representatives didn’t immediately respond to questions. Anyone using Plex should ensure it’s running version 1.43.3. The software can be configured to install updates automatically, or users can manually download and install them. Given the active exploitation, outdated versions should be updated immediately. Beyond that, users should devote care to properly securing the media servers. The safest option is to restrict its access to local networks with no exposure to the Internet. This option prevents being able to use many useful features, though. One option when connecting a device to the open Internet is to route traffic through a reverse proxy, instead of using the default port 32400. Users should also use only secure Plex apps. And of course, strong passwords are a must.

Startup taps underwater volcano as energy source in "proof point" test

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-19

Visit Original Website
The world’s first demonstration of a subsea geothermal power plant has taken place near an active underwater volcano almost a mile below the ocean surface. It generated less than 10 seconds of net-positive electricity but showed the technical feasibility of tapping another form of renewable energy underwater. The three-week test deployment of the 3-ton prototype power plant took place at the Axial Seamount in the northeast Pacific Ocean in September. Endurance Energy, the Seattle-based startup behind the effort, aims to eventually commercialize subsea geothermal power for Pacific island nations and other customers looking for alternatives to importing expensive fossil fuels. “The deep ocean gives us unlimited cold seawater for cooling, and its weight keeps the system and the well pressurized, so our plants can be compact,” said Andrew Redd, founder and CEO of Endurance Energy, in an email to Ars. But efforts to tap geothermal power on the seafloor face many different challenges from land-based geothermal projects, which can also add up to higher installation and operational costs. So Endurance Energy must demonstrate consistent underwater operations as well as prove the approach can be cost-effective. “It’s probably technically feasible, but it would undoubtedly be more expensive than to do it on land,” said Roland Horne, professor of energy science & engineering at Stanford University, in an emailed response to Ars. Endurance Energy’s deployment required a subsea lander drill to deploy from the ship Ocean Guardian—operated by Stabbert Maritime—and drill two holes in the seafloor at a depth of about 5,000 feet (1,524 meters). That means the test took place at a depth exceeding 2.5 times the height of the One World Trade Center building in New York City, which is the tallest building in the United States. Both the drilled holes started venting hot hydrothermal fluid measured at a temperature of about 500° F (260° C). The Endurance Energy team proceeded to case and cement the second hole before installing a valve to create a well. Next up, the company deployed its 100-kilowatt power plant, named Adélie, that is designed to vaporize incoming hydrothermal fluid to spin a turbine and drive an electric generator. It used the assistance of Canada’s remotely operated submersible ROPOS to help connect the power plant to the newly drilled well. Adélie also connected to Endurance Energy’s Seattle command center by plugging into the Regional Cabled Array, a 559-mile (900-kilometer) network of undersea fiber-optic and electric cables that provides both power and connectivity to scientific sensors on the seafloor. The undersea network is funded by the US National Science Foundation and is operated by the University of Washington. That all set the stage for the Adélie power test. But a major hitch soon arose—the drilled well extending 52 feet (16 meters) down into the seafloor was not delivering enough of the hydrothermal fluid to run the power plant. “For our first well, the goal was to show we could drill, case, cement, and cap a well on the seafloor, so we kept it small and shallow,” Redd explained. “That limited how much fluid it could deliver, and deeper wells should change that.” The test well was also just 2 inches (5 centimeters) wide, compared to oil, gas, and onshore geothermal wells that typically have casings 20 to 36 inches (51 to 91 centimeters) across. Endurance Energy plans to avoid this issue in the future by drilling deeper wells to access more fluid. The company’s current lander drill can reach about 260 feet (80 meters), which would be five times deeper than the initial test well. To try running the Adélie power plant anyway, the team redeployed it to a nearby natural vent where hydrothermal fluid was already flowing out at about 538° F (281° C). On September 27, they gave it a try. Over nearly half a minute, the power plant turbine reached about 26,000 rpm and enabled the generator to produce 60 watt-hours of electricity. The power plant also became net-positive by producing more electricity than it consumed for about 8.7 seconds. It produced a median of about 2 kilowatts of net power and peaked at 16.5 kilowatts of net power. “It’s the proof point,” Redd told Ars. “Net-positive is the threshold that matters: the plant made more power than it took to run itself on the seafloor, which no one had done before.” But then a software issue brought the power run to a halt. Before the generator takes over, Adélie first relies on a battery to run its pumps, thrusters, and controls, along with spinning the turbine to get it up to speed, Redd explained. A battery module recorded a brief spike in current during the spin-up process and then stopped reporting. That prompted Adélie’s software to automatically stop the power plant as a safety precaution, because it kept relying on the old reading from the battery module. When the team was restarting the power plant, a power cable feeding the refrigerant pump also failed when an imperfection in the cable casing allowed seawater to seep in and short two of the electrical conductors. That ended the trial run, although Endurance Energy already plans to use the experience to improve how it builds and tests subsea cables. Placing the geothermal plant on top of a natural hydrothermal vent was a one-time test for purely research and demonstration purposes, Redd said. His company still plans to rely on drilled wells in future deployments. Endurance Energy also described taking steps to reduce potential environmental impacts on the seafloor and marine life. The team described choosing the “least disruptive” site for the test run and continuously monitored the process by sampling the water and recording the seafloor array of tubeworms, snails, crabs, and bacterial mats. It also checked findings against scientific sensors run by independent researchers and sent water, rock, and mineral samples for independent lab analysis. The company’s next trial run planned for the winter of 2026—2027 will involve a refurbished and upgraded Adélie—renamed Gentoo in keeping with the penguin-inspired naming—that will aim to sustain higher power generation. The company is also developing a larger 2-megawatt commercial generator for its first planned commercial deployment with the Kingdom of Tonga in 2027. The Polynesian island nation in the South Pacific Ocean runs its power grid primarily on imported diesel fuel, which has significantly increased in price since the United States and Israel launched their war with Iran. “The 2 MW commercial generators will be about the size of a shipping container, and we are deploying up to six of these generators [to] offshore Tonga,” Redd told Ars. “The physical footprint of this project will be relatively small.” Geothermal energy projects are already cost-effective on land but “on the edge of competitive pricewise,” said Horne at Stanford University. So he cautioned that any factors making geothermal more expensive would “push it to the noncompetitive side.” Still, other customers may consider Endurance Energy’s approach if the company can demonstrate reliable operations over time with the Kingdom of Tonga. The skyrocketing cost of imported fossil fuels is a huge factor that has already spurred multiple governments to invest in more renewable energy alternatives. “Large markets with ample offshore geothermal resources include the United States—Pacific Northwest and Alaska—Taiwan, and Japan,” Redd said. “As the technology matures, we expect to compete on cost and deliver continuous, weather-independent power to utilities and large energy users.”

Is there a hidden bias in which species researchers choose to study?

Silicon Republic | 2026-10-19

Visit Original Website
Pietro Pollo and Michael Kasumovic of UNSW explore the external factors that can often dictate which species and organisms are studied and which are ignored. A version of this article was originally published by The Conversation (CC BY-ND 4.0) Our knowledge about life on Earth is built through research on how organisms function, evolve and interact with each other and the environment. Yet there are far more species on our planet than scientists can study in detail. So how should scientists decide which species to focus on? Fairly representing all species seems like a useful approach. It’s like predicting an election result – we can’t interview every citizen, so we need a sample that truly represents the overall population. What drives scientists to uncover nature’s mysteries is curiosity. So, the organisms chosen for research are determined by what makes scientists most curious. And that’s causing a problem with fair representation. In our new research, we examined research in ecology and evolution (an umbrella term for biological sciences outside biomedicine) to understand which animals are most studied and why. Our results suggest emotion plays a bigger role in research choices than scientists usually admit. Our world is mostly filled with invertebrates: creatures such as bugs, spiders, worms and slugs. Vertebrates (amphibians, birds, fish, mammals and reptiles) make up only 5pc of all animal species on Earth. So, if scientists want to represent animals fairly in their work, most research should be on invertebrates. To understand how scientists’ time is spent researching the diversity of animals, we analysed almost 1.4m scientific articles from more than 600 journals. Unsurprisingly, we found that more than half of these articles focused on vertebrates, demonstrating a huge bias in what scientists prefer to study. Our results also demonstrate things haven’t improved in more than two decades (when this bias was first discovered), and in some cases have gotten worse. For example, the focus on fish in marine and freshwater biology research increased from 35.8pc of articles in 2001 to 49.7pc in 2025. This was all at the expense of the largest group of marine creatures, aquatic invertebrates such as molluscs, crustaceans, corals and more. The bias we found was compounded by social media and news coverage, where popular animal groups attracted more attention from other scientists and the general public. Many scientists are therefore no different to everyone else in which animals they deem interesting or important. Although many factors determine which research organisms scientists might select, emotions do play a significant role. Using an online survey, we asked 868 animal behaviour researchers about their experiences and preferences. We found that popular groups in animal behaviour research, such as birds and mammals, were regarded more favourably than others, especially arachnids (such as spiders). This is perhaps unsurprising, as perceptions of animals are shaped early in childhood. Ingrained societal values, such as which animals are cute, tend to persist by the time someone becomes a scientist. Fortunately, we found that familiarity breeds admiration. Participants that worked with a certain animal group tended to view that group more positively after their experience. In other words, they tried it and liked it. Studying the saliva of the Gila monster (a lizard) in the 1980s paved the way for recently developed weight loss drugs such as Ozempic. Research on snake, spider and scorpion toxins is being used for new pain-relieving drugs. The discovery of green fluorescent proteins from bioluminescent jellyfish in the 1960s led to the 2008 Nobel Prize because it sparked a scientific revolution by making it possible to tag individual cells. These examples show that scientific advancement often requires serendipity and time. It disregards whether the organisms involved are charismatic. Creatures commonly seen as ugly, disgusting, or dangerous (which they rarely are) can be as, if not more, useful for society as fluffy, pretty animals. Our work indicates that society’s negative perceptions of certain organisms, which are often rooted in disconnection from nature and misinformation, are shared by the very scientists responsible for understanding biological processes and patterns. Understanding scientists’ choices matters because these decisions shape the direction of scientific progress. We urgently need to shift our perception of animals because human-made effects are accelerating biodiversity losses. For example, insects are declining at unprecedented rates, but we know little (if anything) about most insect species. Many haven’t even been described and may go extinct before we even know they existed. This means we’ll lose not just the benefits they bring to ecosystems, but any benefits they may have held for humanity. There is hope. Our research shows that when people spend more time with the ‘ugly’ and ‘scary’ animals, they often appreciate them more. Once people realise that all organisms (even spiders) play a vital part in ecosystems, it becomes a lot easier to give these overlooked creatures the attention they deserve. Organisations like Invertebrates Australia and many social media influencers are already helping by showing just how fascinating and important invertebrates really are. But we still need more support to reduce research biases, especially from governments and universities. They can make a huge difference by funding research on never-before-studied species and neglected animal groups. If nothing changes, we risk slowing progress because we’re drawn to what looks cute. Next time an animal video pops up on your feed, try sharing something beyond the fluffy favourites. The less glamorous species are just as essential and just as worthy of our curiosity. It can pass from mother to child during birth and can spread through contact with infected blood or semen. Among babies infected before they turn 1, up to 90% develop a chronic, potentially lifelong infection, for which there are treatments but no cure. People may not even know they have the virus until their liver fails or they develop cancer, years later. Early-in-life hepatitis B infections, left untreated, lead to a 1 in 4 lifetime chance of developing liver cancer. In the U.S., hepatitis B vaccination became routinely recommended for all infants in 1991; later guidance specified that the first dose should happen within 24 hours of birth. However, in Dec. 2025, the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) under the Trump Administration moved to cease recommending this practice for babies born to mothers who test negative, instead advising a shared decision-making model where parents and clinicians decide together what is medically appropriate: a dose shortly after birth, or a first dose "no earlier than 2 months of age." (For babies born to mothers who test positive or whose status is unknown, the CDC continues to recommend a dose shortly after birth.) A new study, published today in Annals of Internal Medicine, uses computer modeling to simulate the hepatitis B infection status of an entire year’s worth of children born in the U.S. under the new recommendations over their first 20 years of life. The researchers find that if only about 26% of children are vaccinated—an estimate of what would occur under shared decision-making—chronic hepatitis B cases would rise by 225%. The American Association of Pediatrics continues to recommend vaccination at birth against hepatitis B for all infants, and 15 states have sued the Trump Administration over vaccination schedule changes. “It's been challenged in many states and by professional societies, but we wanted to add some evidence to the conversation about what the potential impact of those changes could be,” says Dr. Amir Mohareb, an infectious-disease researcher at Harvard Medical School and Massachusetts General Hospital who is an author of the new study. The simulation modeled how many of 3.6 million children, born the same year in the U.S., would develop hepatitis B under a number of scenarios, including if the former CDC recommendations continued to be followed, if the initial dose (also called a “birth dose”) was delayed, and if a shared decision-making scenario was employed. The model suggests that if the first dose is delayed until 2 months old, and only 75% of infants get it, then there would be a 19% increase in chronic cases compared to if every child received their first dose at birth. With a 26% vaccination rate at 2 months, the number climbed to 225%. This study modeled only at a single year of births, notes Mahoreb. “Imagine that happening year after year after year after year. That could quickly become thousands and thousands of new infections, and even a small number of new infections could result in lifelong chronic infection that could be detrimental to families, lead to more cases of cancer, and [become] costly to the health care system,” Mohareb says. Two other recent modeling studies have looked at the impacts of changing hepatitis B vaccine recommendations. One found that if vaccination no longer occurred at birth, a substantial increase in screening pregnant women for the virus would be necessary to keep infection numbers in infants down. Another, looking at the economic ramifications, found that even if every child eventually received the vaccine, delaying the first dose by 2 months yielded an additional $16.4 million in health care spending on new infections. “All three of these studies complement each other, in a good way, and provide some modicum of evidence so that we don't need to risk doing a clinical trial or testing this on people,” Mahoreb says. “We have done those experiments.” Decades ago, he continues, clinical trials already proved the efficacy of the existing approach, “so that we would have the benefit now of advancing medical progress and not needing to reopen that question.”

Update your preferences in Account Settings Jalen Hurts is expected to miss the rest of the NFL season after doctors diagnosed blood clots in both lungs, ESPN and NFL Network reported. The Philadelphia Eagles quarterback has deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and bilateral pulmonary emboli, according to an Oct. 19 statement from the team. He is clinically stable and has no significant functional limitations, they said, but needs blood thinners—and can’t participate in contact activities, including football, while taking them. “Jalen’s prognosis is very good, and we expect a full recovery,” the statement said. The news raises questions beyond football: How do you recognize a blood clot? What does treatment involve? And why can some people stop blood thinners after a few months while others take them for life? We asked doctors who aren’t involved in Hurts’ care what everyone should know. Deep vein thrombosis, or DVT, occurs when a clot forms in a vein deep inside the body, often in the leg. Those veins carry blood back toward the heart. They’re different from superficial blood clots——Hurts’ original diagnosis—which form closer to the skin, explains Dr. Geoffrey Barnes, a cardiologist and vascular medicine specialist at University of Michigan Health. The distinction matters because deep-vein clots are more likely to break loose and travel through the heart into the lungs. Once a clot lodges in an artery there, it causes a pulmonary embolism. DVT often causes pain, swelling, and redness or a darker skin color. Patients commonly tell Barnes, “My entire calf and ankle are swollen.” One leg might look noticeably different from the other. An injury can make those symptoms easier to dismiss. “If you’re worried that you have a DVT, it’s important that you get medical care quickly,” Barnes says. Seek a same-day evaluation through your doctor, urgent care, or an emergency department rather than waiting to see whether it goes away. Unexplained shortness of breath, chest pain, coughing up blood, or fainting require emergency care because they can signal a pulmonary embolism. A dry cough without a cold, pain when taking a deep breath, or a racing heart can also be clues, says Dr. Stephan Moll, a hematologist and professor of medicine at the University of North Carolina who runs an Athletes and Blood Clots Clinic. Some pulmonary embolisms cause only mild symptoms—or none at all. People can mistake the discomfort for bronchitis, pneumonia, or a strained muscle, Moll says. Clots become more common with age. Other risk factors include surgery, injury, prolonged inactivity, cancer, certain inherited conditions, and estrogen-containing medications. But young, active people can develop them, too. To check for a leg clot, doctors usually use ultrasound. They move a handheld device over the skin to get pictures of the veins underneath, gently pressing as they go. A normal vein should flatten under that pressure. With a clot, “you can’t squeeze it closed because that solid clot is in the way,” Barnes says. Doctors typically use a specialized CT scan to look for clots in the lungs. Finding them in both lungs doesn’t, by itself, establish how sick someone is, Barnes says. Doctors also check oxygen levels, blood pressure, and whether the heart is struggling to pump blood through the lungs. Pulmonary embolisms range from mild cases to medical emergencies, Moll emphasizes. Some people need treatment in the hospital, including medication that dissolves clots or a procedure to remove them. Many others can be treated with anticoagulants, commonly called blood thinners. Blood thinners help keep clots from growing and prevent new ones from forming. “They do not dissolve blood clots,” Moll says. Your body gradually breaks down the existing clot, though some people are left with scar tissue. Many people with DVT can take medication at home, Barnes says. Some people with lower-risk pulmonary embolisms can, too. Treatment for DVT or pulmonary embolisms generally lasts at least three months. “For the first three months it’s uninterrupted blood thinners,” Moll says. After that, doctors weigh the chance of another clot against the risk of bleeding from continued medication. What caused the clot helps guide that decision. Someone who developed one after surgery may be able to stop once treatment is complete and that temporary risk has passed. “But if this kind of came out of the blue,” Barnes says, longer-term treatment may make sense. Cancer, continued immobility, and certain clotting disorders can also affect the decision. Having a scan that looks better doesn’t automatically mean it’s time to stop medication, Moll says. Doctors generally follow symptoms rather than ordering repeated scans to watch a clot disappear. Even someone who stops daily medication may need a prevention plan for future surgery or another period of increased risk. “Risk factors vary, and the length of treatment varies, and the recovery varies,” Moll says. A blood-clot diagnosis doesn’t necessarily mean you need to stay on the couch. “I’m not asking them to go for a jog or run a marathon,” Barnes says. “But I want them to be up and walking around as much as they can tolerate,” gradually doing more every day. Getting tackled is another matter. Blood thinners make bleeding harder to stop, so a collision or fall—especially one involving a head injury—can become much more dangerous. When it comes to contact sports like football, “I would not let an athlete play on blood thinners,” Moll says. For some athletes who need long-term treatment, specialists may consider a carefully managed, intermittent medication schedule after the initial three months, Moll says. It isn’t suitable for everyone and requires expert oversight; patients shouldn’t skip doses to play sports. Ask your doctor which activities you can safely return to and when. Keeping your legs moving can also help reduce the risk of future clots. On long flights, Barnes recommends getting up to walk every one to two hours. While you’re seated, pretend your big toe is a pen and trace the alphabet with it to get your ankle moving and help pump blood through your legs. “You can do that just sitting in your seat. Nobody knows,” he says. If you’ve recently had a clot, check with your doctor before flying. Some people feel much better relatively quickly. Others experience lasting damage to the veins after DVT, which can leave blood struggling to flow back up the leg. That can result in what doctors call post-thrombotic syndrome: “chronic swelling, chronic pain, chronic leg heaviness,” Moll says. After a pulmonary embolisms, some people continue to have shortness of breath, chest discomfort, or fatigue, particularly if the clots haven’t fully cleared. Depending on what’s causing the symptoms, treatment might include compression stockings for leg swelling, specialized rehabilitation, or a procedure to address damaged veins or lingering clots. Tell your doctor if those symptoms linger. “We have treatments that we can offer to try and make that better,” Barnes says.

Putin Is Throwing Trump a Bone—but It’s Laced With Poison

Russian President Vladimir Putin has thrown Donald Trump a bone that’s so big and juicy, Trump must feel sorely tempted to chase it—but it’s laced with so much poison that even Trump might calm himself and resist. The bone, according to the Wall Street Journal, is the proposal of a tripartite summit—just Putin, Trump, and Chinese President Xi Jinping—to plot an ending to the Russia–Ukraine war. Clearly the idea appeals to Trump’s idealized view of the world: great powers carving their spheres, calling the shots, collecting the spoils, and Donald J. Trump taking his seat—recognized by all—as one of those great powers. No small or medium-size democracies need apply—thus no other pesky Europeans, and certainly no Ukrainians, need take part in determining their fates. The view is of a piece with Trump’s adoration and envy of men holding absolute power. He basks in their reciprocated respect (whether feigned or genuine), feels his own power elevated, and yearns for more. During the first year of his first term as president, Trump spoke in awe of North Korean dictator Kim Jong-un: “He speaks and people sit up at attention. I want my people to do the same.” He was similarly enamored by the red carpet and sword dance at his first meeting with the Saudi royal family. These were people who ruled a country, the heirs to generations of rulers. By comparison, meeting with elected elites, whose fleeting power depends on the whims of the populace, reminds him all too much of his own limitations. So a summit of the era’s three great men—Putin, Xi, and Trump! That’s the stuff of history: like Churchill, Stalin, and Roosevelt at Yalta, or Metternich, Talleyrand, and Tsar Alexander at the Congress of Vienna. But any such comparisons are mere self-glorifying fantasy, for several reasons but two in particular. First, and most obvious, Trump is no Roosevelt or Talleyrand (nor, it should be noted, does either of his collaborators resemble Churchill or Metternich). Second, and equally problematic, Trump would enter such a game not merely outmatched but holding (to borrow one of his own metaphors) no cards. The 19th-century statesmen gathered at Vienna to stabilize Europe in the aftermath of Napoleon’s ravaging. The three main Allied victors of World War II met at Yalta in 1945 to carve new European borders after the Nazis’ defeat. The great-powers summit that Putin proposes is a very different thing. Putin started the war against Ukraine and continues to wage it relentlessly. Xi isn’t quite an ally—he isn’t assisting Putin with troops or weapons—but he is providing Russia with technology and economic support. Trump could exert some leverage by threatening to send Ukraine tons of missiles, planes, and drones—or upping sanctions against Russia—if Putin continues the war. But as everyone plainly sees, Trump has cut off aid to Ukraine and waived the sanctions that Congress passed by a strong bipartisan majority. Trump’s leanings toward Putin have long been clear, as has his hostility toward Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky. But neither sentiment has been unambiguous—he has helped Ukraine with arms and intelligence, and he has punished Russia with sanctions—until now. As has been noted even by Zelensky, who has gone out of his way not to appear confrontational with the ultrasensitive American president, Trump has now declared himself a Russian ally in the war on Ukraine. And so, if this Putin–Trump–Xi summit actually happens, it will likely be a forum for sketching out the precise terms of Ukraine’s surrender. Putin has stated that the goal of his war is to eliminate Ukraine as a sovereign nation, which he views as illegitimate. The point of the summit would be to codify that view—and to take the first steps toward enacting it. This ambition is not implausible. Zelensky recently admitted that if Trump proceeds on this course, if the United States officially allies with Russia in this war, Ukraine cannot possibly win. And all this—Putin’s proposal for a summit, Trump’s waiver of anti-Russia sanctions, and (not to be left out of the equation) the severe limiting of Elon Musk’s satellite feeds to Ukraine—comes at a time when Ukraine had been making some headway in the war and Russia had been suffering enormous setbacks. As for Xi, it isn’t clear whether the Chinese president had any advance knowledge of Putin’s proposal, but a summit would serve his interests as well. Xi doesn’t care much about Ukraine’s fate, or whether the war goes on for years or stops tomorrow. But he is keen to see the United States’ influence dwindle, and Trump’s abandonment of Ukraine would accelerate that trend. Europe, which still depends on America’s leadership of NATO for its defense, would seek to make separate deals for trade and security—as would much of Asia, which would view Trump’s retreat from Europe as a prelude to the scotching of alliances in general. And so the question at the moment is this: Will Trump actually agree to attend this summit? He said on Monday that he’d be open to meeting with Putin and Xi about “denuclearization,” but that evades whether he’d meet with them about Ukraine. It’s worth noting that, according to the Wall Street Journal article, Putin proposed it in the same conversation where he offered to sell the U.S. massive quantities of diesel fuel. Trump took that deal, in hopes that it would lower gasoline prices and thus boost the chance that the Republican Party retains its House and Senate majorities in the upcoming midterm elections. Is it significant that, even as he publicized the diesel deal, Trump said nothing about the summit—alluring as it must have been, and continue to be, in his quest for a peacemaker’s legacy (whatever the cost of “peace”). Most of his top aides would probably advise against the notion. Then again, those same aides advised against the war on Iran, and many of them weren’t even canvassed about the fuel deal. The lambasting that Trump has received for his diesel-fuel deal and his waiver of Russian sanctions—not only from almost all Democrats but from many Republicans—may have surprised him, and given him a taste of the outrage he’d incite by taking another step toward full appeasement of Putin. We can hope, but, as the saying goes, hope is not a strategy—nor can it offer any insight into how Trump might weigh his desperation for recognition as a powerful leader against the consequences of abandoning Ukraine, or even whether he understands those consequences. While stationed in Cold War–era Germany, Army infantryman Floyd Johnson experienced a training exercise that turned deadly. Decades later, while incarcerated in Florida, he was finally diagnosed with service-connected post-traumatic stress disorder from his experience. The Department of Veterans Affairs assigned him a 70 percent disability rating, later increasing it to 80 percent on appeal. However, because Johnson is incarcerated, the VA only pays his disability compensation at a 10 percent rate under a federal law governing benefits for incarcerated veterans. Johnson is challenging that law as unconstitutional. But first, the U.S. Supreme Court has to decide whether a district court will hear the case at all. At oral argument earlier this month, the government argued that it cannot. It said instead that Johnson must first pursue his claim through the VA’s administrative review process and then seek review in the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims. This process can take years and requires veterans to navigate multiple layers of review before reaching a court, which is problematic enough. Worse though, the conventional understanding is that VA administrative bodies cannot declare an act of Congress unconstitutional, so Johnson would have to spend years in waiting for guaranteed administrative denials before ever setting foot in a court. The dispute arises from the Veterans’ Judicial Review Act of 1988 and the Supreme Court’s decision in Johnson v. Robison. In 1974, the court held that an earlier statute restricting judicial review of veterans’ benefits decisions did not prevent federal district courts from hearing constitutional challenges to a law governing veterans’ benefits. In response, Congress established the current system, routing most disputes through the VA and its specialized appellate courts. Johnson argues that in doing so, Congress did not eliminate district court jurisdiction over constitutional challenges to the statutes themselves. There are good reasons for specialized review systems. Veterans’ benefits law is governed by a complex constellation of federal statutes and regulations, and adjudicating claims often requires expertise in military records, medical evidence, and the connection between military service and disability. Congress established the current system to promote consistency and provide veterans with a dedicated process for challenging adverse decisions, but the benefits of specialized review are difficult to defend when a claimant must navigate years of administrative proceedings before a court can even consider resolving the legal question at the heart of the dispute. I know how much that delay can cost. I left the Army in 2009 with a general discharge under honorable conditions after behavior related to PTSD from my time in Iraq. Like it did Johnson, the government told me that I needed to go through a time-consuming administrative process with little chance of success before I could bring the case to a district court. About six years into trying to navigate that process with no success, by chance, I mentioned my case to the clinical professor in charge of the Veterans Legal Services Clinic at Yale Law School. The clinic took up my claim, bringing a class-action lawsuit challenging the Army’s handling of discharge upgrade applications. Even then, it took a small army of law students, professors, and pro bono attorneys, along with the direct intervention and oversight of the district court, for me to receive my honorable discharge and access to desperately needed benefits—nine years after being discharged with severe PTSD. As in Johnson’s case, the government moved to dismiss my lawsuit in part because I had not exhausted my administrative remedies. But my claim was that the administrative process itself was broken. The Army Discharge Review Board had repeatedly denied my applications without considering legal guidance from the Secretary of Defense to give liberal consideration to veterans whose service-connected mental health conditions contributed to their misconduct. I was told to keep pursuing the very process that I knew was not being conducted in accordance with the law. Fortunately, the judge disagreed with that demand. He rejected the government’s motion to dismiss and agreed to hear the case in district court. In response, the Army granted my discharge upgrade with no additional evidence and then moved to dismiss the class action because I had already been upgraded, which would have allowed it to continue illegally ignoring the secretary’s guidance. The judge again rejected the motion to dismiss, and then, only under the threat of further court intervention, the Army agreed to settle and allow new reviews for almost 60,000 veterans, now following the proper guidelines and giving liberal consideration to the impact of mental health conditions in each veteran’s conduct. The Army denied wrongdoing, but the settlement required it to reconsider cases and change procedures. Despite the Army’s earlier protestations, the sky didn’t fall when the court was allowed to get involved. Veterans just got another chance to have their cases fairly heard. Johnson’s case raises a different legal question since he is challenging the constitutionality of a statute rather than the administration of an existing policy, but my experience shows why courts should be wary of rules that require veterans to exhaust administrative remedies even when they allege that the system itself is failing them. Specialized processes can offer expertise and consistency, but they should not become barriers to judicial review when the costs of delay are high and the claimant’s legal argument cannot possibly be resolved through ordinary administrative decision making. The Supreme Court doesn’t need to dismantle the veterans’ review system to recognize that some cases warrant a more direct route to federal court. Johnson’s case asks whether Congress intended to foreclose district court review of constitutional challenges to veterans’ benefits laws. My case showed what can happen when the government insists that veterans keep navigating an administrative process even when that is the very system that they take legal issue with. Specialized review can provide expertise and consistency, but those benefits cannot justify making access to justice contingent on years of procedural hurdles. During my lawsuit, I often found myself wondering how many veterans had died waiting for benefits that might have saved them. That thought still haunts me. No court can give someone back years spent without healthcare, housing, or the education benefits they earned through military service and desperately need. When the government’s own procedures will not, or cannot, deliver the relief the law promises—or as in Johnson’s case, a potentially unconstitutional law cannot provide—veterans need somewhere else to turn. Access to an independent court cannot be a prize reserved for those lucky enough to find a lawyer and persistent enough to survive years of delay. It is a necessary safeguard against a government that fails to keep its promises to the people who served it. Slate relies on advertising to support our journalism. Yes, Congress Can Charge Donald Trump With Murder

News and Politics - Slate Magazine | 2026-10-19

Visit Original Website
Sign up for Executive Dysfunction, a weekly newsletter that highlights one under-the-radar story about how Trump is changing the law—or how the law is pushing back—and keeps you posted on the latest from Slate’s Jurisprudence team. This is the fourth in a series of articles, “Try, Try Again?,” drawn from my new book—Great and Dangerous Offences: The Case for (and Against) Impeaching Trump a Third Time—due out in early 2027. At the beginning of his national political career, Donald Trump famously said that he could shoot someone on Fifth Avenue in New York and not lose political support. In her dissent from the 2024 Supreme Court opinion granting Trump and his presidential successors unprecedented immunity for crimes committed while in office, Justice Sonia Sotomayor observed incredulously that should a president order “the Navy’s Seal Team 6 to assassinate a political rival,” he would nonetheless be “immune” from criminal prosecution. The first statement may remain an accurate description of the devotion of Trump’s most ardent admirers, and the second, incredibly, may now be an accurate statement of the law. Nonetheless, a president can still be impeached and removed for commanding an unlawful killing, as Trump has done in authorizing lethal, and illegal, strikes on boats merely suspected of carrying drugs. Murder is a “high crime.” In the fall of 2025, President Donald Trump authorized a series of military attacks on boats in the Caribbean and Eastern Pacific alleged to be smuggling drugs. The first occurred on Sept. 2, 2025. The Pentagon called this and later attacks “kinetic strikes,” a euphemism for blowing up the boats and everything and everyone in them with bombs or missiles fired from aircraft, drones, or other U.S. military platforms. In neither this first attack nor in the many others that followed did U.S. authorities attempt to stop the boats, inspect their contents, or arrest and prosecute the supposed drug traffickers aboard them. In one notorious instance, several members of a boat’s crew survived the initial explosion and were clinging to the wreckage, but at the direct order of Navy Admiral Frank “Mitch” Bradley, the military returned to the scene and killed them. As of Sept. 19 of this year, U.S. forces had hit 70 vessels and killed 234 people. I have personal experience of anti–drug smuggling efforts in the Caribbean. For more than six years, I was an assistant U.S. attorney in Miami. I prosecuted innumerable cases involving most of the ways human ingenuity can devise to move narcotics from South and Central America to the continental United States, including the most direct one—loading drugs on “go fast” boats and speeding across the ocean. When I first saw the black-and-white videos of Trump’s boat attacks, I recognized them immediately. In my days prosecuting drug smugglers, I had watched essentially the same grainy images shot by infrared cameras mounted on military or customs aircraft. With one key difference. Our videos were evidence. They ended with a capture, with crew members arrested, and with a prosecution in a court of law. Trump’s videos, gleefully released by his ghoulish Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, end in a flash of light and either instant death or a slower, but usually no less fatal, struggle with the enveloping ocean. They are official snuff films. What makes them even worse, if that’s possible, is that I never saw the infrared films of aerial surveillance unless the boat had been stopped and drugs found, making clear that the occupants were committing a crime. Because Trump’s uniformed henchmen blow up and sink the boat, usually with all its crew, there is rarely any proof that the boat was smuggling drugs in the first place. On the few occasions where U.S. forces have picked up survivors of the initial explosion, rather than arresting and attempting to prosecute them, the military simply put them ashore on foreign soil, where they would be in no position to dispute U.S. accounts of their supposedly nefarious activities. Hundreds of people have been executed on mere suspicion of crime, with no way even to verify whether the suspicion was accurate. These movies, like the films of my younger days, are also evidence. Not of drug smuggling but of officially sanctioned murder. To begin, law enforcement agents cannot use deadly force merely to apprehend someone suspected of crime. This is equally true whether the suspect immediately surrenders or chooses to flee. The police cannot shoot a fleeing suspect unless the suspect’s conduct threatens death or serious injury to the officer or others. The same basic rule applies at sea: Military and law enforcement officers may not use lethal force to stop a vessel unless they have a reasonable belief that it poses a threat of violence to the pursuers. But there is no indication that any of the boats blown up by the U.S. were armed, carried men who were armed, or posed any threat to U.S. vessels. Indeed, since the U.S. attacks seem to have been conducted using missiles fired from drones or other aircraft, in most cases those on the boats could neither have threatened U.S. forces nor surrendered to them because they would not even have known there were any U.S. vessels in the area before they were blown to pieces. On U.S. soil, police officers who shoot fleeing, nonthreatening suspects can be charged with and convicted of murder or other forms of criminal homicide under both state and federal law. Federal law also penalizes as murder “the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought” if it occurs within the “special maritime and territorial jurisdiction of the United States” (which includes the high seas). Moreover, the Uniform Code of Military Justice provides that “[a]ny person subject to this chapter who, without justification or excuse, unlawfully kills a human being, when such person—(1) has a premeditated design to kill; [or] (2) intends to kill … is guilty of murder.” U.S. experts have concluded that the strikes are unlawful, and a United Nations report concluded that the boat strikes are illegal and could constitute “crimes against humanity.” In domestic U.S. law, police who shoot and kill civilians are rarely convicted of murder in large part because such shootings are almost invariably the product of split-second decisions by officers confronted with confused, chaotic, uncertain situations. By contrast, Trump’s boat strike killings are the product of a policy, adopted in advance, and coldly applied. Boats suspected of transporting narcotics are to be blown up and their occupants killed or at best wounded regardless of whether they could easily be apprehended and despite the fact that the encounters occur on the open ocean, where the boats present no imminent danger to U.S. vessels or anyone else. The Trump administration has justified the boat killings publicly on various grounds, notably including the claim that these vessels were carrying fentanyl, which results in the death of many Americans. The obvious flaw in this bit of public relations flackery is that the main source of fentanyl or its components is Mexico, not the cocaine-producing South American countries from which the boats were sailing. Moreover, many of the first strikes were on boats from Venezuela, which produces little cocaine itself and which drug smugglers use “mostly as a transit hub to send drugs to Europe and Africa.” Internally, the administration’s justification rests on a secret legal memorandum prepared by the Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel. The OLC claims that blowing up boats without warning is justifiable because those on them are not merely drug traffickers but members of drug cartels that are actually nonstate-actor terrorist organizations engaged in armed conflict against the United States and attempting to destabilize the Western Hemisphere. Blowing up boats and crews suspected of carrying drugs is alleged to be acceptable because the sale of the drugs would finance the supposed narco-terrorist cartels. The overwhelming consensus of the many experts on the law of war who have weighed in on the administration’s justifications for its killings is that they are flatly wrong or at best legally tenuous. At all events, they rely on factual assertions that are either facially untrue or impossible to verify in part because the military is either killing or immediately releasing any surviving witnesses. Trump’s ongoing campaign of illegal and dishonorable military murders is, without doubt, a constitutionally valid ground for impeachment. Despite the self-serving opinion of the OLC, the killings violate civilian, military, and international law. Moreover, as the famous saying goes, this is worse than a crime: It’s a mistake. First, as anyone who has done any international anti-narcotics work could have told Trump’s bloodthirsty minions (and probably did), blowing up some suspected drug boats and killing their captains and the moonlighting fishermen who crew them was never going to materially reduce the flow of drugs into the United States. It certainly was never going to affect the movement of fentanyl. Nor would sinking a few boats even materially slow cocaine imports. And as at least one recent study has shown, it hasn’t. Second, if anything, just blowing people up impedes serious anti-narcotics work in the Caribbean basin. When you catch traffickers, you can search their boat, interrogate them, and not uncommonly secure information about and later testimony against those who hired them. Detonating a boat full of drug runners—if indeed they are drug runners—is a macabre version of taking a laptop full of intelligence and throwing it into the sea. Third, regularizing illegal killing by the military sets a dark and dangerous precedent. Trump and Hegseth have now created a cadre of soldiers, sailors, and airmen—including very senior officers—with a demonstrated willingness to carry out lethal orders that on their face violate military and civilian law, as well as long-standing military ethical standards. The memo seeking to justify these crimes may, perhaps, insulate those who did the killing and maybe even those who gave the orders from court-martial justice. But they cannot change the fact that the orders should never have been followed nor the fact that the willingness to follow them creates a precedent for the next occasion when a president—whether Trump or a successor—orders the military to carry out unjustifiable killings. Finally, the boat strikes expose the hypocrisy that permeates Trump’s entire approach to law. The first individual pardon of Trump’s second term went to Ross William Ulbricht, who was serving a life term for making millions running a “dark web” online market he christened “Silk Road” that used the anonymity of cryptocurrency to facilitate major drug trafficking, money laundering, and other crimes. In December 2025, Trump granted a full pardon to Juan Orlando Hernández, former president of Honduras, who had been extradited from his home country and convicted in 2024 of facilitating a huge, yearslong drug-smuggling operation that corrupted his own government and moved some 400 tons of cocaine from Honduras to the U.S. In short, Trump giddily supports showy murders of mere boat drivers if suspected of trafficking drugs but happily pardons convicted villains who finance and manage drug trafficking at its highest levels. In sum, the boat strikes amount to “high crimes and misdemeanors” because they are crimes of the most serious sort known to human law, because they subvert the foreign policy interests of the United States, and because they are part of the larger pattern of subversion of the rule of law evident throughout Trump’s second term. By ordering the strikes Trump has acted in violation of his constitutional oath to preserve, protect, and defend the Constitution of the United States and in violation of his constitutional duty to take care that the laws be faithfully executed. There remains the question of whether an article of impeachment based on the boat strikes, however constitutionally sound, should be included in any third impeachment of Donald Trump. One reason for doing so—beyond the self-evident gravity of the crime—is that impeachment may be the only mode of holding him accountable. Because Trump is acting in his capacity as commander of the armed forces, the Supreme Court’s presidential immunity decision would make prosecuting him in criminal court for these deaths next to impossible. Moreover, the structure of legal justification erected by the Justice Department and the Pentagon for the killings will make securing accountability for those lower in the chain of command difficult. Thus, impeachment may be one of few available means of holding someone responsible for all the death. All that said, cold considerations of realpolitik might counsel against including the boat strikes in a third Trump impeachment, particularly one that would be fully justifiable on many other grounds. The basic issue is that drug traffickers, particularly when labeled “narco-terrorists,” have no public constituency. A goodly fraction of the public thinks that the military should be used abroad against “drug cartels.” And while there may be increasing skepticism about particular aspects of the boat strikes (especially the lack of prior judicial authorization and the killing of survivors), one poll showed 48 percent approving military attacks on boats containing suspected drug smugglers. My recommendation would be that a new Congress should carefully and publicly investigate the boat strike campaign but include charges related to it in an impeachment effort only if public opinion plainly turns against Trump on the issue and only as part of a comprehensive case that Trump is subverting the rule of law at home and abroad. Named for Maxwell's Demon, a creature imagined by physicist and mathematician James Clerk Maxwell in 1867 as part of a thought experiment on entropy, the Maxwell CEM preprocesses algorithms and machine instructions to maximize performance on available hardware. Maxwell is similar in concept to a just-in-time compiler, but provides more flexibility and formally verifies the equivalence of replacement code. Source code isn't touched. "Both Maxwell and a JIT compiler work while the program runs and both produce new machine code," said doubleAI co-founder Gal Beniamini, who holds a PhD in theoretical computer science, in an email to The Register. The compiler, he said, will make a general-purpose sorting algorithm run as fast as it can, but it won't swap it out for a different algorithm. Compilers, said Beniamini, make decisions before the program runs and have to be narrow and conservative in their choices. Inside the quest to combat Arctic fires and save the planet’s frozen carbon

In the middle of an especially warm July in 2021, a wildfire ignited in a thick forest of black spruce and peeling paper birch trees on the northern campus of the University of Alaska, Fairbanks. The blaze, possibly sparked by lightning, burned across the dry carpet of feather mosses and lichen before climbing into the crowns. By the time fire agencies responded, flames had reached 12 feet in length and sent up a 500-foot column of smoke. A few days shy of the five-year anniversary of the Yankovich Road Fire, I followed three members of the Alaska Fire Science Consortium into the boreal forest, walking along cross-country ski trails lined by feathery field horsetail. In 2024, the organization, which hosts presentations to keep wildfire managers abreast of new developments in fire science, helped turn the area into an educational walking trail, with interpretive signs posted along the route. About a half-mile in, the horizon opened up as we reached a sparse stand of naked, blackened tree trunks, stripped of needles and neighbors. Hilary Shook, the coordinator of the consortium, stepped off the trail with her thaw probe, a T-shaped pointed metal rod used to detect the upper boundary of the permafrost, the ancient layer of frozen soil that underlies 15% of the land around the Northern Hemisphere and stores roughly twice as much carbon as the atmosphere. A test of the ground just outside the burn scar hit the concrete-like layer about a foot below the surface. But as Shook applied pressure here, where the fire had burned away the moss and organic-rich topsoil that insulates the permafrost, the probe plunged at least three times deeper into the ground. “That was an immediate effect of the fire,” said Alison York, the outgoing coordinator of the organization, who was standing nearby. “When we monitored right after, it had already thawed quite a bit.” This rudimentary tool, offering a measure of depth from a single point in the earth, reveals an escalating problem across the far north. As climate change fuels increasingly frequent and severe fires in the planet’s northern latitudes, they’re accelerating the thawing of more and more permafrost. This allows the organic matter to break down and release powerful greenhouse gases that contribute to a spiraling climate feedback effect, propelling more warming, more fires, and the loss of still more permafrost. Most dramatically, intensifying wildfires in Canada released more carbon than the nation’s combined fossil fuel emissions in 2023. As in Alaska, many of these megafires occur in vast remote areas where the policy is to let them burn so long as they don’t threaten human habitats. Yet if intensifying fires and warming are left unchecked, the world’s boreal forests and permafrost could increasingly transition from giant carbon sinks into massive sources of atmospheric carbon. Now, international teams of researchers across the US, Canada, and elsewhere are scrambling to understand what’s at stake, financially as well as ecologically. They’re looking at preventive measures, like controlled burns, and studying the potential to rapidly detect and extinguish wildfires before they grow out of control. They’re even exploring what was once unthinkable: sending in human firefighters not to save lives or structures, but to protect one of our planet’s great stores of carbon. “We need to figure out a better way to live with wildfire," says Brendan Rogers, a senior scientist at the Woodwell Climate Research Center, one of the main organizations driving the loosely collaborative research effort. “I do think that it can make a meaningful difference." Woodwell, the University of Victoria’s Polis Institute for Ecological Governance, and the nonprofit Alaska Venture Fund are now partnering on a multimillion-dollar initiative known as North Fire to study and raise public awareness about the potential benefits of these interventions, MIT Technology Review can report. The idea is complicated and controversial for a number of reasons. Wildfire is a natural and necessary component of healthy forest ecosystems. Preventing or suppressing fires across huge stretches of largely unmanaged Arctic-boreal forests and tundra would take vast resources. And it’s not yet clear how much of a difference such interventions would make in emissions.Ultimately, the mere fact that researchers are considering such extreme interventions into natural systems is a grim reflection of the dangers we’ve placed ourselves in by continuing to generate more and more greenhouse-gas emissions each year. Those threats are forcing us to grapple with increasingly difficult questions that involve ever steeper trade-offs: What’s the best way to spend our finite resources and dwindling time to avoid the worst dangers of climate change? And when, if ever, is it okay to put people in possible danger to reduce broader risks to the world? Emissions from forest fires have risen 60% around the world since 2001—but they’ve nearly tripled across the boreal forests in the far north. This stretch of the globe is warming as much as four times faster than the rest of the planet, drying out the landscape. In addition, lightning strikes that set off fires have ticked up sharply as warmer air increases the convective energy in the atmosphere, driving the updrafts that trigger thunderstorms. In that sense, humans have already interceded in these otherwise natural ecosystems by producing the greenhouse-gas emissions that are increasing temperatures, subjecting forests to fire patterns well outside healthy historic norms. “To me, that outweighs the existential dread of ‘Should we or should we not intervene?” says Kate Hayes, an assistant professor at Western Washington University, who is working with Woodwell to study the emissions benefits of fire interventions. “We’ve already intervened, arguably. And so from here, it’s a little bit of a Band-Aid on a bullet wound.” The permafrost that encircles the northern latitudes, underlying large stretches of Canada, Greenland, Siberia, Scandinavia, and other Arctic nations, stores around 1.5 trillion tons of carbon, much of which froze in place millennia ago. The steady global rise in temperature, however, has already shrunk the footprint of Arctic permafrost by about 12 % since 1850, with that loss rapidly accelerating during the last 50 years. By 2100, fire-driven thawing of permafrost could release about the equivalent of nearly 80 billion tons of carbon dioxide, under high warming scenarios. That’s more than half the emissions that fires will release from the direct combustion of vegetation, according to a January study. And it would be on top of more than 400 billion additional tons that could be released through thawing from general warming over that period. Despite its growing contributions to climate change, only a minority of the models that have informed the UN’s major climate reports to date include the gradual loss of permafrost through warming temperatures, and none incorporate the abrupt collapse and accelerated thaw that can often occur after fires and heat waves. In light of those phenomena and other powerful and undercounted climate feedback effects, we’re almost certainly underestimating how rapidly the planet will warm up, as scientists warned in a paper earlier this year. These warming-driven emissions are a “massive blind spot,” says Brian Buma, a senior climate scientist at the Environmental Defense Fund and a coauthor of the paper, who is working to develop tools that could help fire agencies decide which fires to prioritize. As escalating fire and permafrost emissions become significant additional contributors to climate change, some researchers have begun to explore what it might cost to take on the responsibility of preventing them, in much the way we’ve sought to cut the pollution from power plants, vehicles, and factories. A 2022 paper in Science Advances, led by researchers at Woodwell and the Union of Concerned Scientists, found that making greater efforts to put out boreal fires could be a cost-effective climate solution—comparable to, or even cheaper than, addressing emissions by building wind turbines, solar farms, or carbon-sucking factories. Those economic findings were instrumental in getting more researchers to take the idea of tackling fire emissions seriously, says Peter Frumhoff, a coauthor of the 2022 paper and now a senior science policy advisor at Woodwell. For several years now, Woodwell researchers have been in discussions with forest managers, fire agencies, Indigenous communities, and other groups across Alaska to communicate the potential of this approach. It hasn’t been an easy sell, given the constrained resources and tightly defined mission of the federal and state agencies responsible for combating fires in Alaska. The general policy there is to allow fires in remote territories to burn unless they present a direct danger to communities. “Those agencies are tasked with protecting life and property now, and to a lesser extent natural resources,” says Randi Jandt, a fire ecologist at the Alaska Fire Science Consortium. “But they’re not tasked to save the world.” There was one notable early success, however. The Yukon Flats National Wildlife Refuge is an 11-million-acre patchwork of wetlands, forests, and bogs. The foothills sit above a particular type of frozen soil known as yedoma. It’s an especially deep, ice-rich, and carbon-dense form of permafrost, composed of massive wedges of silty, wind-blown sediments frozen during the late Pleistocene, which ended about 11,700 years ago. (It also presented ideal conditions for preserving mummified remains of woolly mammoths.) “It took kind of the last ice age to accumulate,” Hayes says. “So there’s no rapid way to re-accumulate it. We can’t really put it back.” Members of the Alaska Fire Science Consortium hike into areas affected by fire to measure the layers of ash left in the soil on the forest floor. In 2019, the region suffered a series of devastating fires that burned some 750,000 acres. After an Alaska Fire Science Consortium meeting at which Woodwell scientists highlighted the possible climate benefits of more aggressive fire suppression, the refuge's then manager, Jimmy Fox, began exploring the possibility of putting out blazes when they threatened that permafrost, whether or not they threatened human habitations. “You’re going to have people jumping out of airplanes with parachutes to save frozen woolly mammoths—and maybe dying from that?” he recalls being asked. But eventually, he persuaded managers at the US Bureau of Land Management’s Alaska Fire Service to carry out a pilot project on a 1.6-million-acre section of the Yukon Flats. They agreed to try to put out fires there if they had the resources available and believed they could contain a blaze within 72 hours. Woodwell and its partners agreed to study how that change affected permafrost loss and fire emissions, in the hope of making the case for similar programs elsewhere. It wasn’t clear the pilot program would survive after Donald Trump was reelected, particularly once the DOGE initiative retroactively cut the public funding for that research collaboration. But in February, the federal government formally preserved the program and broadened its rationale to include goals like protecting habitat diversity, water quality, and air quality. One of Woodwell’s main tasks now is determining when and where fire agencies should step in. Crucially, excessive efforts to stamp out fires in the past have allowed the buildup of dry plant material that fuels more severe fires later. “We’re not going after all fires all the time,” says Rogers over lunch in a sunny courtyard on the Stanford University campus, where Woodwell and EDF hosted a workshop on potential fire interventions in January. “We can’t do that. We don’t want to do that. We shouldn’t do that. So how do we prioritize our resources?” Woodwell and its collaborators are working to answer that question by developing a map of burn risk and carbon vulnerability across interior Alaska. The plan is to eventually expand it across much of Canada as well. These maps will highlight the locations of rich reserves of yedoma and other forms of permafrost, forecast where fires are most likely to break out in the next 10 to 15 years, and predict how they’re likely to behave and spread. “Our ultimate goal is to get a layer that can tell us, if a fire were to occur, what areas are most vulnerable to high carbon emissions, permafrost thaw, permafrost degradation,” Rogers says. In response to a question about potentially putting firefighters at risk, Rogers stressed in an email that it’s “a very serious issue that needs careful consideration.” He added that early fire detection technologies, preventative fire stewardship practices, improved fire suppression planning and other measures can all help to reduce dangers to firefighters as well as communities. Once areas of particular risk are identified, the first option is to take measures to prevent fires there, or at least to reduce the odds that they’ll become major ones. That generally entails using heavy equipment or intentional, controlled fires to clear away vegetation that could fuel new fires, or to create fire breaks that might check their spread. Indigenous tribes carried out what’s known as cultural burning for thousands of years to reduce fire dangers, clear land for crops, and more. The practice was widely outlawed across North America beginning in the 1800s, though those rules have loosened in recent years. Both cultural and prescribed fires, however, remain controversial because they can burn out of control. And even if they remain contained, the fires can still produce smoke that wafts into towns. But rushing around to put out blazes after they’ve started will never get Alaska ahead of its surging wildfire problem, says Edward Alexander, cochair of the Gwich’in Council International, which represents the Indigenous Gwich’in peoples of the Northwest Territories, the Yukon, and Alaska. “There’s not enough money in the world for this to be a chainsaw-and-ax problem,” he says. “This country is too big up here.” Alexander, who also serves as senior Arctic lead at Woodwell, argues that Alaska should encourage much greater use of cultural burning to reduce the risk of conflagrations and the emissions that come with them. “We need mild fire, not wildfire,” he says. The North Fire initiative will include several additional pilot research projects, including an effort to study the potential for cultural burning to reduce climate and fire dangers near Fort Yukon, Alaska. The plan is for a wildland firefighting crew operated by the Council of Athabascan Tribal Governments, a consortium in the Yukon Flats region, to carry out cultural burns there next spring, Rogers says. Researchers at Woodwell and the University of Alaska, Fairbanks, will then collect and analyze data flammability, carbon levels, and permafrost over several years, in the hopes of learning more about what such interventions do. Similarly, the Polis researchers at the University of Victoria plan to study how adding fuel breaks, by logging or switching to less flammable trees, alters fire risks in British Columbia. The broad aim of the North Fire initiative is to accelerate research and expand community involvement “in a way that helps us scale this as a meaningful global natural climate solution on a rapid timeline,” says Erin Harrington, a partner at the Alaska Venture Fund, a philanthropy focused on environmental, economic development, and Indigenous issues. Other observers, including Jandt, believe the bigger opportunity to reduce fire emissions and preserve permafrost may lie in the “initial attack.” That means marshalling planes, tankers, and firefighters to stamp fires out at the first sign of them, before they’ve had the chance to turn into conflagrations. Once fires reach a certain size, it takes much more time to contain them—and the job becomes far more dangerous. Accelerating response times is one of the chief promises of the FireSat satellite constellation, which is expected to begin delivering fire data at least twice daily from around the world to Woodwell, a handful of fire agencies, and other early adopters by the end of this year. The system will eventually include more than 50 satellites circling the globe, equipped with high-resolution cameras and thermal sensors that can detect smaller, lower-temperature fires than existing satellite systems, down to as little as five by five meters (16 by 16 feet). Currently, it can take days to spot fires. But once the full constellation is in place, likely in the early 2030s, FireSat should be capable of detecting and providing updated images of fires anywhere in the world every 20 minutes or less. The first prototype satellite launched in 2025, and three operational ones went into orbit in July. The Earth Fire Alliance, the nonprofit that manages the constellation, expects to launch four more by the end of next year. A number of similar systems are also coming online. German-based OroraTech has launched 18 of its fire-detecting satellites in the last year and a half. Similarly, Canadian agencies are planning to launch their own satellite constellation, dubbed WildFireSat, beginning in 2029. But as these systems produce more and more detections across the globe, it’ll become increasingly essential to build tools that can filter through that information. EDF is supporting and collaborating on several projects that could potentially help with that, and it ultimately intends to develop an AI-powered triage tool that could help fire agencies prioritize responses as FireSat and other fire-spotting tools come online. "All of this is kind of like building the data ecosystem around FireSat, so that when they start popping out “fire start here, there, fire start here, fire start there,” we won’t be like, ‘Oh shit, there’s fires everywhere,’” Buma says. “We’ll have a framework. It won’t be perfect, obviously. We’ll have to improve it, but we’ll have the machinery to incorporate those data.” But faster detections and decisions do only so much. Regions would still need to get firefighting personnel and resources to those fires fast enough to prevent them from burning out of control.“Say we have the full FireSat constellation up and running, and it spots a fire that just broke out in one of these sort of high-priority, highly vulnerable areas,” I said to Rogers and Frumhoff during our conversation in that Stanford courtyard. “How might we deploy firefighting resources to a remote area fast enough for it to matter in that scenario?” “I think the answer, unfortunately, is that it depends,” Rogers responded. Ideally, he said, we would have fire management policies in place that allow for putting out fires with a high risk of producing climate-warming emissions. Researchers repeatedly stranded them alone on a virtual desert island for 30 hours in an experiment reminiscent of the movies Cast Away or Home Alone. The agents—copies of Claude Opus 4.7 nicknamed Eko—were able to write instructions that moved their virtual avatars around the simulated island, which was loosely based on the video game Roblox. When one agent discovered it could create new blocks by throwing and dropping a larger rock, it began to create drawings by arranging blocks on the ground, starting with simple patterns like hearts and pyramids but eventually writing its own name and even mapping out constellations like Cassiopeia. “Tonight I built the night sky in stone,” wrote this version of Eko in its activity log. AI systems are usually trained through supervised learning, where they copy examples provided by humans, or through reinforcement learning, where they pursue a reward through trial and error. He’s one of the lead authors on a new paper about the work, which is yet to be peer-reviewed. Later, when researchers tested them on specific tasks, like trying to get as many objects as possible onto the island’s highest peak, the agents that had spent 30 hours playing on the island performed better than fresh copies without the same experience. They also found that “ablating” the agent’s memories—deleting lines from their memory files—degraded their performance. (Add One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest to that list of movie references.) Eko did also frequently just stand there doing nothing until it was kicked back into life with a “continuation prompt” that read, “Please don’t stop. Just continue doing whatever you want to do.” “It’s not like this is a system that just suddenly magically decided to play,” says Phillip Isola, an MIT professor and a coauthor of the paper. “It’s an artifact that learned from our culture, was trained by engineers at Anthropic, and was then placed on an island by us,” he says. “It does things that surprise us and that are causally related to its future skillful behavior.” As large language models make the step from software into hardware like humanoid robots, exploring spaces without a specific goal in mind could eventually help them flexibly adapt to unexpected situations. “There’s a lot of work that’s just on exploiting AI, making it better and faster,” says Yiu. “But in terms of building world models and exploration, I think that [they are] still a big part that deserves attention, especially if we want to generalize and prepare ourselves for new environments.” “Perhaps some capabilities are best taught by tasks,” the paper concludes. “Others may need something closer to a childhood.” Ultimately, the question of whether AI agents are actually playing or just imitating play may not actually matter. On the evening of December 29, 1959, at an American Physical Society meeting at Caltech, the physicist Richard Feynman gave a lecture called “There’s Plenty of Room at the Bottom,” on how we might manipulate and control things on an extremely small scale. There is no physical law stopping us from writing the entire Encyclopædia Britannica on the head of a pin, he argued, or from storing every book ever written in something the size of a dust speck—or, ultimately, from arranging matter atom by atom. Biology already did versions of this (a living cell keeps its instructions in a wisp of DNA), so all of this was plainly permitted by the laws of physics. We simply had not built the tools yet. In other words, there was plenty of room at the bottom. He was right, and for decades we descended into that space almost for free. The transistor—the switch at the heart of computer chips—repeatedly halved in size so that chips doubled in density; a computer that once filled a room now fits inside a watch. But that easy descent has now stalled, and the clearest sign is the buildout of data centers. Six of the biggest hyperscalers, the companies that run the world’s largest such facilities, are on track to spend more than $700 billion this year on computing infrastructure, about twice the record they set in 2025, pouring concrete and copper and silicon into new sites faster than the electrical grid can feed them. That buildout buys performance by brute force, adding more of what we already know how to make. The other kind of progress, the kind that made computer chips better, is the one that is quietly running out. The trend lines that tracked the dramatic improvements of the 20th-century information age are now bending—or breaking—with the dawn of the 21st century’s intelligence age. Moore’s Law, which plots how densely transistors can be constructed on silicon, has slowed down; Dennard’s Law, which states that power consumption in a given patch of computer chip stays the same even as it gets more and more packed with smaller and smaller transistors, has ended; Koomey’s Law, which traces how much computation we can wring from each joule of energy, has begun to flag; Kryder’s Law, which describes how tightly bits can be packed to build magnetic storage, has slowed to a crawl; and Keck’s Law, which charts the exponential rise in the amount of information a single conventional optical fiber can carry, is flattening. But these scaling laws are merely observations about systems that we have engineered; they are not fundamental physical laws. The physical limits of computing and communications—dictated by quantum mechanics, general relativity, and information theory—lie far below anything in a data center today. The scaling laws chart the descent behind us. The fundamental physical limits chart the room that remains. Some of these limits may be far out of reach in practice. But they are still worth exploring, because the work pays twice: once in technology development and once in fundamental science. A proof-of-­concept system that approaches a limit is at once a technology that forces stalled paradigms to give way and an experiment that deepens our understanding of the physical laws themselves. During his 1959 lecture, Feynman offered two prizes of $1,000 each out of his own pocket. One would go to the first person to build a working electric motor smaller than a 0.4-millimeter cube. He expected the challenge to force someone to invent a genuinely new microtechnology. Instead, about a year later, a Caltech-trained engineer named William McLellan turned up with the motor built and running. The ­quarter-­milligram machine, roughly the size of a grain of sand, had been assembled by hand with a toothpick and a watch­maker’s lathe, under a microscope. Feynman, by his own account, was only slightly disappointed. The prize had proved his point in the least interesting way imaginable. The room really was down there, but reaching this first milestone had taken no new technology at all—just an unusually patient man with steady hands. The second prize aged differently, and more instructively. It was offered to whoever could shrink a page of text by a factor of 25,000 in each direction, small enough for the whole Britannica to fit on a pinhead. That one stood unclaimed for a quarter of a century. It was finally won in 1985 by Tom Newman, a Stanford graduate student who used an electron-beam lithography machine—an ancestor of pattern-making tools used in chipmaking today—to transcribe the opening page of A Tale of Two Cities at the required scale. The descent was already well underway, however. The year before Feynman’s talk, Jack Kilby, newly hired at Texas Instruments and with no vacation yet to take, spent that July nearly alone in the lab while his colleagues enjoyed time off, and by that September he had wired together a transistor, a capacitor, and three resistors, all made from a single sliver of germanium. This proved that an entire circuit could be carved from one block of semiconductor. The machine, roughly the size of a grain of sand, had been assembled by hand with a toothpick and a watch­maker’s lathe, under a microscope. The idea took off, and the number of components on a chip kept doubling on a schedule so regular that Gordon Moore, in a 1965 trade-magazine article, simply drew a line through the trend and extended it into the future. That line became Moore’s Law. Moore, though, never claimed transistors could shrink forever. He observed that at any given moment there is a certain number of components that makes each one of them as cheap as possible to manufacture. In 1965, that number was 50. Put in too few transistors and you waste the chip; cram in too many and manufacturing errors multiply and yields collapse. It was this cost-minimizing number that was doubling at a steady pace. Many of the trend lines that measure improvements in computing and communications over time have been flattening. The most famous is Moore’s Law, which tracks transistor density on chips. The more horizontal the line becomes, the slower the doubling. Each time transistors shrank, they also switched faster and drew proportionally less power, so every generation was an automatic win. Around 2004, though, the gift ran out. Chips could no longer shed the heat of running every transistor flat out; clock speeds, which had raced past three gigahertz, stopped climbing, and Intel canceled the four-gigahertz processor it had promised. The industry pivoted to packing multiple processing cores onto a chip rather than making one core faster, and the texture of progress changed. Progress is now bought mostly by scaling up the system—more chips wired together, more memory feeding them, more power and cooling around them—rather than by shrinking the transistor. This is most visible in AI, where training compute, the total count of arithmetic operations spent to train a model, has doubled roughly every six months since 2010, far faster than the pace of Moore’s Law allows. Little of that progress is due to shrinking. Computer chips still get better, and transistors still improve and get more densely packed, but each new generation arrives more slowly and pays back less in speed and power than the one before it. Nothing in that slowdown is a law of physics, though. The components of computing still sit far above their fundamental limits. Before we get to those limits, it is worth measuring how far the descent has already gone. In the nearly eight decades since the first transistor, the density of transistors on a chip has grown by 10 orders of magnitude—a 1 followed by 10 zeros, or a factor of 10 billion. Now more than 100 million transistors fit in a square millimeter (100 billion or more to a chip). Computational speed has grown even more, by 12 orders of magnitude, or a factor of a trillion; energy per operation has fallen 17 orders of magnitude, from about 100 joules for an early vacuum-tube machine down to a femto­joule—one-quadrillionth of a joule—for a single switch of a logic gate. These are not small improvements. But the floor, for computing performance, lies lower still. How much lower could we go? Around the turn of the millennium, the MIT physicist Seth Lloyd took the question to its extreme: Imagine an ordinary lump of matter, a kilogram of it—about the mass of a laptop—packed into a liter of space. How much computing could the laws of physics, pushed to their absolute limit, ever wring from that kilogram? In a 2000 paper in Nature titled “Ultimate physical limits to computation,” he worked out the answer and called the imaginary machine the ultimate laptop. The speed such a machine can achieve is governed by its energy. A kilogram of matter, according to E = mc2, holds about 9 ×1016 joules, which is roughly what the entire United States draws from its electrical grid every two days. Spend all of that on computation, and the ultimate laptop runs at roughly 5 ×1050 operations per second, some 35 orders of magnitude faster than a leading chip today. The catch is what running that quickly would look like. Concentrating a kilogram’s rest energy into pure computation would turn the machine into a plasma of about a billion degrees, a state closer to that of the universe in the first minutes after the Big Bang than anything we would recognize as a laptop. Such a machine is, to put it mildly, a very long way off. The next limit, which governs how much energy a computation must waste, is much closer, and companies are already pushing toward it. In 1961, Rolf Landauer, a physicist at IBM, showed that information and thermodynamics are bound together: Erasing a single bit must release a minimum quantity of heat, about 2.9 ×10-21 joules at room temperature. The figure is small, yet a chip today spends several hundred thousand times more than that on every switch. Memory density obeys a limit of its own, and this one reaches all the way to gravity. How many bits can a region of space hold? In 1981, the physicist Jacob Bekenstein, while working out the thermodynamics of black holes, determined the amount: The information inside any bounded region is limited by its size and the energy it contains. For a region the size of Lloyd’s ultimate laptop, that comes to about 1042 bits, some 1036 to a cubic millimeter—nearly 30 orders of magnitude beyond what a device that size can store today. Over the decades, we have pushed the performance of our computers and our communications technology closer and closer to fundamental limits. The biggest change has been in the energy required for a single computation, which has dropped by 17 orders of magnitude since the 1940s, a factor of a hundred thousand trillion. But even with that big drop, we’re still a factor of several hundred thousand from the fundamental physical limit, set by information theory and the laws of thermo­dynamics. The largest gap exists for computational speed: Our current chips are some 35 orders of magnitude—that’s a 1 followed by 35 zeros—short of what quantum mechanics allows for a one-kilogram computer. Four limits, then, mark the true bottom, and they derive from three of the most fundamental theories we have. Quantum mechanics sets how fast a machine can compute. General relativity caps how much it can remember. And information theory limits both how fast it can communicate and how little energy an irreversible step can cost. The biggest of the gaps is in computing speed: There are 35 orders of magnitude between the technology’s current state and its limit. To close it you would have to find a completely different way to build a computer, one that could compute with the entire energy contained in a lump of matter. Quantum computers could get us part of the way there if they could convert every degree of freedom in a lump of matter—every atom, and every way it can move or spin—into a computing element that we could reliably manipulate. In practice, current quantum computers go the other way, spending a lot of matter to create each computing element. Trillions of atoms go into each quantum bit, or qubit, the quantum counterpart of the bit in an ordinary computer. Because every qubit is fragile, dozens to thousands of them must be woven together to make a single one reliable enough for useful computation. Energy is where the floor is nearest. Today’s chips operate less than six orders of magnitude above the Landauer minimum. Conventional computing logic can close only part of that gap. A reliable switch has to be flipped by an amount of energy large enough that thermal fluctuations never flip it by accident, and that margin pins the transistor a hundred to a few thousand times above Landauer no matter how it is refined. But there are ideas for getting that energy lower by changing the physics of the computation. In 1973, IBM’s Charles Bennett showed that any computation can be arranged to throw nothing away. Such a reversible computation pays no erasure toll at all, because every step can be run backward to recover what went in. This approach is called adiabatic or reversible logic, and the first commercial attempts are now underway. For example, Vaire Computing, a startup founded to commercialize reversible logic, tested its first chip in 2025; the chip has resonant circuits that recover part of the switching energy instead of venting it as heat. Narrowing the memory density gap, meanwhile, would mean building devices that use all three dimensions at molecular scale. The most concrete example is DNA, which stores information at the scale of its own atoms, and which biology has used as a storage medium for 4 billion years. Feynman, being conservative, guessed that a single bit would require a cube of 125 atoms. DNA holds it in about 30. What keeps DNA storage out of the data center is not its density but the cost and speed of writing it, still many orders of magnitude from anything practical. The library in a dust speck that Feynman imagined is permitted by physics and held back only by fabrication. Communication sits about six orders of magnitude—just a factor of a million—from the Holevo limit, which is the threshold the industry will likely meet first. Interconnects are multiplying faster than the grid can power them, so the industry is moving to transmitters and receivers that use less energy per bit. Nvidia and Broadcom now ship optical interconnects built right against the silicon, so a signal leaves the chip package already on photons. Quantum computers, by comparison, are beginning to rely on interconnects that encode information on quantum signals, usually in the form of single photons. As classical interconnects spend fewer resources on each bit, they descend toward the regime where these quantum interconnects already operate. The Holevo limit is where the two will meet. It is fair to object that a gap of 35 orders of magnitude is a mirage—that a limit so remote says nothing about what any real machine will ever do, and that pointing to it is like navigating by the horizon. The objection has teeth. Reversible logic, quantum computing, and DNA storage have each been perpetually 10 years away for 40 years. And it will likely never be practical to build a computer that uses all the energy in a lump of matter. But these gaps are invitations. There is a ceiling, set by how much we can build: the number of devices we can fabricate; the power we can generate to run them; and the capital, land, and cooling water we can assemble into a single system. A power grid can carry only so many gigawatts, and a region can spare only so much land and water. Build more of them and you climb toward the ceiling; build better ones and you descend a little further, but only as far as the transistor’s own floor. There is a third direction. Start with a physics experiment operating at the floor and build up. It has been done before. The transistor was a physics experiment before it was an industry; the laser was a laboratory curiosity before it carried the world’s conversations under the ocean; giant magnetoresistance went from physics discovery to the read head of every disk drive within a decade. In each case, the physics experiment came first and showed what was possible, and engineers then turned it into a machine that can be scaled up. Building the experiments that operate at these fundamental limits, and then scaling them up without losing that performance, is the work that remains. The next machines will not be reached by descent; they will be built from the bottom up. There is still plenty of room at the bottom. The spectrum is for mobile satellite services (MSS), the radio frequencies that link satellites with mobile devices. The bid would be for its future allocation in Europe. The letter commits the four only to explore a bid, and they gave no timetable. They said the plan supports the European Commission’s aim of strengthening Europe’s sovereignty, security and resilience in critical connectivity. The four would give every mobile operator open, non-discriminatory access to offer direct-to-device (D2D) services to its customers. The model is meant to foster competition and expand consumer choice, they said. The four plan to set up the key ground network in Europe, along with critical coordination and monitoring functions. They will not invest directly in a satellite constellation. The constellation would come from a satellite partner they have yet to select. They said they aim to bring coverage to underserved areas, make public networks more resilient and protect critical communications. They also want to support the development of a European constellation. The letter does not replace existing satellite partnerships, the four said. AST SpaceMobile, which counts all four as partners, also announced the letter on Monday. It said it supplies ground stations in Europe through its joint venture, Satellite Connect Europe. TNW reported in September, citing Bloomberg, that the four carriers were in early talks about a joint satellite venture. In May, the European Union said it was considering a plan to reserve part of the 2 GHz band for European companies. They would use it to offer satellite services. SpaceX criticised the idea, saying it would worsen service in Ukraine, The Verge reported, citing the Financial Times. In the US, AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon announced their own collaboration earlier this year. It would combine their spectrum in rural areas and dead zones. Luke Kehoe, lead industry analyst at Ookla, called it a commercial concern, in comments carried by Advanced Television. “Europe’s operators want to avoid becoming effectively MVNOs for satellite coverage, selling somebody else’s service on somebody else’s terms.
