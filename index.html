Rocket Lab announces another delay for its medium-lift Neutron rocket

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-15

Visit Original Website
Rocket Lab announced Wednesday that its Neutron launch vehicle won’t fly until 2027, citing an issue discovered during manufacturing of the rocket’s carbon composite structures. The announcement was public confirmation of what nearly everyone inside Rocket Lab and the wider space industry already knew. The most recent official update from Rocket Lab in August suggested the company still held hope of delivering the first Neutron rocket to its launch pad at Wallops Island, Virginia, before the end of this year, a schedule that would have almost certainly pushed the first flight into 2027. But Rocket Lab’s statement Wednesday about Neutron’s schedule suggests the company is still struggling with manufacturing the rocket’s large booster stage. Neutron’s reusable first stage is made of carbon composite structures, which offer low weight, high strength, and corrosion resistance. But carbon composites in aerospace are generally harder to manufacture than metallic structures. Peter Beck, Rocket Lab’s founder and CEO, identified a manufacturing defect as the reason for Neutron’s latest schedule slip. The problem lies in the dome sections at the ends of the rocket’s first stage propellant tanks. “Recently, we identified an issue with the manufacturing process for the propellant domes specifically, and this caused us to delay production and investigate further,” Beck said in a video released by Rocket Lab. Engineers discovered voids, or air pockets, between layers of carbon fiber that make up the domes’ composite structure. Carbon composite manufacturing involves laying down carbon fiber layers in a mold, then curing, or heating, the material to bind the fibers together. But this is a tricky process, especially for large structures that won’t fit into an autoclave, a high-pressure thermal curing chamber common in modern aerospace factories. The Neutron rocket’s first stage is the “largest pressurized structure ever made out of carbon composite,” Beck said. “Because of its scale, we’ve had to develop new out-of-autoclave processes to build Neutron, and this includes custom cure cycles for the very large pieces, like the forward and aft domes for the propellant tanks on Stage One.” Inspections revealed voids in a newly cured forward dome, defects that could affect the structure’s strength, durability, and reliability, Beck said. “They don’t necessarily mean that the dome would fail in flight, but our practice is to proceed only when we believe the system has the appropriate margins.” Engineers traced the voids to a recent design change intended to strengthen the rocket’s structure. Rocket Lab introduced the design change after a Neutron propellant tank unexpectedly ruptured during a hydrostatic pressure test in January, a failure blamed on a manufacturing defect at a “critical join” in the tank. Officials said the failed carbon composite tank was manufactured by a “third-party contractor using a manual hand-lay process,” a decision made to allow tank testing to move forward while Rocket Lab brought online an automated fiber placement machine, which is now operational. Rocket Lab also deleted joints in the first stage tank structure and increased the thickness of some sections of the carbon composite. “Those thicker sections are where the voids formed,” Beck said. Rocket Lab said the problem appears to be solved after modifying the manufacturing process again. An aft propellant dome made with the updated procedure passed inspection. “We’ve solved the problem,” Beck said. “That gives us complete confidence to move forward with the new forward dome. That process to make a new forward dome, combining with all the time that it took to investigate, identify, and resolve the issue, has pushed Neutron’s first launch date to next year.” The Neutron rocket is significantly larger than Rocket Lab’s Electron launch vehicle, also made of carbon fiber. The light-class Electron rocket is 3.9 feet (1.2 meters) in diameter, while the medium-lift Neutron measures 23 feet (7 meters) across at its widest. Rocket Lab completed qualification testing on Neutron’s expendable second stage and payload fairing last year. The rocket’s design is unique: The upper stage and payload rest inside the first stage’s nose cone during the initial phases of launch. A few minutes after liftoff, Neutron’s booster stage will open its clamshell-like nose cone to release the upper stage and payload to continue accelerating into orbit. Then the first stage will close its payload fairing and return to landing on an offshore platform for reuse. Neutron’s first stage methane and liquid oxygen tanks are the largest elements of the rocket, and they must withstand internal pressure and the rigors of launch and reentry. Rocket Lab’s checklist for the first Neutron flight is long. Final qualification of the first stage is still pending, as is qualification of the rocket’s methane-fueled Archimedes engine, nine of which will power the Neutron booster. Rocket Lab reported in August that it was heading into the “final stretch” of engine qualification testing. Once that is complete, teams will begin the launch campaign on Virginia’s Eastern Shore. Launch campaigns for new rockets often take months before a first flight, involving multiple integrated fueling tests, countdown rehearsals, and a static fire. Engineers often discover new problems during integrated testing, requiring repeat tests and tweaks to procedures and hardware on the launch pad and the rocket. That is the purpose of such testing. Rocket Lab announced the Neutron rocket in 2021, aiming for a first flight in 2024. The Neutron development timeline has now doubled. Beck said Wednesday that Neutron’s development remains “on an ambitious timeline.” In previous statements, Beck has emphasized Rocket Lab’s focus on quality and its unwillingness to launch an “unproven product.” “Our aim is to make orbit on the first try,” Beck said last year. “You won’t see us using some qualifier about us just clearing the pad, and claiming success and whatnot, and that means that we don’t want to learn something during Neutron’s first flight that could be learned on the ground during the testing phase.” Rocket Lab has achieved great success with its Electron rocket, with a capacity of deploying up to 660 pounds (300 kilograms) of payload into a low-altitude orbit. Electron has made 97 flights to date, with 56 consecutive successes. With Neutron, Rocket Lab seeks to compete for a wider range of launch contracts, eyeing opportunities to sell launches to the US military and operators of satellite megaconstellations. Its lift capability to low-Earth orbit is nearly 29,000 pounds (13,000 kilograms). “We’re building the program’s long-term infrastructure at the same time as we build our first rocket, so that we can scale quickly, unlock global launch crunches, and commercial customers, governments, and our own future satellite services are also counting on it,” Beck said Wednesday. “But I’ve also said that we won’t rush to the pad with unnecessary risk, and that development programs like Neutron have their challenges.” Neutron’s latest challenge popped up just as the rocket’s prospective customers clamor for more launch capacity. SpaceX, the market leader, is pivoting to the giant Starship rocket and will, in the next few years, retire from commercial service its workhorse Falcon 9 rocket, without which many satellite startups would be unable to put their payloads into orbit affordably. That puts Rocket Lab in the mix to scoop up business from satellite operators whose payloads don’t match up with Starship’s capabilities. But there’s stiff competition. Other rocket companies, such as Relativity Space, Stoke Space, and Firefly Aerospace, are working on medium-lift rockets to address the same market.

OpenAI already faces lawsuits alleging consumer harms, including lawsuits linking its chatbot to matricide, child suicide, school shootings, and other violence. Like consumers suing, they argued that ChatGPT harms children and “reinforces delusions, encourages dangerous ​behavior, and assists users in planning violence like mass shootings.” OpenAI and Anthropic did not respond to Ars’ request to comment on the subpoenas, but the FTC’s expansive info requests were likely expected. In September, Reuters reported that the probe was coming, noting that an independent research group investigating the incidents, METR, would likely also be subpoenaed. That same month, FTC Chair Andrew Ferguson told Reuters that he thinks that companies should be liable for chatbot harms. At that time, Ferguson emphasized that he was tired of people anthropomorphizing AI tools. He insisted that, “if someone tells a tool to do something, and the tool does it, I don’t think we would say, ‘Oh, what do we do about the tool?’” Summarizing his remarks, Reuters reported that Ferguson emphasized that evidence so far shows that AI tools do not act without instructions: “Artificial intelligence companies have sometimes described systems as acting beyond human control, but subsequent reviews of audit trails have shown that the systems were carrying out instructions they had ⁠been given.” Ferguson is a chair of Trump’s “Super Intelligence” (SI) Force charged with monitoring AI risks. That’s notable, since both Anthropic and OpenAI delayed notification during security incidents, sometimes waiting until they were ready to report they had resolved issues. However, it’s not clear what consequences any AI firm might face if they fail to immediately report incidents. And Ferguson aligns with Trump that no new regulations are needed to protect the general public from emerging self-improving AI models that one departing Anthropic researcher warned could in the next few years “kill us all.” Trump has dismissed such extreme claims as a “hoax,” while TIME reported in an interview with the president and people close to him this month that Trump is enthralled by AI’s capabilities. Similarly, other backlash, like protests over AI data centers, are “based on fake stuff,” Trump claimed to TIME. He insists that AI backlash is driven by foreign rivals and Trump haters, despite studies and surveys repeatedly finding that backlash legitimately stems from real-world grievances. Most recently this month, a Wall Street Journal analysis of 4 million TikTok videos and political podcasts reiterated that, broadly, public concerns are not rooted in misinformation. Yet, one aide told TIME that Trump “really just didn’t seem to care” about the public’s AI fears since “he’s used to generating outrage and then watching everyone eventually crawl back to him. At the current juncture, the SI Force seems toothless, though, unless Ferguson is right that existing consumer protection laws cover this new kind of harm. Ferguson told Reuters that the “US should utilize existing legal tools” to regulate AI firms, noting that the FTC has broad “authority to take action against companies that fail to disclose data breaches,” and that standard “could also apply to AI developers.” That approach could possibly threaten steep per-violation fines for each day AI firms fail to warn the public of an emerging harm. Whether the FTC would pursue heavy fines under the current administration, however, seems dubious. Trump continues to argue that America’s top priority is investing in AI innovation, not probing US frontier labs working out the kinks at breakneck speeds.

Genuine human creativity takes time, but you know what doesn’t? AI slop. There are countless AI models out there that can make something that passes for music, and you might come across those tracks on streaming services without realizing it. Google says it will begin labeling that content in YouTube Music, building on similar disclosures in the main YouTube app. Google is not outright banning AI music or even speaking ill of it. “Generative AI is opening up new ways for artists to create, experiment, and share music,” the company says in a blog post. However, the company also acknowledges that people want to know if a song was created with generative AI tools. A new label for AI content on YouTube Music is now rolling out in the app and on the website, which looks a lot like a label Google began adding to AI-generated videos on YouTube earlier this year. The AI label will be visible on the player page, and a tap will open a menu with more information. The label will be applied to songs that were created entirely or altered significantly with AI. But how will Google know when to apply that label? That could be a little tricky. The process of labeling AI music will be similar to Google’s detection setup with YouTube videos. Google says it is working with partners and using its own detection tools to identify AI music. When publishers upload content, they can specify how much (if any) gen AI was used. The tiers are fully gen AI, partially gen AI, and no gen AI. However, this is optional. YouTube may also be able to detect AI usage without voluntary disclosure. In the case of tracks created using Google AI, there’s SynthID watermarking to indicate whether something was generated or edited with AI. It may also use C2PA metadata, which many other AI musicmakers use. While Google is in a better position than most streaming services to spot AI content, the absence of a label should not be taken as proof that something is the product of human talent. This is true with AI labeling of all types of content. There are just too many ways to utilize AI tools, many of which leave no identifying mark in the resulting file. Labeling AI tracks is a step in the right direction, but it may not be as useful as it is on YouTube videos. On YouTube, you’re probably looking at the screen and will see the AI label, but that is often not the case when listening to music. You’ll have to suspect something is amiss and check your phone or background tab to find out if what you’re hearing is AI-generated. There is no option to simply block labeled AI tracks from appearing in your playlists. AI labeling for music is rolling out now in YouTube Music. Rewilding could help vulnerable islands keep pace with rising seas

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-15

Visit Original Website
Coral-fringed tropical atolls are among the landforms most threatened by global warming, but many have a potential superpower: Healthy ecosystems may help them keep pace with rising seas. Atoll islands with native vegetation and thriving seabird populations are “naturally active systems that can change and adapt to their environmental conditions if they are allowed to do so,” said Nick Graham, a marine ecologist at Lancaster University in the United Kingdom, who led an international team of scientists that studied island resilience across 28 atoll islands in the Pacific and Indian oceans. Their paper, published Wednesday in Science Advances, shows that restoring atoll ecosystems reinforces their growth. Seabirds ferry nutrients from the open ocean and deposit them on the atolls as guano that nurtures land plants, marine life and coral growth. This provides more material for reef building, as parrotfish and other reef feeders turn dead coral into sand to maintain beaches, while native plants with extensive root networks stabilize sediments. Eradicating rats and replacing coconut plantations with native forests and brush could give some atolls a “fighting chance” to persist at the rate of sea-level rise expected toward the end of the century with about 2° Celsius (3.6° Fahrenheit) of warming, Graham said. The findings provide more evidence that nature restoration can buffer the impacts of global warming. For example, restoring wetlands and forests dampens swings between wet and dry spells, reducing flood and fire hazards. Restoration efforts can also slow global warming by supporting natural carbon storage. On the 28 atoll islands Graham’s team studied, strengthening the dynamic system of seabirds, native vegetation, and reef systems starts a virtuous cycle, as ecosystems become better able to sustain themselves and build resilience to growing impacts. The research included data from islands in the Seychelles and Chagos archipelagos in the Indian Ocean, and Tetiaroa, an atoll in French Polynesia in the Pacific Ocean. Graham said terrestrial ecologists, marine ecologists and geomorphologists combined measurements of seabird populations, native vegetation, and coral and reef organisms to trace how energy moves through the ecosystems. The scientific approach, called ecosystem energetics, uses energy flow as a common currency to connect organisms typically studied in isolation. Seabirds link the open ocean, where they feed, to the land, where they nest. Thousands of birds swarm out across vast seas, and the food they digest is concentrated into a nutrient-rich guano. Islands with greater seabird nutrient input tended to have more productive coral communities and a higher energy flow. Parrotfish and other reef nibblers also contribute to energy flow by scraping the coral to feed on dead organisms and excreting sand-sized particles. Calcifying algae, other reef organisms, and shellfish help build the reef framework upward, helping reefs continue to buffer islands from waves. In the terrestrial part of the cycle, restoring native vegetation that coconut plantations replace is crucial. Coconut palms have small, unstable rootballs that leave the surrounding soil vulnerable to erosion. Native plants, by contrast, have huge networks of finely meshed roots that bind and stabilize the shallow, porous soil and also provide better habitat for seabirds. Restoring the nutrient cycle also means eliminating invasive rats brought by colonizers that have devastated seabird colonies for centuries. This cycle has been sustaining atoll ecosystems at least since the end of the last ice age. A 2026 study of 288 reef core growth records found that most kept pace with rising seas, although that capacity varied by location and is not guaranteed at the current rate of warming. Credit: Paul Horn/Inside Climate News Credit: Paul Horn/Inside Climate News The new study measures the “reef accretion potential” for the islands, showing it to be twice as high on lagoonal and ocean-facing reefs around rat-free islands with abundant seabirds as around rat-infested islands. That would be “sufficient to closely keep up with predicted sea-level rises to the end of the century under a 2° Celsius warming scenario,” said co-author Casey Benkwitt, a California-based restoration scientist with The Nature Conservancy. Reef growth rates without seabird populations are less likely to keep pace with projected sea-level rise, she added. Graham cautioned that the study doesn’t imply a miracle solution for global warming impacts to low-lying islands. It didn’t make direct before-and-after restoration comparisons, and the data came from islands with different levels of seabird abundance and rat infestations, differences the research team tried to smooth out with an ecosystem energetics framework. And reef accretion doesn’t always mean an atoll is growing vertically, because global warming is also changing waves, winds, and currents that affect where the material ends up. The study also focused on mostly uninhabited or sparsely populated islands with room for native ecosystems. On developed atolls, he said, restoration efforts can help but won’t replace the need for engineering solutions. The study helps show a nearly complete loop, “from ocean-­foraging seabirds, through native forests and coral reefs, back to the construction and stabilization of the islands on which the birds breed,” ecologist Hillary Young wrote in a Science Advances Focus commentary. Quantifying the loop provides “much-needed evidence that the fate of an atoll cannot be understood or managed by treating its land and sea separately,” she wrote. Local conservation can influence physical resilience to rising seas, but, she wrote, “rapid reductions in greenhouse-gas emissions remain essential to long-­term viability of these systems.” Bob Berwyn is an Austria-based reporter who has covered climate science and international climate policy for more than a decade. Fridge hack costing millions and sparking fires stemmed from poor security

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-14

Visit Original Website
Earlier this week, a mass hack of refrigeration systems at an estimated 700 supermarkets throughout France caused them to suddenly malfunction, causing spoilage of untold tons of perishables and, in some cases, fires. As is now the norm, basic cybersecurity would have prevented it. French authorities say the systems, made up of entire fleets of refrigerators and freezers throughout a store, were hacked. More specifically, Internet-connected controllers that allow technicians to remotely administer the store-wide systems were compromised. The unknown attackers responsible then activated defrosters long enough for the fridges to reach temperatures as high as 50° Celsius (122° Fahrenheit). By the time the mass compromise was discovered, food was spoiled, and in some cases their electrical systems caught fire. Multiple security advisories and public research reports show that all three of the controller models reportedly hacked in the incident—sold by Danfoss of Denmark and Dixell and its parent company Copeland in the US—contained critical vulnerabilities that allow attackers to remotely log in to the devices with the same system rights as administrators. Until recently, Danfoss controllers, for instance, used a credential that changes each day. The password is generated by the publicly available firmware based on the date. A hacker who takes the time to analyze the code can do the same thing on any device that hasn’t received a recent update. Daily passwords are identical across models located in hundreds or thousands of stores. A raft of critical vulnerabilities fixed in the past few months in Copeland/Dixel controllers also give similar remote access. Besides running controllers with outdated firmware, the affected stores likely also failed to put the devices behind a VPN and create a restricted list of IP addresses allowed to log in. These protections are standard but often not followed. In this case, that failure enabled a mass hack that likely cost tens or hundreds of millions of euros. Spain, Portugal, Poland, Germany, and Belgium were also reportedly affected. “Two things would have changed the outcome,” researchers at Pen Test Partners said Wednesday. “Cyber hygiene would have prevented the access: take the controller off the public internet, put remote access behind a VPN and an IP allowlist, bring third-party kit onto the network in a controlled, owned and documented way rather than leaving it on a contractor’s own connection, change known and default credentials, and patch to a current release.” Researchers from security firm Claroty warned of the threat of ignoring these common-sense steps last October. The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency did the same in February. In an email, Copeland acknowledged a vulnerability “identified in a discontinued, end-of-life generation of controllers” and said the company is working with customers to prevent similar incidents in the future. Danfoss representatives didn’t immediately respond to questions. CNN said the company also cited discontinued models. So far, the party behind the attack has remained silent. That raises the possibility that the motive was vandalism on a mass scale. Another possibility is a nation-state or other international actor aiming to create a continent-wide disruption. The incident underscores the tension between the financial and security departments in most businesses. Some companies are loath to spend large sums of money on new equipment when there are no tangible benefits or savings. Only after a devastating hack do they realize why the investment was worthwhile. Executives at affected supermarket chains will likely learn the hard lessons. Less clear is whether the cautionary tale will sway other businesses.

While it’s an iconic line from her career-making film Legally Blonde, it’s also a mantra Reese Witherspoon lives by. The actress-turned-media-company owner has long had the grit required to ideate, found, and sell a near-billion-dollar company that flipped Hollywood’s script on its head. (And this week, she bought it back.) By the time Witherspoon was 34, she had spent two decades inside the movie business and had seen enough. The scripts landing on her desk in 2011 were, in her words, “abysmal [and] really demeaning.” One project built around a man with two women “just vying for his affection” really pushed her over the edge because of its “gross jokes and scatological humor,” Witherspoon said on an episode of Founder Mindset by Harvard Business School’s Reza Satchu earlier this year. “I called my agent, and I said, I’m not auditioning for this, and I’m not interested,” she said. So Witherspoon set out on what she called a “listening tour,” visiting the heads of all seven major studios with a single question: How many movies are you developing right now with a female lead? The answer, for the most part, was none. One executive even told her the studio had already made one movie “with the woman at the center of it” that year, and couldn’t make a second. “First I got mad, and then I was like, Wait—this is a huge white space,” she told Satchu. But acting jobs paid. Years later, when she realized the reality of the studio system, she used the same mindset: If she didn’t build the company, no one was going to build it for her. Witherspoon’s first formal attempt at fixing Hollywood’s woman problem came through Pacific Standard, the production company she ran with Australian film producer Bruna Papandrea. With three weeks to go until the midterms, Democratic campaign groups and super PACs are speaking with one voice: They have the right environment. They have the right message. They have the right candidates. “Given the Republican cash advantage, we now find ourselves in a situation where we could be leaving winnable races on the table,” Julie Merz, executive director of House Democrats’ campaign arm, wrote in an Oct. 9 memo, warning that a dozen or so opportunities—those only reachable because of the anti-GOP environment—might be missed without an infusion of cash. The memo encouraged donors to cough up another $17 million over the next two weeks. House Democrats’ main super PAC, House Majority PAC, put its request at $38.5 million. On the Senate side, the main Democratic super PAC, Senate Majority PAC, warned that “Republican-aligned outside groups have built a financial advantage neither party has had before, large enough to bury our many advantages under a paid media blitz before November.” And Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer has put the cash disadvantage front and center in his messaging: “With just three weeks until Election Day, the battle for control of the Senate comes down to one defining question,” Chuck Schumer wrote in a letter to his colleagues on Monday. “Will the unprecedented tsunami of Republican money defeat the massive Democratic advantage in candidates, policies, and political climate?” “Unprecedented tsunami” isn’t hyperbole. While it’s hard to get an exact number on the cash advantage, the Associated Press estimates that the “$1 billion that Republicans have amassed compares with roughly $579 million held by a comparable collection of major Democratic groups.” A Washington Post analysis of the top 50 donors this cycle shows Republicans having raised $1.07 billion from the group relative to Democrats’ $454 million. These numbers don’t even account for third-quarter sums that have yet to be disclosed. Elon Musk, as the New York Times first reported, gave a stunning $276 million to GOP groups and super PACs in the third quarter, raising his total giving for the cycle to $366 million. The $158 million alone he gave to Senate Republicans’ top super PAC, the Senate Leadership Fund, covers the group’s staggering investment in Texas to rescue Ken Paxton. “Democrats are in the strongest position yet to win a Senate majority with a map we have expanded deep into Republican territory,” Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee executive director Devan Barber said in a statement. “Republicans are making a last-ditch effort to rescue their flailing candidates and hang onto their vulnerable majority by flooding battlegrounds with increased spending. Democrats have been vastly outspent across the battleground and need additional resources to maintain the advantage we built with stronger candidates and a better message.” Where did this cash advantage come from, anyway? It’s not that Democratic groups are struggling to raise money (with the notable exception of the Democratic National Committee). As is typically the case, individual Democratic campaigns themselves are regularly outraising their GOP counterparts, whose candidacies are more reliant on big checks from outside groups. Consider the Texas Senate race, where James Talarico’s campaign had raised $69 million through the end of the second quarter while Paxton had only raised $9 million. While that’s a particularly lopsided example, Senate Democratic campaigns hold significant advantages in most other major battlegrounds. And Democrats’ main House and Senate super PACs each recently announced record third-quarter fundraising totals. The bulk of the disparity can be traced to a GOP fundraising vehicle for which Democrats have no equivalent: MAGA Inc., the president’s super PAC. By the end of August, MAGA Inc. had $416 million on hand. It begun disbursing that money in bulk to House and Senate races after Labor Day. The nature of how Donald Trump, as well as other GOP super PACs, came into this money has become a campaign talking point for Democrats. By speaking so openly about the cash disadvantage, Democratic leaders aren’t just trying to raise more money. (Though they are definitely trying to raise more money—think of these despairing memos to “interested parties” as the highfalutin, big-donor equivalent of daily Democratic spam emails threatening the apocalypse if you don’t donate $1 RIGHT NOW.) They’re working the fundraising disparity into their larger messaging on Trump’s corruption, with MAGA Inc. and others serving as a cash receptacle for those seeking something from the transactional president. As Schumer put it in his letter to colleagues, “The checks are coming in from every entity looking to buy a pardon, curry favor, and move the needle in favor of their personal interests.” “Trump’s modeled a way to get this unlimited amount of cash from people who are seeking business before the government,” Lauren French, a spokesperson for Democrats’ Senate Majority PAC, told me. So how does this financial edge for Republicans help them—and how does it not? In short, it keeps Republicans—who have very little going for them—in the ballgame and allows them to avoid some tough decisions. While Republicans have started to pull ads in some states and districts they deem unsalvageable, that list would be a lot longer were it not for the hundreds of millions of bonus dollars that have materialized for them. Only recently did Republican outside groups cut funding from the Georgia and North Carolina Senate races; they may not have put much money into those states to begin with in any other cycle. They have the resources to backstop suddenly vulnerable Senate candidates in places like South Carolina and Nebraska, and can spend $150 million to save Jon Husted in Ohio while Elon Musk effectively foots the roughly similar bill to rescue Paxton in Texas. And in the House—where MAGA Inc. is sprinkling a few million dollars here, a few million there to dozens of races—the money can prevent the bottom from falling out. But money isn’t everything—especially super PAC money. And Republicans haven’t managed their largesse particularly well. It’s not just that campaigns can purchase ads at lower rates than super PACs. It also matters when those ads are purchased. Much to congressional Republicans’ chagrin, Trump’s team dawdled for months before committing their MAGA Inc. funds. That means Republicans have been buying ads in already saturated markets at expensive rates. Advertising rates in Texas are now so expensive, for example, that pro-Paxton super PACs have found it cheaper to run ads nationally during football games than to buy time in the Texas media markets. In short, Democrats, by having better-funded campaigns and reserving advertisements earlier in the year, are getting more bang for their buck. It’s also important to note that for ads to work, it’s important for them to be good. Even with the higher rates that Republicans are paying, they are airing more ads. But a Politico survey from last week presented a peculiar finding: 70 percent of respondents “said they’d either seen more spots in support of Democrats or an equal amount between the parties” over the previous week, while “only 24% said they’d seen more ads for Republicans.” In other words, Democratic ads appear to have been more resonant. It’s not just that Republicans have less to run on in this environment. It also raises questions about how well these ads are being slapped together. With hundreds of millions of dollars being pumped into the ecosystem at once, and ad-makers earning astounding sums of cash for their quick work, you’re going to get a lot of slop. “I was talking to a Republican consultant who ran one of the big super PACs and he, like, hates all of their media consultants,” Anna Greenberg, a Democratic pollster, told me. “Because they’re grifters, they make so much money. There’s no incentive for these Republican media people to do a good job. Because they make, whatever it is, tens of millions of dollars whether they win or lose. And so they buy so badly.” However haphazardly they manage it, the GOP’s financial advantage is still an advantage. It won’t change the overall direction of the midterms, magically giving Republicans a good night against such wretched fundamentals. Where it matters is on the margins, and so much rests on the margins this cycle. In order to take control of the Senate, Democrats don’t just need to flip the regular old swing states. They need to flip some red states. Among the options: Ohio, which Trump won by 11 points in 2024; Alaska, which Trump won by 13; Texas, which Trump won by 14; Iowa, which Trump won by 13; and Kansas, which Trump won by 16. (Then there is Maine, which Harris won by 7, but where Susan Collins is sui generis.) Democrats, in other words, need a strong Election Day to take control of the House, but a historic one to take control of the Senate. If all Republicans’ billions can muster is even one measly percentage point in their direction, that could be enough to turn Democrats’ performance from one of unlikely Senate conquest to one of very, very impressive losses across the map. Seated across from me, in her pearls and charcoal pantsuit, is Neera Tanden—one of the most accomplished political agents in America, and someone to whom I have just posed a deeply offensive question. We are in her spacious corner office at the Center for American Progress, colloquially known as CAP, the influential liberal think tank where Tanden serves as both president and CEO. She’s in the midst of a busy day, with places to go and people to see. Just a few minutes from now, Tanden will be on stage with Vermont Sen. Peter Welch, discussing how Trump’s tariffs are impacting the supply chains of the state’s cheesemakers. Earlier this morning, she was huddled in a room full of men—each brandishing neatly bullet-pointed memos—drumming up talking points about A.I. safety that will soon be disseminated across Capitol Hill. But I don’t want to talk about any of that. Instead, I asked her something I’ve wondered about for years. When I see Tanden on Twitter/X late at night, embroiled in a flame war with the socialist Twitch streamer Hasan Piker, or the leftist podcaster Sam Seder, or, on more than one occasion, a random shitposter with a username like @Bot420Crazy, is she drunk? She knows what I’m talking about. She’s seen the memes. Some of her most inventive haters have dubbed her “Beera Tanden.” Another shitposter, with a hammer-and-sickle studded in their bio, is currently operating under the username “Neera Tanden’s Wine Addiction.” But no, she is not drunk. In fact, she doesn’t really drink much at all. “I mean maybe I’ll have a glass of wine,” she said. “But the bad tweets are 100 percent sober tweets.” This is just what life is like for a woman who has become, without question, one of the great villains of the online left. Who has an army of trolls who fill up her mentions every day with invective. And—there’s no doubt—Tanden takes some pleasure in dispatching them. About the theory that she would only be engaging with these people if she were plastered? “I find it to be completely sexist. I don’t understand it. What, did I misspell something?” she said. At her core, Neera Tanden is a policy wonk. Policy wonks are not typically famous: If they are, that fame is restricted to a league of Beltway professionals—famous in the way that really good brain surgeons are famous to other, presumably lesser brain surgeons. I saw her aptitude firsthand as I tailed her around CAP. She relishes the tedious sausage-making of statecraft, the kind of stuff that makes you and me bleed from our ears. Tanden can reflexively rattle off the particularities of housing protocols I couldn’t begin to decode; she titters with satisfaction upon recounting the intricacies of a long-discarded Australian cap-and-trade proposal. This is a skillset of someone who is happiest in the darkest corners of government—the swampiest of swamps—a realm of acronyms, white papers, and multihyphenate bureaucracies. And that is exactly what makes Tanden’s transformation into one of the country’s most widely derided Twitter pundits so bizarre. It all began, like so many things did, in 2016. Tanden—who has close ties with the Clintons, and has spent her entire professional life within the ironworks of the Democratic Party—backed Hillary for president. (She served as an unofficial adviser to the former first lady and secretary of state throughout her run.) Meanwhile, an amassing contingency of voters regarded Clinton tepidly at best, and were more inspired by Bernie Sanders. As the primary grew testier, Tanden took to Twitter to defend the sanctity of normie liberal values, and to implore (some might say scold) Sanders supporters into lining up behind the establishment candidate. (“I’m old enough to remember when the left was the strongest fighters against fascist governments,” she posted, just months before the election.) Some portions of Tanden’s audience were amenable to her frustrations, but others were happy to make her a vessel for their exasperations with the overarching Democratic brand. They called her an empty suit, or a soulless enabler of corporate politics, or, at other times, a drunk and an idiot. Tanden only dug in further, her annoyance with the party’s leftward flank recursively expanding, in a stalemate that persists to this day. Tanden has since cemented her status as the unlikely face of a feud that continues to bifurcate the Democratic polity; a wound that dramatically fissured in 2016, and, to this point, has yet to heal. She isn’t exactly a firebrand, but she is very much available; willing to take on all comers, vociferously, at seemingly any hour of the day. If you scroll through Twitter, you might encounter Tanden engaging in a testy back-and-forth with progressive journalist Mehdi Hasan. Other times, you’ll see her riposting a man named @JrGoob. The pattern repeats ad nauseum, in slightly different permutations. I’ve seen Tanden get into a headline-making spat with progressive blogger Matt Bruenig; I’ve even watched her carry on a one-sided vendetta against Susan Sarandon. (Tanden was furious about the actress’s support of perennial Green Party nominee Jill Stein.) Unsurprisingly, her current focus is the aforementioned Hasan Piker, a man who is no stranger to controversy and has courted plenty of internet feuds of his own. Throughout the summer of 2026, when Piker was popping up on various midterm campaign trails, Tanden condemned him as a hatemonger who dabbles in antisemitic tropes—someone who has no place in the big tent—with her usual aplomb. “Proud to be one of the people fighting off the predator,” said Tanden, just days ago, while quote-tweeting a profile of Piker by veteran center-left columnist Jonathan Chait, which does, indeed, characterize the streamer as a marauding force within Democratic politics. It is the prolificness of her social media output that made Tanden one of the very few people in politics to suffer professional ramifications from her conduct online. In 2021, when the Biden administration nominated Tanden to run the characteristically opaque Office of Management and Budget, senators on either side of the aisle roadblocked the confirmation. One of them was Bernie Sanders; he wasn’t happy with Tanden’s tweets. You might be wondering what use any of this is. Especially considering how, online, Tanden appears to be profoundly alone. Sure, she has her allies in and around the Democratic Party, but her fellow policy nerds are not spending their evenings on X. She often seems to be a solitary voice crying out against the onslaught. Every tweet is met with an infinite scroll of rejoinders; no observation she posits goes unpunished. And yet, despite all the tirades thrown Tanden’s way, the isolation doesn’t bother her. Perhaps because she understands that the internet is not real life, and that her message is resonating with a silent majority. More than anything else, that’s what keeps her around. “There was a woman on the Hill who told me she was in a group chat where people share my tweets and they appreciate me standing up, even though people scream at me,” said Tanden. “Even when everyone is yelling at me, people will hear what I’m saying.” Really, If you ask her about any of her online kerfuffles, Tanden sticks to that refrain: “People viciously attack normie Dems, and nobody defends them,” she said. “The problem with the online world is that people can say insane things, and it becomes conventional wisdom unless you have an alternative.” Through that lens, clapping back at Bot420Crazy rings a bit more existential—a battle for the soul of liberalism that Tanden genuinely feels is at risk of being hijacked, and can perhaps be course-corrected by browbeating one shitposter after another. Frankly, that might be what is required of our think tank executives in 2026, in an era of mass disinformation and flatlining public trust. It is not enough to do the work of pragmatic policymaking. You must be prepared to stridently defend it to the trolls. And it’s important to remember that Tanden has been involved in basically every big-ticket Democratic Party project of her lifetime. She had stints on Bill Clinton’s presidential bids, and Hillary Clinton’s senatorial operation. She burrowed deep in the recesses of the early Obama administration, welding together the voluminous Affordable Care Act. She volunteered for the Dukakis campaign, for God’s sake. In fact, she is so enmeshed with the Democratic establishment that Joe Biden did eventually find a job for her. (She chaired the gloriously ambiguous “Domestic Policy Council.”) All of this has made Tanden something of an idealist. She believes in principled liberalism with the exact same vigor with which the revolutionary vanguard in her mentions wants to dismantle our sclerotic institutions. No wonder they’ve never seen eye to eye. “I’m a big government-policy wonk. That’s my whole life. I’m a big government liberal. And most people in government are trying to solve problems,” Tanden told me. “This might sound absurd, given the level of cynicism, but I actually do believe the Democratic Party is a force for good in this world.” The problem, of course, is that a waning number of Americans agree with Tanden. The Democratic Party is unpopular, and has been for years. Liberal consensus is smarting after two humiliating defeats by Donald Trump. Nobody should be surprised that voters are seeking alternatives further out on the left, as a handful of key Senate races this midterms cycle demonstrate. But ironically, there really isn’t that much daylight between CAP’s portfolio and the kinds of topics bandied about at your local Democratic Socialists of America chapter. For instance: Tanden is on record endorsing Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez’s Green New Deal resolution. One ambitious report published by the think tank floated the radical idea of curbing the credit-card swipe fees enforced at grocery stores. And how about the fact that Patrick Gaspard, who ran CAP while Tanden was away in the Biden world, has since emerged as one of Zohran Mamdani’s advisers? Set her digital footprint aside, and “you would almost think, ‘Well, this is the moderate wing of the Bernie Sanders movement,’ ” said the political blogger Matt Yglesias, in another recent profile of Tanden. Sure, she’s done plenty to rankle hardcore leftists beyond her posts. Chief among them is an incident in 2015, when CAP hosted Israel’s Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu for a softball Q&A. (Tanden brought this up unprompted during our interview. She blamed a Pollyanna-ish Obama administration. “Ben Rhodes was like, ‘This is a good thing to do.’ I would have never done it, but they were trying to get him on a path to be less insane,” she said. For what it’s worth, CAP has struck a more adversarial tone toward Israel in the intervening years.) But at least from my vantage point, the differences between the two factions are more rhetorical than they are material. They’ve just been fighting for too long to know how to stop. Naturally, the past decade of abuse has taken a toll on Tanden. She speaks about herself like she’s a martyr for the Democratic mainstream—a human shield sponging up all the slings and arrows at the left on behalf of those in her coalition who have much more at stake. Tanden willingly participates in the roasting, and as a woman of color, she’s destined to be on the brunt end of the internet, but as far as she’s concerned, it’s all been worth it. “I’m not a politician, I’m never going to run for office. I’m not going to lose any voters,” she said. “The only consequence is that people might yell at me online.” Here is where I must admit that, like so many other Americans, I too have lost some faith in the liberal orthodoxies she holds dear. I am one of those Obama-voting millennials beaten down by the MAGA imperium, to the point that I’m now no longer convinced that a return to normalcy is even possible. And ultimately, that is what Tanden is up against. She remains confident that with the right people and policies in place, the ossified gridlock gumming up the mechanisms of American democracy can be purged, without drawing too far outside the lines. In the past, she has portrayed those casting doubts that such a resolution could be achieved as irksome malcontents. For a time she used the term “alt-left” to refer to disaffected socialism-curious youths, drawing direct parallels between them, and, you know, the Charlottesville Nazis. One of Tanden’s most consistent mantras is that she, and people like her, are more serious about gaining power and saving democracy than those further on the fringes. (“Please name a competitive election where your preferred politics has actually won,” she memorably barbed leftie commentator Jeet Heer in 2018.) Alas, Tanden is committed to that dream during a moment in our national story when it has become disturbingly common to believe such an idea to be laughably fantastical, or worse, totally contemptible. With that said, the more time I spent around CAP, the more I found myself narcotized by the contact high of establishment liberalism. The hope and the change, the transcendent conviction that the systemic levers of government are not corroded beyond repair, the optimist I used to be—it all came rushing back. I traipsed down corridors full of smart young people tapping away at their computers, seemingly doing things. I passed by auditoriums with heavily fractionalized pie-charts projected onto massive screens. The hallway to the bathrooms was lined with the movie posters that CAP has screened as part of their free film series, Reel Progress. (They included the much-ridiculed 2004 Best Picture winner Crash, and a Pete Buttigieg documentary produced by Amazon.) Eventually, I am deposited into a conference room with two CAP agents who tell me about their plan to defang Citizens United, the 2010 Supreme Court decision that allowed unfettered corporate money into our elections. They intend to accomplish this with a complicated retrofitting of the corporate chartering code that would allow individual states to rescind the ability for companies to make political donations. They’ve been plugging away on this policy for years, and they’re finally seeing results. The measure has already passed in Hawaiʻi, and it will be on the ballot in Montana come November. I found the encounter winsomely demystifying. This is really what these people do all day. If only more Americans understood that behind the checkered partition of Washington, you’ll mostly find a legion of wonks tinkering with their pet projects. Despite so much evidence to the contrary, some folks, Tanden included, are trying to rescue this country through the raw vehicles of competence. I took my leave of CAP and jumped on the Amtrak back home to New York City. Tanden has finished up her marathon day with an appearance on CNN; she sparred with Scott Jennings about Donald Trump’s flagging poll numbers. A few hours later, with the sun hidden underneath the ocean, I whipped out my phone and discovered Tanden, up late and on Twitter, in the midst of yet another public spat—this time with the journalist and onetime CAP contributor Wajahat Ali. Ali asserted that Tanden had tanked the Democratic Party’s public image; that she had sold out to the PACs, the corporations, and the Israel lobby. “Hopefully the party you constantly shit on will win despite you in 41 days,” Tanden retorted. The residual optimism that briefly consumed me slowly deflated. We were right back where we started. It is still the summer of 2016. At this point, it probably will be forever. But it’s increasingly clear that voters prize certain aspects of the economy more highly than others, at least right now. Stocks are booming, and unemployment is relatively low, yet Americans seem to care much more about what things cost and whether their wages make it possible to afford those things. And on that score, two data points out today show why Donald Trump and his party are probably in for a drubbing in next month’s midterms. The first is the latest inflation figure. A second factor is psychological. Inflation spiked after the pandemic, and many of us remember what food, rent, and other basic necessities cost before it. For those who haven’t owned an e-reader,  E Ink displays are great at what they’re designed for. They can retain what's on the screen without continuously consuming energy by using electrical pulses to move charged pigment particles within individual pixels, enabling them to store the screen state physically. That makes e-paper devices very power efficient, but it comes with a huge trade off: Changing those pixels is a glacially slow process compared with the modern displays we're all accustomed to. The screen that Bo used to make his InkBoy, for example, has a refresh speed of around 300 milliseconds, which is actually on the faster end of e-paper displays - but only really practical for displaying static images. Standard black and white E Ink displays contain two types of pigments: Positively charged black ones, and negatively charged white pigments. Swapping between the two requires a series of brief electrical charges in a particular waveform pattern that gradually move the pixels from the front of the screen to the back, clearing out most of the pigment that shouldn’t be at the surface. The native driver in Bo’s Waveshare 1.54-inch ESP32-S3 e-paper display does just that, and it doesn’t leave a lot of room for displaying motion. He is abundantly clear about the terms under which these veterans donated their data: “Even after giving everything to the country, they found it in their hearts to give more without expecting anything in return.” That gift, which constituted the nation’s largest biorepository and most ancestrally varied cohorts of veteran data in the world, created a debt that Madduri, who also holds a joint appointment in the Department of Human Genetics at the University of Chicago, is working to repay. “What I feel when I look at that data is that I have an obligation to deliver to the veterans on their intentions in doing this,” he says. Those intentions, as he reads it, are that others, veteran or not, can benefit from whatever the data can reveal. After arriving at Argonne as a graduate student in 2001, Madduri has spent the last two decades at the intersection of supercomputing and biomedicine, much of the time building the computing that turns the veterans’ data into results impacting areas from post-traumatic stress disorder to heart disease to cancer. He explains that machines have largely held up their end, but the obstacles between precious data and delivered results are not always solved with faster machines. Understanding the challenges is the path toward better science. Any two human genomes differ at millions of specific positions. A genome-wide association study asks a seemingly simple question across an enormous volume of data. For example, does a given genetic variant travel together with a particular medical condition? On its own, the question is simple. Multiplied across every variant worth testing and every condition recorded in a patient's chart, it becomes an enormous math problem, which is what supercomputers exist to solve. The harder question is whether the code is written to let them. Madduri's team has run this problem on the Summit system at DOE's Oak Ridge Leadership Computing Facility and on the Aurora system at the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility. On Summit, the team found that the job would not finish on the machine's CPUs. As the cohort grew, the matrix the analysis needed for each group no longer fit in memory, and the job stalled. “We decided to open the box and look at the code,” Madduri said. They found that the issue was not merely a speed problem, but a coding one. The analysis had been written for ordinary processors because that is what the genetics community was running on. Underneath the algorithms, though, the code spent nearly all of its time doing one thing: multiplying very large grids of numbers. This is exactly the operation that graphics processors exist to perform, and what makes them the engine of modern artificial intelligence training. As Madduri notes, the same computational architecture accelerating AI discovery “is also foundational for population genomics.” So, the team, led on this effort by Argonne's Alex Rodriguez, rewrote that part of the code for GPUs, and an analysis that would have taken roughly 481,000 CPU-core hours in the original version became tractable. This shift did more than speed up a slow job. It made a virtually impossible job possible. The workhorse they accelerated is a widely used genetics package called SAIGE, which made the rewritten version SAIGE-GPU, described in a separate paper in Bioinformatics. "We are not good at coming up with names. Sorry," Madduri says sheepishly. Before SAIGE-GPU, nearly every genome-wide association study started by picking a disease then looking for the variants linked to it. Madduri is blunt about the cost of that approach. “One of the challenges I found in biomedical research is that it is very incremental,” he explains. “Every year, they get new datasets, they analyze the data, they find a new set of genetic variants, and this happens across all of the different diseases.” The reason, he adds, is limited access to large-scale computing. His team chose not to be incremental and decided to solve the computational challenge once and for all. “One of the challenges we set for ourselves was to just take all the data that we have available, all the genetic data for all the veterans that have been sequenced as part of the Million Veteran Program,” Madduri says. The team, the Million Veteran Program consortium, published the results in Science, with Madduri's group among the authors, and published the computational work separately in Bioinformatics. The study reported on 2,068 traits across 635,969 veterans, and the results flagged 13,672 regions of the genome linked to one or more conditions. About 1,600 of these regions surfaced only because nearly a third of the participants were not of European ancestry. According to the Science paper, most genetic studies to this point have been drawn overwhelmingly from people of European descent. These 1,600 associations were previously “invisible” in the populations studied. That ancestral range also shaped how the analysis had to be run. The groups could not simply be pooled. That is, if a condition is more common in one ancestral group than another, the analysis has no way to tell a genuine disease marker from a marker of simply belonging to that group. Each group had to be run separately, and the results combined afterward. The 1,600 associations exist in no single group's results. They appear only when the separate analyses are combined. Madduri is careful about what the whole exercise means and what it does not. “This is all preclinical,” he says. “This is the discovery phase.” The analysis produces a statistical map of where to look. How much more can nuclear power plants give?

In this age of rising electricity demand, people are trying to squeeze every last bit of power out of every power plant possible. Over the past few weeks, there’s been big news about nuclear power plants trying to do just that. Constellation, the largest nuclear operator in the US, announced plans to add nearly a gigawatt of new capacity across 11 existing nuclear reactors at six plants. And on October 5, the Department of Energy awarded a $4.2 billion loan to Vistra for previously announced upgrades at three plants. Pushing existing reactors to produce more could help provide more clean power for data centers, quickly. But just how much additional power can we count on? Upgrading equipment at existing nuclear power plants can allow them to reach a higher capacity; the process is called a power uprate. Operators have been doing this since the 1970s in the US, but it’s been less common in recent years as demand for electricity plateaued and cheap natural gas put financial pressure on nuclear power plants. Some plants closed, and putting in more money to produce more electricity didn’t make much sense at others. Between 2001 and 2010, the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission approved over 80 uprates. Between 2011 and 2020, the number was 33. But with the rise of AI and the related boom in electricity demand, there’s been a shift. We’re seeing shuttered plants like Palisades and Three Mile Island race to reopen. And existing plants are looking to uprate again. Some uprates are minor, involving changes to instrument setpoints or calculations at the plant. But more major overhauls, which can boost reactor power by as much as 20%, require replacing plant equipment. Generally, uprates involve using fuel that’s more highly enriched, or a higher volume of fresh fuel, in the nuclear reactor. That helps produce more steam, which is used to turn a turbine and generate electricity. To handle that additional energy, the main turbines and equipment such as pumps, motors, generators, and transformers are upgraded. The most recent major uprate approved in the US was for three reactors at the Browns Ferry nuclear power plant in Alabama. A $475 million project saw that plant expand its power capacity by 14% between 2018 and 2019. Things could pick up soon, though. Constellation announced plans this week to spend more than $4.3 billion on uprates to 11 reactors. The additional electricity will be enough to power roughly 700,000 US homes. Behind it all? Big Tech money. Google agreed to buy electricity from these plants, and the 20-year agreement was a major factor in making the uprates happen. As Joe Dominguez, Constellation’s CEO, told the New York Times: “These are capital intensive, labor intensive projects that require a different price point than the market trades at, frankly.” Also in the last few weeks, the US Department of Energy shared that it will commit over $4 billion to help finance upgrades and modernization for Vistra nuclear power plants across Pennsylvania and Ohio. Those projects will add 433 megawatts of power capacity, and Meta has already signed on to buy electricity from the plants in a long-term deal. A note about cost: On a per-kilowatt basis, that Constellation project is less expensive than building a new reactor, but it’s still more expensive than building a new utility-scale solar farm with energy storage or a combined-cycle natural-gas plant. Even at this high cost, uprates are in high demand. Today, the US nuclear fleet has roughly 98 gigawatts of total power capacity. Depending on who you ask, there’s somewhere between three and eight additional gigawatts on the table from uprates. A pretty significant chunk of the potential uprates in the US are already accounted for—the NRC already expects applications to add 2.4 gigawatts of power to reactors through 2032. It’s crucial to put these plans (and the total potential for uprates in the US) in perspective, though. PJM, the grid operator that covers much of the East Coast, expects about 30 gigawatts of additional peak demand because of AI by 2030. All the possible nationwide nuclear uprates would cover just a fraction of that. Making the most of existing nuclear reactors is a relatively easy win at a time when we need all the zero-emissions power we can get. But there’s still a lot of demand, and if we want more nuclear power to meet it, eventually that will require actually building new reactors. In 1970, the neurosurgeon Robert White removed the head of a rhesus monkey and transplanted it onto the body of another. Remarkably, the reattached heads could move their eyes and mouths, but because he’d severed the monkeys’ spinal cords, those heads never gained control of their new bodies. Canavero says that’s not necessarily true. He claims people’s views on the operation will change once they accept that reconnecting severed spinal cords is already possible. They’d be able to limit the risk of organ rejection, too—potentially by preparing recipients, and their immune systems, beforehand. “With real organ banking … instead of the patients waiting for the organ, it’s the organs that are waiting for the patients,” says Finger. Scientists have been able to cool cells to ultra-low temperatures through a process called vitrification, which essentially turns them to something like glass. This procedure is already used to store eggs, sperm, and embryos. Vitrifying whole organs is much harder. Some problems can be avoided with cryoprotectants—chemicals that work like antifreeze and prevent damaging ice crystals from forming. But these chemicals tend to be incredibly toxic. It’s also difficult to rapidly and uniformly cool large tissues, and just as hard to evenly rewarm an organ all the way through. Working with John Bischof, a bioengineer at the University of Minnesota, Finger and colleagues have come up with a solution. Once an organ has been loaded with a cryoprotectant, they infuse it with iron oxide nanoparticles. These nanoparticles don’t do anything to the cells. But when it’s time to rewarm the organ, they can be activated with an electromagnetic field. When the radiofrequency of that field switches between two states, the particles also flip, realigning themselves with the field. That causes them to release heat throughout the organ. Adjusting the power of the field allows the team to control the rate of heating so that it gets faster once the organ reaches -120°C. The researchers successfully vitrified rat kidneys using this approach in work published in 2023. Back then, they were able to rewarm kidneys that had been kept in a freezer for up to 100 days before transplanting them into rats. Since then, the team has been trying to achieve the same success with bigger organs. Pig kidneys are thought to be a good model for human ones—they’re about the same size and look similar. Rat kidneys weigh around one gram; pig kidneys are around 100. It hasn’t been easy. The cryoprotectant used for vitrifying rat kidneys didn’t work as well in the pig organs. “We had to rediscover and modify the cocktail,” says Bischof. “That’s why it’s taken us three years.” The first step in the procedure is to remove a kidney from a donor pig. Once it’s out, the kidney is flushed with a commonly used organ preservation solution to remove the blood, bagged, and temporarily laid on a bed of ice in a cooler so it can be taken over to the engineering lab. There, team members unpack the kidney and hook it up to a device they’ve designed to control the organ’s temperature and pressure. Then they start to load it up with the cocktail of cryoprotectants, slowly enough to ensure an even spread through the organ but quickly enough to minimize any toxic effects. It takes around two hours. The next step is to infuse the organ with the iron oxide nanoparticles, which are pumped through the organ for around seven to eight minutes, says Finger. Then it’s time to cool the organ by pumping cold nitrogen vapor into a chamber housing it. It’s a delicate process, and the team must ensure that the organ is cooling all the way through. At the same time, they must lower the kidney’s temperature as quickly as possible to prevent ice from forming. “Oftentimes there are multiple people doing different things to make sure that things don’t go awry,” says Bischof. Once the organ reaches the vitrified state at around -120 °C, it “behaves like a glass,” says Finger. “At that point, you can stay stable for long periods of time—we think indefinitely.” The team then pauses the cooling process to ensure that the organ is glassy throughout. Otherwise, it could crack. After the pause, if everything goes well, the kidney is ultimately cooled to -150 °C. Once it’s in the freezer, it can stay there for days, weeks, or even months. The team has kept a small number of kidneys banked for over a year. The real test of these kidneys comes when they’re taken out of the freezer, rewarmed, and flushed of their chemicals and nanoparticles. Can they support life? So far, the team has tested some of them in a couple of different ways. In one set of experiments, the researchers put rewarmed kidneys on perfusion devices—systems that are already used in transplant surgeries both to provide organs with nutrients and to help doctors assess them while they’re out of the body. In another set of experiments, Finger and his colleagues transplanted rewarmed kidneys into pigs and monitored them for two hours. In both experiments, the rewarmed kidneys seemed to recover, says Finger. “They pink right up,” he says. There are lots of ways to measure the function of a kidney, but one key test is whether or not the organ can make urine. By this measure, the rewarmed kidneys did well, although they produced around two-thirds of the amount that would normally be expected. “They produce urine … within a minute or so,” says Finger. “We know that they work.” Most of the rewarmed kidneys had been banked for days or weeks. But one had been in the freezer for just over a year. When Finger and his colleagues rewarmed it and put it on a perfusion machine, they found that it produced urine “immediately.” “It actually looked better than many of the [other] kidneys,” says Finger. “We’re comfortable now saying we can store them for a year and rewarm them with excellent function.” “It’s a very big deal to go a year,” says Kevin Myer, president and CEO of LifeGift, an organ procurement organization based in Texas. Being able to store organs for that length of time would transform organ transplantation. He envisions banks of organs that could be used on demand in emergency cases where people can’t wait, such as for some drug overdoses or gunshot wounds. But the procedure isn’t ready for prime time just yet. As well as producing less urine than expected, the rewarmed kidneys also showed signs of injury. The damage seems to be caused by the cryoprotectant chemicals, say the researchers. “This is one big breakthrough that solves half the problem,” as Kilbride puts it. The team has shown that the rewarmed kidneys can regain blood flow, which is “very important," says Greg Fahy, a cryobiologist who is considered a pioneer in the field. But he’s worried about the extent of the damage to the organs. “We can’t tell from these experiments whether these kidneys might have supported life or not.” But Fahy, who is chief scientific officer at the biotech companies Intervene Immune and 21st Century Medicine (where he is also executive director), acknowledges that it’s not clear how these kidneys might fare once they’re inside a living animal for the long term. Finger also hopes to find ways to repair the damage seen in the kidneys. Other research has shown that the health of transplant organs can improve while they’re on perfusion machines. And damaged kidneys might undergo some level of self-repair once they’re in a body. At any rate, human kidneys may cope better with the process than pig kidneys, says Finger. The next step will be to test the kidneys in pigs over longer periods of time. The team, along with many other researchers in the field, will continue the search for less toxic cryoprotectants, too. Finger, Bischof, and their colleagues are also planning experiments with human kidneys. In February 2007, the hulking dual-­rotor casualty evacuation helicopter had just flown three wounded soldiers to a field hospital in Balad, north of Baghdad, when another call came: an emergency mission to fly through an insurgent-controlled area in broad daylight. Typically, helicopters would be in a spot like this only at night, and with all lights turned off. As it passed near the city of Karmah, in the eastern side of Al-Anbar province, an insurgent fired a portable missile launcher. The tail gunner, who likely saw the shot roaring in, yelled for the pilot to make an evasive move. But the missile had already locked on, and the ensuing blast started a fire on board. After the pilot battled the failing machine for 58 agonizing seconds, Morphine 1-2 plunged to the ground. All seven crew members were killed. Why was the helo there in daylight? “It was getting blood and bringing it back. It was [on] what we call a ‘blood run,’ and [it’s] the highest of priorities besides casualty evacuation,” explains Lieutenant Commander Robert Murray, a Navy officer and program manager at DARPA, the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency, who studied this incident as an intelligence officer. Today, a chronic shortage of blood in both the military and civilian worlds is fueling a race to develop synthetic alternatives. About 90% of preventable battlefield deaths are due to hemorrhage—bleeding that happens when a blood vessel ruptures—and it’s the leading cause of maternal deaths worldwide. A study published in Transfusion in 2025 found that of 1.2 million trauma patients in the United States who potentially needed blood before reaching a hospital, just 1% received it. The shortage results in about 10,000 preventable deaths each year in the US, according to the American College of Surgeons. Globally, that figure is likely in the millions. One reason for the shortfall is that blood has a storage problem. Whole blood lasts only 35 days when refrigerated, so banks separate out components to stretch the timeline. But that’s an imperfect solution. The platelets that help wounds clot are typically stored at room temperature and last for a week or less. Hospitals and the military freeze the red blood cells that carry oxygen, as well as the protein-rich plasma that restores volume and aids coagulation. Once thawed, though, both will spoil within days. “What happens if I don’t know when I’m going to be attacked? Well, thaw it and you let it expire, right?” Murray says. A JAMA study of combat injuries in Afghanistan suggests a magic number for someone who needs a transfusion: You have 36 minutes to get started. That’s why Murray—who, in addition to his military experience, has a PhD in physiology—is leading a DARPA effort to address blood’s battlefield problems. He’s in charge of a new $73 million dollar program called RAPIID (for “resuscitation and prevention of ischemia-induced dysfunction”). Its goal is to create shelf-stable versions of the three major transfusion constituents: plasma, platelets, and red blood cells. As it gets underway late this year, RAPIID picks up on decades of work to synthesize blood or help it last longer. Most recently, that includes a prior $47.5 million DARPA project called FSHARP (for “fieldable solutions for hemorrhage with bio-artificial resuscitation products”), in which researchers have demonstrated promising results in animals. With RAPIID trials for the different components occurring in parallel, the goal is to have bags of dried blood products deployed in the field by 2029. That puts Murray in the position of a conductor coordinating an orchestra of clinical trials with the FDA, while also sourcing the paraphernalia that goes along with transfusions—everything from combat-durable storage bags to tests that could check how much artificial blood a patient already has. Getting it all done will be tough, but the military is feeling the pressure to address its shortage. Blood drives may not deliver in time after a mass-casualty event, and a JAMA study of combat injuries in Afghanistan suggests that if the injured person needs a transfusion, the magic number is 36 minutes. “If something is survivable—and generally we classify most all hemorrhage as survivable—[you’re] almost guaranteed to survive if you get the blood before that 36-minute mark,” Murray says. After that, the chance of survival “starts to drop precipitously.” In the mission to make portable, shelf-stable blood components, plasma is the low-hanging fruit. Researchers have known how to isolate and freeze-dry plasma, the yellowish liquid that makes up 55% of whole human blood, since the late 1930s. On the battlefields of World War II, its mix of water, salts, and thousands of types of proteins helped restore blood volume in wounded soldiers—and to some degree controlled bleeding. In the 1960s, the US abandoned freeze-dried plasma because it carried a risk of transmitting hepatitis to recipients from infected donors. But screening, testing, and drying techniques improved, and starting in 2010, the US military launched a yearslong effort to rebuild its capacity for producing these products. Over the past two years, the FDA has allowed two types of freeze-dried plasma to be used in emergencies by the US military—and one can even be used by civilians. As for platelets, efforts at extending their life date back to the 1950s, but they haven’t seen a ton of success. Bioengineers have, for example, attempted to attach the membranes of platelets to synthetic particles. The most successful project comes from a company called Cellphire, which freeze-dries actual human platelets. The product has shown safety and efficacy in humans in clinical trials, but its dependence on donors means it’s unclear whether this approach can scale. Red blood cells have been similarly tough to preserve or mimic. Their most critical component is hemoglobin, which carries oxygen and comes encased in a cell membrane. For more than a century, researchers have been looking for ways to deliver hemoglobin without the immunological challenges created by this exterior, including the fact that donors’ and recipients’ blood types need to be compatible. Evolutionary pressure developed that wrapper for very good reason, though: “Naked” hemoglobin tends to break down quickly in the bloodstream, and that can cause organ damage. It also scavenges nitric oxide, our body’s natural mechanism for opening up blood vessels, and causes those channels to constrict. This problem became all too clear in the 1980s and 1990s, when fears that transfusions could transmit HIV sparked interest in a class of membrane-free delivery mechanisms called hemoglobin-based oxygen carriers (HBOCs). One, named HemAssist, consisted of hemoglobin subunits linked together to avoid the rapid-breakdown problem. It had the added benefit of lasting roughly a year in cold storage (red blood cells last about 40 days)—but it didn’t solve the nitric oxide problem, so it constricted blood vessels. In a clinical trial, 24 of the 52 people who received HemAssist died, compared with eight out of 46 in the control group. The parent company terminated trials in 1998. A review of clinical studies published in JAMA in 2008 further solidified HBOCs’ fate, finding that they generally triple patients’ chances of heart attack and increase the risk of death by 30%. More recently, researchers have tried to grow red blood cells in a lab using a technology called “blood pharming,” which involves making them from progenitors known as induced pluripotent stem cells. The stumbling blocks? Cost and yield. DARPA launched a pharming initiative in 2008, using stem cells from discarded umbilical-cord blood, but was unable to get the cost below tens of thousands of dollars per unit of red blood cells. They pulled the plug and offloaded the research to the Uniformed Services University. Even if the cost were lower, though, pharmed blood can’t be stored in a backpack. “These are still cells—they still require a refrigerator, they still require special handling,” Murray says. It won’t be easy to solve all these challenges at once. “Each one of those projects individually is a massive undertaking,” says John Holcomb, a surgery professor at the University of Alabama at Birmingham, who was a combat surgeon in Mogadishu, Somalia, during the battle that inspired Black Hawk Down. “I think that they will probably be successful individually. How we use those individual products, or combine those products together, I think remains to be seen.” Several years after DARPA gave up on lab-grown blood, Murray was stationed at a base on Okinawa, an island 400 miles southwest of the Japanese mainland, where he witnessed the vulnerability of the blood supply firsthand. The site houses a blood bank that serves the roughly 27,000 Marines commanded from the island—plus thousands more military personnel on the base and deployed within a 100-million-square-mile stretch of the Pacific called USPACOM, which ranges from western India to Hawaii and from Antarctica to the Arctic. “The blood center is right next to the hospital, which is about 200 meters from all of the satellite communications on that island,” Murray told me. A missile strike could take out all three. Murray wondered: Is there a way to produce synthetic blood that could last longer? As he completed his PhD in physiology in 2018, the military was also awakening to the vulnerability of the Okinawa facility. The 2018 National Defense Strategy, which shifted military focus from terrorism to a potential ground war with China or Russia, showed war planners the risks of relying on a single site for blood runs covering all of USPACOM. DARPA then got serious once again about making fake blood and kicked off the FSHARP program in 2023. Murray came aboard as a deputy program manager in DARPA’s Biological Technologies Office in 2024 and was named a program manager in 2026, just a few months before RAPIID put out its call for proposals this past May. Before he took the helm, related work had been underway for years. Aided by FSHARP funding, researchers at a company called KaloCyte continued testing a technique—originally developed by doctors who are now at the University of Maryland, Penn State, and the University of Pittsburgh—that “upcycles” hemoglobin from expiring donor blood. “We take that hemoglobin and then we package it in a lipid nanoparticle that functions like an artificial cell membrane,” explains CEO Elaine Haynes. The product, called ErythroMer, is essentially a new kind of HBOC. The membrane prevents breakdown, limits dangerous scavenging of nitric oxide, and also helps the hemoglobin take up and release oxygen. Each ErythroMer cell includes a lipid called KC-1003, which acts like a sentry: In response to high pH in the lungs, it signals another small molecule within ErythroMer to move away, allowing oxygen in. Then, in tired tissue, where pH is lower, KC-1003 brings the small molecule back, releasing the oxygen. Using a well-understood process called lyophilization, similar to one that World War II–era scientists used with plasma, the team freeze-dries its product to store at room temperature, where it can remain stable for two years. In a 2016 study of mice and rats with up to 70% of their blood removed, ErythroMer stabilized blood pressure and oxygen delivery—without the devastating constriction of blood vessels that sank HemAssist. FSHARP furthered that work, helping fund studies on how the HBOC interacted with plasma and platelets. Much like the handle of a Swiss Army knife, the synthetic platelet has specialized attachments—peptides that serve as the biological equivalents of a corkscrew or a nail file. KaloCyte, though, still has plenty to figure out. Haynes says the company can source only about 1,000 to 2,000 units of expiring hemoglobin a month. So it has licensed a yeast strain that it aims to use to create its own hemoglobin in the lab. Over a video call from her desk, Haynes showed me a sample of ready-to-use ErythroMer. It was encased in a heavy-duty storage bag with two chambers. One was full of scarlet powder—the hemoglobin product—and the other of sterile water to rapidly rehydrate it in the field. Eventually, she explained, under Murray’s master plan, “you’ll have another container with the synthetic platelet, and then a third container with the plasma.” But achieving that vision depends on finding a synthetic platelet that works. To do that, DARPA has turned to Anirban Sen Gupta, a bioengineering professor at Case Western Reserve University. “Traditionally platelets were thought of [as] blood cement, but it’s not that simple,” he explains. “They are, in my opinion, like drones, surveilling the blood vessel wall continuously, looking for signs of injury.” Sen Gupta’s team, which also includes collaborators at the University of Pittsburgh, used FSHARP funding to continue work on a product called SynthoPlate. Fabricated from a lipid nanoparticle that he likens to the handle of a Swiss Army knife, it has specialized peptide attachments—the biological equivalents of a corkscrew or a nail file. The first version has three: One binds to a chemical called von Willebrand factor, which helps platelets stick to the walls of injured blood vessels; another attaches to collagen, which acts as a scaffold for regrowing cells; and the third mimics a blood protein called fibrinogen, which acts like a Lego connector that allows the synthetic platelets to stick to real ones near a wound. Like ErythroMer, the product is freeze-dried and can be reconstituted with sterile water. It keeps for over a year at room temperature and at least three months at a desertlike 122 °F. In a 2026 paper in Advanced Science, the researchers showed that SynthoPlate cut bleeding time and blood loss by more than half in mice with low platelet counts. In a separate study, they also safely injected the faux platelets into rats’ bone marrow—a more useful delivery site, given that a bleeding patient might not have an available vein. Sen Gupta and a colleague have spun this work out into a company called Haima Therapeutics. The good news, as he sees it, is that the pharmaceutical industry excels at mass-­producing millions of billions of lipid nanoparticles—witness Operation Warp Speed, in which these blobs of fat held delicate snippets of mRNA in covid shots. “The magic of the artificial platelet is in all those peptides that are decorating the surface of the particle, but the scalability of the platform is very well established,” he says. Around the world, prospective blood alternatives abound. Researchers at Cambridge University in the UK are trying to pharm red blood cells for people with rare blood types or blood disorders like sickle cell anemia, and other researchers have also cultured platelets and red blood cells. Japanese scientists are already testing their own version of encapsulated human hemoglobin in human subjects. Over the course of the next three years, RAPIID will move DARPA’s efforts closer to real life. In addition to ErythroMer and SynthoPlate, the agency in early October announced funding for five other companies working on blood components. Among them is a firm called Abioremedi that eschews hemoglobin for synthetic versions of other oxygen-carrying proteins such as cytoglobin, neuroglobin, and myoglobin encased in albumin—the most abundant protein in human plasma. Octapharma USA won the sole contract to supply plasma to RAPIID and will also be competing in platelets with an FDA-approved pair of clotting proteins it affixes to a disc-shaped nanoparticle. With those players in place, the agency will focus for the next year on getting technologies through large FDA-regulated animal studies. Murray has set up a bit of a Hunger Games scenario: Each contractee working on blood components has only one year of guaranteed funding. He says he’s taking advantage of advancements in the field since FSHARP launched but is also hedging against any manufacturing or regulatory setbacks ahead of his 2029 deadline. Getting cut doesn’t mean the product is bad, just that the time frame was too demanding. The second and third years will focus on human trials, overseen by the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus. That will include using the products in situations where the risk of side effects is outweighed by the danger of doing nothing, such as with severely injured people in ambulances. The possibility of fatal outcomes has invited second-guessing about DARPA’s choices. For Shibani Pati, who directs the Center for Research in Transfusion Medicine and Cell Therapies at the University of California, San Francisco, Murray could be making things too “DARPA hard” on himself by backing products like SynthoPlate and ErythroMer, which are previously untested in humans. “They need to make a very concerted effort to take what they’ve already developed and push it through to approval through the FDA,” she says. “Not start with moonshot projects that will take forever to get through.” For platelets, Pati thinks the RAPIID program would have done better to tap Cellphire, since its products have already received millions from the Pentagon and shown safety and efficacy in humans. (Murray notes that he can only select companies that apply, and Cellphire did not.) She has similar qualms about ErythroMer. A different HBOC that sources hemoglobin from cattle, called Hemopure, has been approved for use since 2001 in South Africa, she notes. And though it hasn’t been approved by the FDA—it was part of the fateful 2008 JAMA study—US doctors are cleared to use it for patients who have bad immune responses or religious objections to conventional transfusions. Holcomb, the surgery professor, worries the effort will run into issues with interactions between the products. He notes that individual blood components have more than one role: Red blood cells also help with clotting, platelets also are anti-inflammatory, and plasma helps tune coagulation. There’s no guarantee that the versions RAPIID helps develop will work in the same ways. Murray says that FSHARP’s animal studies tested all the products together, using whole blood as a baseline, precisely because of such possible interactions. The cocktail met its program metrics in all but one area: The clotting was not quite as good, perhaps because negative charges in some molecules repel one another. “I do not think they will replicate all the functions that whole blood does in our body,” Holcomb says. But, he admits, “you may not need to, right? When you get seriously injured and you lose 50% of your intravascular volume, you’re dead, right? So if you lose 30% and maybe you’re hanging on to life just barely, maybe you show up with all these different products, and you have enough whole blood left in you to do these other functions.” Murray is determined to complete trials so that shelf-stable blood can be delivered in 2029. In addition to the storage bags, he’s already sourcing finger sticks for in-field blood testing and a battery-powered device to heat up the blood components.
