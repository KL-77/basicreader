Physicists listened to electrical noise to crack a superconductor mystery

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-08

Visit Original Website
Superconductors carry electricity without resistance, but most only work a few degrees above absolute zero. In the late 1980s, copper-based materials called cuprates were found to superconduct at higher temperatures—reachable with affordable liquid nitrogen. Yet nearly 40 years later, physicists still don’t agree on why. In a recent Science study, a team of physicists led by Milan Allan of Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich and Ingmar Swart of Utrecht University in the Netherlands has found some answers by listening to the faint electrical crackle of current flowing into a cuprate. Central to the mystery of cuprates’ high-temperature superconductivity is a strange state called the pseudogap. “The pseudogap is a phenomenon of missing electrons,” Allan said. Above the temperature at which cuprates become superconducting, some of their electrons seem to vanish—measurements find far fewer of them at the energies where they should be. “Everybody knows that from different probes, you see that there’s these vanishing electrons, but nobody knows why,” Allan said. Over the decades, physicists have put forward two main competing explanations. The first hypothesizes that the electrons get stuck in some sort of structure. In this view, electrons freeze into patterns, such as charge density waves, and superconductivity develops separately. The alternative explanation tightly links this phenomenon to superconductivity. “Other people think they’re missing because they form pairs,” Allan said. Pairs of electrons, called Cooper pairs, are what carry current in a superconductor. If, as the material cools, these pairs appear before the material becomes superconducting, they would be a precondition of superconductivity, a waiting room of sorts, where pairs exist but can’t yet move together in lockstep. Settling this proved extremely hard. “Lots of different experimental signatures are kind of lumped inside this,” Allan said. Different instruments all see something, but their readings, Allan said, are never conclusive. “It’s always something kind of incoherent, hard to grasp,” he said.  What the researchers needed was a way to see pairs directly, and Allan and his colleagues figured it out. The team decided to listen to shot noise—tiny fluctuations caused by the arrival of individual charges that form the stream of an electric current. Allan says it resembles rain drumming on the roof.  “If you imagine that you have raindrops and then all of a sudden you make the drops twice as heavy, there’ll be more noise,” Allan said. “With the electrons it’s similar. If you have two electrons coming always together, because they’re associated with pairing, then it will be more noise.” The researchers did this measurement with a scanning tunneling microscope, which passed current between a needle-sharp tip and a cuprate sample, letting them listen to the noise at specific energy levels and different locations on the surface. In conventional superconductors, pairing produces a clean doubling of the noise. In cuprates, the signal is much weaker, because single electrons keep flowing alongside the pairs. Making the instrument sensitive enough to discern the pairs took years. “What it took is very talented and persistent students who worked for, I don’t know, four years on this to get it working,” Allan said. “There was not a single trick. It was electronics craftsmanship.” The team examined crystals of a cuprate known as BSCCO, cooled to a few degrees above absolute zero, and compared the noise to the energies at which electrons go missing. By adjusting the voltage between the tip of the needle and the sample, the team could choose the energy of the electrons they were listening to, a bit like turning a radio dial. If the pseudogap came purely from frozen patterns, the team reasoned, the extra noise of pairing should only kick in at energy levels associated with superconductivity. Instead, pairing showed up right at the pseudogap energy. “The fact that there is more noise indicates that pairing is associated with this large gap,” Allan said. The pseudogap’s energy varies from spot to spot on the surface, but wherever the team listened, the extra noise always started rising at the local pseudogap energy. The same pattern held in samples with different chemical compositions. Ruling out other sources of noise, such as atoms shifting on the surface, took a lot of effort. “Noise is super hard to interpret, and that was basically the thing that we had the most sleepless nights about,” Allan said. The decisive check involved placing the samples in magnetic fields of up to six tesla, which suppresses pairing while leaving the overall pattern of missing electrons largely intact. As the magnetic field grew, the noise dropped back toward the single-electron level, exactly as expected if pairs were behind it. The strength of electron pairing can be expressed as the energy required to pull a pair apart. Lowering the temperature helps boost pairing because the warmer a material becomes, the more violently its particles jostle and the more pairs get broken. In conventional superconductors, two ingredients needed for superconductivity—pair formation and phase coherence—occur simultaneously at the critical temperature. (Phase coherence is where the electrons start moving together as a fluid.) In cuprates, Allan and Swart’s study points to a different sequence. The pairing energy the team measured is well above the temperature at which the material superconducts. “So the picture that emerges is that you first have pre-formed pairs, and then only at lower temperatures, phase coherence sets in,” Swart said. That suggests the superconductivity bottleneck is not getting electrons to pair, but getting the pairs to move together. “If you have pairing at such high energies, that means that you might be able to push Tc [superconducting temperature] higher,” Allan said. “This is something people hoped for in the very early days of cuprates, and now everybody’s afraid to even mention that. But to me it gives hope.” An optimistic reading of the results is that cuprate electrons could theoretically pair at room temperature, and we only need to make them coherent to achieve a room-temperature superconductor. The team, though, thinks that’s a bit naive. “I think Ingmar and I both believe that it’s not going to be that simple, because you have all kinds of other effects that are coupled there,” Allan said. The researchers also stress that their experiment does not fully answer the central question. It rules out the idea that the pseudogap comes from frozen patterns alone, but not that such patterns are intertwined with pairing. “Whatever the detailed explanation of the pseudogap is, pairing should be part of it,” Swart said. Because the technique used in the study loses sensitivity as temperature rises, the team couldn’t yet listen above the superconducting temperature, where the pseudogap is usually defined. “Hopefully other groups will do that,” Allan said. “What limits Tc, especially if the energy of pairing is high? What happens at Tc? That’s for me the most interesting question.” Swart wants to make noise maps of the surface with higher resolution and, eventually, an instrument that can detect coherence directly, which he hopes should give us separate markers for the onset of phase coherence and electron pairing. Allan, meanwhile, increasingly thinks the route to better superconductors runs through chemistry, aided by AI, rather than pure physics. “When it comes to trying to make the superconducting material better and increase Tc, I have more hope in direct trial and error,” he said.

The Bantu expansion changed Africa. Scientists are still tracing how.

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-07

Visit Original Website
It’s not easy to extract iron from its ore. But the peoples of the ancient city of Great Zimbabwe—the capital of a thriving kingdom in southern Africa between 1000 to 1700 AD—were masters. At sites near the city’s stone ruins, clusters of iron-smelting furnaces survive today as mounds of rubble. At Mashava, a village 25 miles northwest, fragments bear fingermarks of those who long ago smoothed the structures’ clay interiors. Some of the furnaces were nearly an adult’s arm span in width, with walls thick and strong to contain the heat of more than 2,100° Fahrenheit needed to smelt iron. Unlike other furnaces that required workers to force air into them with bellows, these were designed to suck air in, then out through a chimney, no human labor required. This would have made it significantly easier to produce the iron, which was fashioned into hoes, axes, and arrowheads. “The number and size of these furnaces, together with the use of natural-draft technology, indicate intensive and technologically sophisticated iron production,” says archaeometallurgist Ezekiel Mtetwa of Chicago’s Field Museum of Natural History, who has long studied the furnaces. Scientists are extremely interested in the peoples who created this and other iron-powered societies across eastern and southern Africa. Research points to an adaptable and innovative group from western Africa as the masterminds. Known as Bantu speakers, these communities began gradually expanding across the continent’s southern half, starting from 6,000 to 4,000 years ago. In doing so, they introduced a fundamentally new, sedentary way of life—characterized by farming, raising livestock, pottery-making, and metalworking—to regions previously dominated by hunter-gatherer communities. This migration transformed not only the region’s history and technology, but also its languages: Today, around 350 million Africans speak one of roughly 500 distinct Bantu tongues, from southern African Zulu to eastern African Swahili. And at least as many Africans—as well as many African Americans and Afro-Latinos—carry Bantu ancestry in their DNA. Reconstructing this expansion has been a tough puzzle, especially given the region’s sparse written historical records and limited research funding. But a unique blend of scientists—linguists, archaeologists, and geneticists—have teamed up to unearth clues about the deep origins of the Bantu-speaking people, their path across Africa, and their relationships with other cultures. Although much debate and uncertainty remain, little by little the work is uncovering one of history’s largest and longest human migrations. “It completely reshaped the demography in sub-Saharan Africa, and we are really interested in trying to understand it,” says population geneticist Sandra Beleza of the United Kingdom’s University of Leicester. The first evidence of the Bantu expansion came from observations of similarities among Bantu languages. They’re so alike, in fact, that when the Portuguese explorer Vasco da Gama sailed around the southern tip of Africa in 1497, a Portuguese crew member who had spent time in west-central Africa could reportedly communicate with locals in southeastern Africa. Linguists working in the 19th century started referring to the language family as Bantu, the plural of muntu, meaning “human being” in many Bantu tongues. Later linguists reasoned that the languages stemmed from an ancestral people who migrated across the continent from western Africa, which harbors the greatest diversity of Bantu languages (as would be expected for where the languages originated). Soon, archaeologists found supporting evidence: across eastern and southern Africa, pieces of pottery of similar yet diverse styles, alongside first-time traces of village life, metal-making, domesticated animals, and western African crops—all developed within the last 2,000 years. Northern sites usually dated back to earlier time periods than sites farther south. “You can clearly see a north-to-south progression,” says archaeological scientist Shadreck Chirikure of the University of Oxford. By the 1990s, however, some scholars began to question the idea that Bantu languages and skills spread through a migration. Couldn’t these cultural habits have spread while people stayed in the same place, with hunter gatherer communities passing along new technologies, languages, and habits to each other? Genetic techniques that could analyze people’s ancestry helped to resolve that debate. All of us share DNA with our relatives: 50 percent on average with siblings, 12.5 percent with cousins, 3.3 percent with second cousins, and so on. Though these proportions become minute the further back in time a shared common ancestor lived, statistical techniques can tease out patterns. Sure enough, a 2009 genetic analysis of 2,432 Africans found that Bantu-speaking people from western, central, and southern Africa are much more closely related than one might expect given their geographic distance. And in 2024, a team of scientists analyzed the genomes of 1,763 Africans and found the greatest genetic diversity among Bantu speakers in western Africa. As distance from the region increased, diversity declined. “This is a typical signature of a population expanding from one place to another,” explains coauthor Carina Schlebusch, a geneticist at Uppsala University in Sweden. But while linguistic and genetic data point to western Africa—particularly the Grassfields region of Cameroon—as the Bantu homeland, conclusive archaeological proof is thin. At the best-studied archaeological site in the Grassfields, a rock shelter called Shum Laka, pottery fragments appear dating from 7,000 to 6,000 years ago and become abundant by 5,000 to 4,000 years ago. Bowls and jars at first decorated simply with incisions and stamps and later bearing the marks of carved or braided tools rolled over wet clay are a possible, yet inconclusive, trace of Bantu ancestors. South of Cameroon, archaeologists have searched for artifacts in one of the Bantu-speaking people’s first destinations: the Congo rainforest. A handful of times between 2010 and 2023, archaeologist Alexandre Livingstone Smith of Belgium’s Royal Museum for Central Africa traveled via boat along the Congo River as part of a team led by linguist Birgit Ricquier. In a number of villages, Smith found pottery shards of a style consistent with that found farther west, dating back to 2,300 to 2,000 years ago. “That would be the potential first Bantu settlers of the forest,” Smith says. Many experts interpret the trails of pottery as evidence that ceramics traditions were brought by Bantu-speaking people all the way from their original home. What’s unclear, however, is when and how Bantu-speaking peoples acquired their farming and metalworking skills—and, in particular, whether they invented iron smelting themselves or learned it from neighboring, iron-extracting cultures. Scientists have been able to deduce the path that Bantu speakers took to eastern and southern Africa. Some scholars have argued that the path split into two before entering the Congo rainforest, with one group heading east while another went south through the forest. But evidence is now mounting that these groups crossed the rainforest together, and only upon reaching its southern fringes roughly 2,500 to 3,500 years ago did they split into east-moving and southward-moving groups. Some of this evidence comes from linguistic studies. Not unlike the evolution of species, languages diverge and transform over time, creating “trees” that linguists can reconstruct from similarities in grammar and vocabulary. In 2015, a group of researchers did this computationally using data from about 400 Bantu languages. They observed a much larger diversity—and deeper splits—among the Bantu languages spoken in the Congo rainforest, suggesting that Bantu-speaking ancestors spent a long time there, allowing their languages to diversify. Languages spoken farther east and farther south appeared to stem from a less diverse subset of this larger group, explains coauthor Koen Bostoen, a linguist at Belgium’s Ghent University. Later linguistic studies, as well as genetic analyses, have reached similar conclusions. In 2023, Beleza and her colleagues compared the genomes of Bantu speakers from Angola—south of the Congo rainforest—with those from the eastern African country of Mozambique and ones from Cameroon, the Bantu homeland. Remarkably, the eastern African samples were more genetically similar to those from Angola, hundreds of miles to the south of Cameroon and south of the rainforest, than to any of the samples from Cameroon. That points to a recent common ancestor in the Angola region—suggesting that the Bantu split into eastern and southern streams only once they reached the rainforest’s southern edge, Beleza explains. By the time the Bantu people commenced their movement east and south, they had already come into their own as a farming, iron-making, livestock-herding, and pottery-making people, Chirikure says: Many ancient Bantu settlements across eastern and southern Africa contain evidence of this Bantu “package.” Key to the Bantu’s success in southern and eastern Africa, experts suspect, was their sedentary lifestyle. This—and their iron tools—would have allowed them to produce more food and grow in number, propelling them to occupy new territories. Their lifestyle affected the landscape as forests turned into farmland, and it also affected the hunter-gatherer communities who were already living there. Some groups were likely crowded out and disappeared, experts suspect, while others were probably pushed to marginal regions, including Indigenous groups such as rainforest hunter-gatherers and Khoisan peoples, who continue to live in central and southern Africa, respectively, to this day. Still, scientists are learning that Bantu peoples had deep relationships with these two groups. In the 2024 genetic analysis, Schlebusch and her colleagues found that some Bantu-speaking groups in central Africa share about an eighth of their DNA with rainforest hunter-gatherers, and some southern African Bantu speakers share a similar amount with Khoisan groups. Clearly, their ancestors once intermixed. Curiously, in the case of the Khoisan, this mixing tended to arise from pairings of Bantu-speaking men with Khoisan women. One 2021 study of southern African Bantu speakers found that the DNA inside their mitochondria—which people inherit only from their mothers—had greater-than-expected proportions of Khoisan DNA. But the Y chromosomes, which come from fathers, had much higher proportions of DNA with Bantu ancestry. Some experts speculate that this pattern could be the result of Bantu men raiding Khoisan women or preferentially marrying Khoisan women for whom the traditional bride prices would have been cheaper. “Whenever there is a power difference… between two groups, it tends to melt down to this,” says coauthor Ananyo Choudhury, a geneticist at the University of Witwatersrand in South Africa. This pattern also left marks on the language. The characteristic clicks of Khoisan languages also occur in southern African Bantu languages Zulu and Khosa. Bostoen suspects that the children of Khoisan-Bantu pairings were, at first, bilingual. Eventually, “they lost their mothers’ languages, but they left a substrate of the mothers’ languages in the Bantu language.” Archaeologists are also studying the relationships between Bantu farmers and their neighbors. While there may have been conflicts over land, the groups also cooperated with and learned from one another, says Décio Muianga, an archaeologist at Eduardo Mondlane University and the research institute Kaleidoscopio in Maputo, Mozambique. He’s been excavating a rock shelter in Mozambique containing thousands of years’ worth of stone tools, wild animal bones, and wild plant remains—telltale signs of hunter-gatherers living at the site. But interestingly, Bantu-style pottery also appears. “My assumption is that the hunter-gatherers were getting these ceramics from the farmers,” Muianga says. “They were interacting, communicating, and exchanging resources.” There is evidence, too, for cultural exchanges: European anthropologists have reported that some Bantu-speaking groups would solicit San hunter-gatherers to conduct traditional rainmaking ceremonies, for example. Bantu social networks eventually extended well beyond Africa. By 1100 to 1600 AD, many of the complex state societies that Bantu speakers built developed trade networks across the Indian Ocean. Archaeological data shows they imported Chinese porcelain and glass beads; in return, they exported gold, ivory, and iron. In fact, a 12th century Muslim cartographer once wrote that iron from parts of southern Africa—likely referring to Great Zimbabwe’s iron-producing hinterlands, Mtetwa suspects—was deemed to be of much higher quality than that from other places like India. Mtetwa has found evidence that iron smelters near Great Zimbabwe would add bone fragments to the furnaces, perhaps as part of a smelting-blessing ritual, but one with scientific merit: This material contains calcium, which can accelerate the separation of iron from the rest of the ore. That may be a testament to the Great Zimbabweans’ iron-smelting skills: “Some materials introduced within ritual or medicinal practices may simultaneously have had technically beneficial effects on the smelt,” says Mtetwa. Though they’ve made great progress in reconstructing the Bantu world, scientists are aware of, and trying to address, the limitations of their research so far. Linguistic studies, for instance, have been a popular tool to reconstruct Bantu history, but Bostoen cautions that very few Bantu languages have been documented thoroughly enough to definitively trace linguistic trees back in time. Linguists may never get a complete dataset, because some old branches of the Bantu language family may have died out. Another limitation of computational Bantu language-family reconstructions is that they’ve usually assumed that languages have evolved in isolation after different Bantu groups separated. But there may have been extensive cross-pollination among languages as different groups met and interacted. In fact, when linguists factored in the possibility of contact among Bantu languages in a 2026 study, they couldn’t reconstruct the diversification patterns described by previous linguists, says coauthor Igor Yanovich of the University of Vienna, who wrote about the challenges of Bantu linguistic studies in the 2025 Annual Review of Linguistics. In his view, linguists should first focus on thoroughly documenting Bantu languages before attempting to use them to understand Bantu history. In hopes of adding new insights, Schlebusch is working to obtain DNA from ancient human remains. By revealing the identity of people who were living in particular places at particular times, this could help to elucidate when and where different Bantu communities moved into, overlapped with, or repopulated areas. “What we see more and more in genetic data is that the movement was probably complex—that it was these ‘spread over spread’ events,” Schlebusch says. Archaeologists, for their part, hope to fill in the vast gaps in the archaeological record of sub-Saharan Africa—by both conducting new excavations and re-examining existing artifact collections for fresh insights. But all this work requires funding for research and laboratories, especially from African governments, says Chirikure; he, like many of his colleagues, has largely relied on funding from Europe, which is limited. Without more funding, “you are not generating fresh ideas, you are not formulating new hypotheses and testing them using some of the cutting-edge science,” he says. Scientists from all three disciplines are hopeful that they will eventually tell the complete story of how an innovative people spread and diversified across sub-Saharan Africa, mixing traditions with those of earlier peoples and of others who still live there, to create myriad cultures, each one a unique blend of food, philosophy, language, music, and traditions. “If you travel from West Africa today all the way to East Africa, you have a unique cultural diversity that is very hard to find in many other parts of the world,” Muianga says. An Estonian newspaper recently revealed that a British soldier disobeyed a direct order to remove the SIM card from his cellphone before being deployed to a part of Estonia, approximately 90 miles west of the Russian border. As a result of his refusal to engage in this element of operational security (opsec), the soldier, Daniel Barton-Kennedy, had his phone hacked, and nude photos of himself were stolen and used as blackmail against him. Eventually, Barton-Kennedy was court-martialed for disobeying a lawful command, pled guilty, was reduced in rank, and ordered to serve 10 months in detention. According to a 21-page British appeals court document shared with Ars Technica by Eesti Ekspress, the Estonian newspaper that first reported on this saga, the story begins in January 2026. At the time, Barton-Kennedy was a corporal, with over seven years of service, serving in the 1st Battalion of the Duke of Lancaster Regiment of the British Army. His unit was deployed to participate in “Exercise Eastern Warrior” in Tapa, Estonia, roughly halfway between Tallinn, the capital city, and the Estonia-Russia border. While in the United Kingdom, on January 7, Barton-Kennedy, along with other members of his unit, were briefed on their orders and given specific instructions to “remove SIM cards and switch off location settings and to use a trusted virtual private network on all personal electronic devices.” Once the unit arrived in Estonia, days later, they were given further briefings and reminders of this policy. Despite this, Barton-Kennedy ignored the instruction. On January 19, Barton-Kennedy informed his platoon commander that he had received a message “from an unknown party who had acquired nude photographs of him from the photo section of his mobile phone and had identified him on social media.” Barton-Kennedy initially acquiesced to paying the attacker 100 British pounds ($133) but balked when he was asked to pay 250 British pounds ($333), and only then informed his platoon commander what had happened. Eventually, Barton-Kennedy was court-martialed and pled guilty at a hearing on July 30. During that process, according to the court filing, Barton-Kennedy understood the orders that were given to him at the time, but asked the court to “consider the personal circumstances” that he faced at the time. Without his phone, he told the court, he could not reach his daughter on her birthday, nor his pregnant partner. In a written statement, Barton-Kennedy said he was stunned when he received the first blackmail message. “Despite still being in a state of shock and uncertainty, I recognised that I had a duty to report the compromise without any further delay,” he wrote. Additionally, he said that he “deeply regrets” the incident and added that serving in the British Army “has been one of the greatest privileges of my life.” Barton-Kennedy appealed the sentence, which was denied in August 2026. In response to an inquiry for comment, Lt. Col. EP Mackie, a British Army spokesperson, told Ars in a statement that the security measures imposed are meant to protect the safety of military personnel. “Any breaches of Army security measures that might compromise personal safeguarding or operational security will be dealt with swiftly, and offending personnel can expect uncompromising disciplinary action,” he wrote.

Doctors pull knife from man's eye that went through his brain—and he's fine now

A man in his 20s showed up to a UK hospital with the hilt of a knife sticking out of his face. The 15 mm-wide blade hidden within went completely through his right eye and through his brain, with the tip of the blade hitting the top of his skull. Despite the ghastly injury, the man was alive and conscious when he arrived. He could still talk and move all four limbs. His doctors described him as being in a state of agitation. On the Glasgow Coma Scale, he scored a 12 (E3 V4 M5), indicating a “moderate” brain injury, just one point above “mild.” He could open his left eye when told, but otherwise kept it closed (E3 score); he could talk and answer questions, but was confused (V4); and he intentionally moved away when pressure was applied to his body (M5). In a report published Friday in BMJ Case Report, his doctors said they were concerned that in his confused, agitated state he may try to pull the knife out on his own. So, they swiftly sedated and intubated him. They put him on prophylactic treatments for bacterial infections, including tetanus. Then they used images to see what was going on in his head. The blade was entirely through the right eye socket and eyeball. It traversed the left frontal and temporal lobes of his brain, passing through in front of—and without apparently injuring—the corpus callosum, which connects the two sides of the brain and is full of nerve fibers. The blade also missed the left internal capsule, which holds another bundle of nerve fibers. In terms of damage to major blood vessels, doctors found that the blade was close to the left internal carotid artery, a major blood vessel, but didn’t seem to have damaged it. Doctors did find, however, that a branch of the middle cerebral artery looked to be indented by the knife. After consulting with various medical specialties, the man’s doctors moved to emergency neurosurgery. They opted to remove part of his skull to directly see into the brain, but used a conservative “pull-and-see” method to slide the blade out rather than invasively excavate it. After it was out, the doctors saw no signs of significant hemorrhaging. The brain wound was cleaned and drained before surgeons patched up his skull. His right eye, however, couldn’t be saved. Given its extensive damage, it was removed. The man then moved to intensive care, where he recovered rapidly. A day after surgery, doctors were able to remove his breathing tube. He developed some minor right-side weakness, but it resolved two days later. Scans of his brain showed steady improvement—a little bleeding and swelling, and there was some air in his brain tissue from where the knife had been. But otherwise, it appeared to be healing. He developed no infections and had no seizures. Despite the “dramatic intracranial trajectory,” his language function was preserved, and he had no loss of movement or coordination, they reported. He was discharged from the hospital and at follow-up appointments remained “neurologically well,” with ongoing eye and mental health care. As for how the injury happened, his doctors still don’t know. “The mechanism is unclear,” they wrote. However, they noted he had a history of depression. The doctors reflected on how lucky the man was to have survived and to have such a full recovery. Such injuries that go through the eye and the brain are rare. When they do occur, they’re usually from “high-velocity mechanisms” such as gunshots or other ballistic injuries. Knives, rods, and nails fit into the rarer “low-velocity mechanisms. “Such “low-velocity penetrating brain injuries carry substantial morbidity and mortality because of hemorrhage, vascular injury, abscess, meningitis, cerebrospinal fluid leak, seizures and neurological deficit,” they wrote. But such fortunate recoveries aren’t unheard of; they cited reports of other remarkably unexpected recoveries from seemingly devastating brain injuries. That includes the famous case of Phineas Gage, a railroad foreman who survived an 1848 accident that sent a large iron rod directly through his head. The rod entered below his left cheekbone and exited the top of his skull. (Although he survived and recovered, Gage’s personality was said to have changed after the accident. The doctors in the knife-wound case did not mention any such personality changes.) “Echoing classic and contemporary examples of severe frontal lobe penetration with unexpectedly preserved neurological function, including Phineas Gage and a reported nail-gun injury, this case highlights that outcome may depend less on external severity than on precise trajectory relative to critical vascular, cortical and subcortical structures,” the doctors concluded.

Ships loiter near subsea cables amid Iran's warnings to Trump

Several tanker ships linked to Iran have been loitering over subsea Internet cables in the Gulf of Oman near the Strait of Hormuz—a possible indication that the Iranian government is preparing to attack such undersea infrastructure in response to any renewed US and Israeli strikes. The movements of three sanctioned tankers and an Iranian fishing boat have been tracked near major undersea cables by the maritime intelligence firm Windward in October, according to the Financial Times. Six active cables currently cross through the contested Strait of Hormuz, linking the Middle East to Europe, Africa and Asia, with two more cables having segments under construction in the region. Four countries, including Iraq, Kuwait, Bahrain and Qatar, depend entirely upon such subsea telecommunications connections running through the Strait of Hormuz chokepoint to link with the outside world. But other countries also depend heavily on the region’s undersea cable network—one third of India’s westbound Internet traffic goes through the Persian Gulf and Strait of Hormuz. One cable under construction, the 2Africa cable, is owned by a tech consortium that includes the US company Meta. That was one of the major cable corridors visited by the drifting Iran-linked ships. Windward used both ship-tracking data and satellite images to track the vessels as they slowed down and looped around near multiple cables, with some ships then “going dark” by switching off their automatic identification system (AIS) transponders that normally broadcast their location and other information. Such movement and behavioral patterns suggest the Iran-linked ships may be “mapping the cable routes, or placing charges or mines on them while dark,” Windward told the Financial Times. Windward highlighted the examples of two sanctioned tankers linked to Iran that spent days drifting “directly over regional cable corridors… both with no commercial reason to stop” in a blog post on October 26. Both ships carried no commercial cargo. Since the US and Israel first launched a major war with Iran in February 2026, Iran’s response beyond targeting US military bases, ships and aircraft has included attacking commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz and hitting oil refineries and other energy infrastructure along with airports in various Gulf countries. Iran has also made a point of threatening or attacking US Big Tech infrastructure in the Middle East, including launching multiple drone strikes on Amazon data centers. In May 2026, a spokesperson for Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps claimed Iran would “impose fees on Internet cables” belonging to US tech companies and other owners. But more recently, multiple Iranian officials and commanders of Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) have warned that they could directly attack the undersea cables running through the Strait of Hormuz. This came after President Donald Trump repeatedly threatened to renew major US military strikes on Iran and warned he could “annihilate” the country during a speech at the United Nations in New York on September 22. “Seven major cables run through the Strait of Hormuz, supporting global Internet operations and commercial data transmission,” one IRGC spokesperson said on October 20. “We remind the enemy of this.” The latest phase of the war has involved more limited US military strikes against Iranian targets, even as Trump has alternated between threats and touting the possibility of a diplomatic breakthrough. For years, mysterious electrical anomalies have been flummoxing grid managers around the world. Every 60 seconds, the frequency on the grid would drop a tiny bit. Even weirder: The sags happened at the exact same time on power grids across countries and continents. It was a mystery that Dirk Witthaut, a researcher at Forschungszentrum Jülich in Germany, couldn’t pass up. Germany’s largest transmission system operator, Tennet Germany, brought the puzzle to Witthaut, and it took years of investigative and modeling work to determine the cause. From Europe to Australia, the U.S. to Malaysia, the same pattern shows up in the data. Such frequency drops aren’t unheard of, but they are troubling. And the regularity with which they have been occurring can make the phenomenon feel even more ominous. The grid’s orderly operation relies on the frequency being kept within a certain range. If the oscillations get get too large, or if grid operators don’t know the cause and respond improperly, they can lead to blackouts. Even if the sags don’t cause blackouts, they can damage equipment in power plants if they go unchecked, especially in hydropower turbines. Hydropower plants have historically been a main driver of so-called ultra-low frequency oscillations (UFLOs). Every turbine vibrates, and if those vibrations occur with a certain regularity, known as the resonant frequency, they can magnify, sometimes to dangerous levels. Think of a wine glass breaking before an opera singer’s mighty pipes — that’s resonance. So Witthaut and his team started investigating hydropower, the most obvious potential culprit. They used a computer model of a hydropower plant control system on the Scandinavian grid to test the hypothesis. While they managed to find conditions that could generate a resonance that peaked once per minute, they couldn’t explain why it persisted for so long. Typically, resonances in mechanical systems will come and go, and so will the accompanying signal on the electrical grid. But this frequency drop kept happening minute after minute after minute. Every 60 seconds, without fail. It then dawned on the team that anything operating with such clockwork required, well, a clock. How else could they explain the uncanny timing? To that hypothesis, the researchers started close to home, installing more than 80 smart power quality meters around the Jülich campus. The research site is outfitted with medium- and low-voltage distribution lines, transformers, solar panels, and battery energy storage systems that serve as uninterruptible power supplies (UPS). It also has a high-performance computer system as well as the usual office computers powered by much smaller UPS devices. The power quality meters sampled a range of attributes, including frequency, voltage, current, and power, once every three seconds. Before long, the research team had its answer. The measurements recorded on the transformer attached to the battery energy storage system peaked every minute, just like on the bigger power grids. It turns out that many stationary batteries, from data center UPSes to large grid-scale batteries, check their state-of-charge every minute. Since these devices are internet-connected, their clocks are in near perfect synchronicity. Every time one checks its charge, the disturbance is imperceptible on the wide grid. But when they all do it together, that disruption becomes apparent. To find evidence that UPSes were the main culprit on the grid, Witthaut and his team turned to data provided by grid operators. They found their answer in Ireland. There, data centers have grown to consume a large fraction of the country’s electricity. By 2022, data centers represented 18% of the load on Ireland’s grid, and each facility is served by a fleet of UPSes. Sure enough, between 2016 and 2021, the minute-by-minute frequency drop on the Irish grid quadrupled from 3 milli-Hertz (mHz) in 2016 to 12 mHz in 2021. More recently, some drops have exceeded 20 mHz. Ireland’s 50 Hz grid is built to withstand swings of plus or minus 200 mHz, though the grid operator would rather limit the swings to 100 mHz. In that range, 20 mHz isn’t a lot, but data centers aren’t the only loads on the grid. If other loads also cause the frequency to sag, the extra bump from every UPS checking its state-of-charge could push the grid toward blackout conditions. The problem is entirely preventable. Rather than check state-of-charge exactly on the minute, Witthaus and team recommend that UPS and grid-scale battery manufacturers add some randomization to the schedule. While UPSes are driving the problem today, there’s a risk that the phenomenon could persist. The researchers also tested IoT devices. In devices as diverse as a smart oven and Raspberry Pi clusters, they were able to detect periodic power fluctuations. The fix is also a simple software patch, but many IoT devices go unpatched. A recent study found that less than 3% of IoT devices are running the latest firmware. Regulators may soon have to step in to demand changes, but they can only address their corner of the grid. Given the global nature of the issue, it’ll ultimately require global coordination. The mystery may be solved, but the problem could remain for years to come. As anyone who has been involved in a national-security crisis can tell you, there is often too much information, some of it contradictory, and not a lot of time or clear methods to sort through it all. AI can process volumes of intelligence inputs more than any human team, detect patterns and anomalies buried in the data, and make sense of complex information in remarkably little time. But what will it do to the president’s judgment? Based on my experience studying crisis decision-making and assessing intelligence, I believe there is a real risk that AI will amplify leaders’ own biases, handing them back their own assumptions with the authority of objective analysis and the ease of a clear-cut outcome. We tend to talk about AI as if it were wholly independent, a more rational intelligence system that can compensate for our weaknesses. But in reality, humans have built these AI models and trained them on data produced by humans. We then ask these AI models questions shaped by our own beliefs. The outcome is far from objective. The flaws in human decision-making are well-known. Presidents and their advisers have always had to guard against confirmation bias, our tendency to interpret evidence in light of our pre-existing beliefs and to under-weigh or dismiss facts that point to the contrary. We know humans have selective attention, an instinct to hone in on facts that are more vivid and to overlook others. Presidents are not immune from these tendencies. In my view, there is an even more insidious danger lurking within our AI use. Presidential decisions represent countless underlying judgments. Reasonable people can differ on each of the estimates, and those differences are part of what presidential judgment must resolve. Flattening them up into a probability assessment obscures more than it reveals because it does not show which indicators were seen as credible and which were ignored. Consider the night of May 1, 2011. President Barack Obama had gathered his advisors to assess intelligence that indicated Osama Bin Laden was hiding in a compound in Abbottabad, Pakistan. If the intelligence was correct, he would send in American troops. But his trusted, experienced advisors, all looking at substantially the same data, offered individual estimates ranging from roughly 30% to 95%. Now imagine a powerful AI system in that room and synthesized those assessments to give a 63.7% probability that bin Laden was there. We would certainly gain precision. But what would be lost? The number would not tell the president why there was such a marked difference in the assessments. It would not inform him of the competing assumptions on which those assessments were built, or how the implications of action versus inaction were weighed. But worst of all, it would not alert the president to the fact that there was disagreement at all. Such a “clean” number can make a question look resolved. Now factor in that the CIA, DIA, State Department, military services, and other agencies will likely rely on the same foundation models built from overlapping data and sources. You could theoretically end up with 10 assessments pointing in the same direction but all derived from the same fundamental materials. The AI version of that risk is synthetic consensus: institutional agreement that looks independent but is partly generated by common analytical machinery and reproduced at scale. But if the problems are well-known from human decision-making, so too are the strategies to counteract them. We can design our systems to act as benevolent antagonists and train our decision-makers to engage with them as such. On the technology side, we need to build a system that talks back. AI used in the national security context should not be optimized just for speed of response. It should be built to help identify which evidence is credible; to generate the strongest competing explanations; to distinguish facts from inference and prediction; and to make uncertainty visible rather than manufacture precision. A system designed to be agreeable is dangerous. The points of friction and disagreement are often what’s most revealing and valuable for leaders making tough decisions. On the human side, we need to ask better questions. That means noting priors before engaging AI, as well as taking a hard look at what one believes and at what confidence level. It means taking a second and third look at the framing and phrasing of the questions. Rather than seeking confirmation, we should explicitly ask for evidence that disproves our hypotheses and for ways of distinguishing between competing explanations. We should even be questioning whether we are focused on the right question. And structurally, the director of national intelligence should require AI-assisted intelligence products to disclose whether they all came from the same or different data points. When 10 agencies agree, the president should know whether that’s 10 judgments or one. Policymakers should also ask different AI models to assess the same evidence and examine where and why their judgments diverge. In my view, it would be too simplistic to conclude from the reported near-miss with the Chinese vessel that AI has no place in the Situation Room. We must design and engage with AI in a way that makes leaders more aware of the weaknesses in their own thinking rather than reinforcing them. During the fog of crisis, the choices are never obvious, and only one person ultimately carries the weight of decision. The tools we build should never let a president forget it. About two-thirds of humanity now lives in countries where fertility is too low for each generation to replace itself. By 2035, for instance, Italy’s old-age dependency ratio will have risen from an already high 39% to 50%. Greece, Portugal, and Spain are close behind. A similar demographic shift is underway in Latin America, including in Cuba, Puerto Rico, Costa Rica, and Colombia. Just a few decades ago, these countries had much smaller elderly populations compared to Europe and thus had lower old-age dependency ratios. Now they are catching up with Europe on this measure. In some parts of Asia, demographic concerns are more heightened as populations are expected to age even faster than in Europe and Latin America, especially in China, South Korea, Taiwan, and Thailand. By 2035, the old-age dependency ratio is expected to increase from 21% to almost 34% in China. In Hong Kong, it is expected to exceed 55%. North America’s population will continue to grow and will age more moderately, mostly because of immigration. In the United States, the old-age dependency ratio is forecast to increase from around 28.8% today to about 34% by 2035. Immigration policy will shape the exact extent of population change and aging in the country. The implications of this global trend are far-reaching, and for many, simply alarming. As the International Monetary Fund (IMF) puts it starkly: “For those countries where fewer workers are available and labor force participation rates drop, economic output is bound to fall.” Other forecasts warning about pressures on living standards, fiscal problems, and broader social challenges are equally gloomy. And aging will affect politics and social dynamics in various, often unforeseen ways, opening up new fissures in society. Twenty percent of Americans regularly get news from TikTok, and that number jumps to 39 percent for people 18 to 29 years old, Pew found. Viewers favor the stripped-down selfie-style video dispatches from personalities they trust, even if those creators are not themselves reporting and writing the news. Compared with a decade ago, the barrier to entry and cost of making video content has plummeted: Anyone with a smartphone can become a video creator, no fancy equipment or editing needed. Social video is fast, personality-driven, casual, and, at times, unfiltered — the exact opposite from what news outlets are used to. A second pivot-to-video is underway, and the media industry once again wants in. Video is unavoidable on social media, if not an expected part of how people and institutions show up online. TikTok’s influence is felt everywhere: on competitor platforms like Meta’s Reels and YouTube Shorts, of course, but also in unexpected places, like LinkedIn. There are no signs of it slowing down, either: The time spent watching Reels on Facebook was at an all-time high in Q3. Early tests in other countries suggest Meta is toying with the idea of dropping users directly into full-screen video upon opening the Instagram and Facebook apps. The appetite for video seems insatiable, and if news organizations won’t lean in, armies of content creators will be happy to scoop up the audiences. Some news outlets were relatively early to the short-form social video boom. The Washington Post, for example, found notable success on TikTok before many peers had even created dedicated accounts on the platform. (Dave Jorgenson, the face of the Post’s social video effort, left the paper last year. He now makes short and longform videos under Local News International — perhaps a testament to the trust that viewers place in individual creators.) Beyond having a short-form video presence, some traditional news outlets are leaning into formats and styles popularized in the vertical video era. CNN partnered with Jubilee, the popular channel that produces debate-me-style videos on hot-button political topics. The CNN town hall with Texas Senate candidate James Talarico was conducted in the Jubilee style. The New York Times, which for years has produced polished documentary-style videos, recently introduced the vertical video series “The Call.” Unlike the Times’ more buttoned-up videos, the point of “The Call” is to create a sense of intimacy, like you’re getting updates from a friend: The show’s host ostensibly makes a video call to a journalist reporting on the ground to ask them about what they’re witnessing. News outlets are also directly hiring or working with content creators. Some outlets like The Washington Post have spun up so-called creator networks, where they partner with influencers to produce content independent from the newsroom. A new program supported by Poynter and Microsoft pairs content creators with a handful of news outlets so they can create content tailored for social media. And then, of course, there are the clips. Podcasts became videos, which became bite-sized clips of podcasts formatted for vertical feeds. TV shows became web shows, which gave rise to microdramas. News articles — even major investigations — are now adapted for vertical video audiences. Individual reporters (myself included) make talking-head clips explaining our reporting. Earlier this year, I called this phenomenon “the clippening”: the reformatting of all media to be legible in a vertical video feed, even if the original work is neither vertical nor video. It’s hard to fault the people and institutions putting resources into social video — audiences are consuming it — but it’s also feeling a lot like the past. One of the misguided beliefs of pivot-to-video 1.0 was that traction, views, or engagement on social media platforms would be enough to offset (and ideally surpass) the cost of making the content. For many, that ended up not being true, with flawed metrics, of course, being part of the reason: Virality feels nice, but monetizing that attention isn’t always straightforward. These days, social media companies have largely coalesced around the “views” measurement meaning the number of times a post was served up on somebody’s feed, whether they watched the whole thing or swiped away immediately. How many people who watch a clip of a podcast go on to listen to the full episode? Are the people watching my TikToks about my stories actually going to The Verge to read it, or buying a subscription to support my work? You can’t eat views. The hope is that we will find new audiences where they’re already spending time. There are millions of people discussing news that journalists are uncovering — it’s important that we reach them, build trust, and be part of the conversation. What’s certain is that if reporters and news outlets don’t, a whole slew of creators will be there to take their place. Just as we’re getting used to short form, the ground is once again shifting under our feet. Not content simply being social media, platforms are looking to be a destination for longer form, higher production quality entertainment. Tech companies know that people watch videos on their phones, but they would also really like users to watch on their televisions, too. This spring, YouTube’s pitch to content creators (and advertisers) was to make exclusive shows for the platform. YouTube is dominating the TV streaming wars, but other platforms like Instagram are pushing in that direction, too: Its recently announced “Features” effort allows for 90-minute horizontal video uploads, and it seems to be specifically angling for television viewing. (Unsurprisingly, there are a lot of questions from creators about what they get in return for posting longer content.) Some news outlets are already thinking ahead and eyeing longform. The BBC struck a deal with YouTube in early 2026 to create special programming specifically for the platform, then followed it up by announcing a 24/7 YouTube livestream in an effort to reach young people. Short form isn’t going anywhere, at least right now. But these efforts to move into longer, more resource-intensive video content could be a harbinger of what’s to come — and what could soon be asked of news publishers and influencers alike. News media have had a complicated relationship with tech platforms. On one hand, it’s where audiences are, and platforms allow publishers to reach those people. On the other hand, tech companies signal to outlets (and creators) what kind of content they should prioritize, and everyone has to scramble to meet the new demand. Just as soon as journalists start getting comfortable with front-facing selfie videos, we may be facing a whole new beast: videos that are horizontal and longer, with a higher production value and higher barrier to entry that we typically associate with TV and streaming.
