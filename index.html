Website admins, brace yourselves. Fixes for what are believed to be uncommonly severe vulnerabilities are scheduled to drop in a little more than 12 hours for PHP, a programming language that powers websites and Web applications everywhere. PHP maintainers said Monday that they planned to release patches for two vulnerabilities, both with severity ratings of 9.2 out of a possible 10. “All PHP users are encouraged to prepare now so they can upgrade as soon as the release is available,” the advisory stated. “We reserve the right to release earlier should details of either vulnerability become public, or should evidence of active exploitation appear in the wild.” The maintainers published the advisory following the leaking of purported details, which have yet to be confirmed. The report showed a video of an exploit tool dubbed PHP2Shell executing code on webservers running: All of the above use PHP. A video depicting the tool has been reposted here. Attempts to locate the unconfirmed report were unsuccessful, but security firm watchTowr said it claimed to affect every PHP version from PHP 5, released in 2004, onward. The security firm went on to say it has confirmed that the vulnerabilities, tracked as CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, are real. Researchers from another security firm, Sansec, said they “expect mass attacks to start within 15 minutes of the release.” A third security firm, Webfence, also urged admins to prepare now. Huntress has also issued an advisory. Preparation steps include identifying who is responsible for managing PHP inside an organization, taking an inventory of every app built on the language, planning a maintenance window starting at 14:00 UTC with contingency plans for moving earlier, making a backup of site and database files, and monitoring the PHP.net homepage for any late-breaking announcements, including an earlier release time. Some experts suggest unplugging any nonessential systems until they’re patched. As an open source scripting language for building dynamic websites and web apps, PHP has come to be a de facto building block for the Web. It runs multiple CMS apps in addition to WordPress and also powers many internal tools. One security researcher said an estimated 1.3 million Internet-connected hosts are likely affected. Vulnerabilities with the severity the advisories are describing have the potential to inflict major damage across the Internet. Admins should remain aware that the patches can be released at any moment and remain vigilant until they are. Elon Musk said Tuesday that he would like to see the consolidation of his business empire by formally merging his two largest companies, SpaceX and Tesla. “Do I think it would be a good idea? Yes,” Musk said of a potential merger. He was speaking during a question-and-answer period as part of SpaceX’s third-quarter earnings call. Musk said, ultimately, it not his decision. “But at the end of the day the shareholders have to be supportive of this.” Musk’s talk of a merger comes after a busy year in which SpaceX already acquired Musk’s artificial intelligence firm, xAI, in February. In June, SpaceX went public with the largest initial public offering on record. At the request of shareholders of each company, Musk said both SpaceX and Tesla have formed special committees of “independent directors” to discuss the idea of a merger. “I’m a conflicted party; I can’t actually be in the discussions of the special committees,” Musk said. Those committees have not reached an agreement on the terms of a merger transaction, Musk said. “Obviously there’s no guarantee that they will, but I do think there’s a lot of merit to combining the companies as we are pretty much in the superintelligence singularity,” Musk said. “There’s lots to be gained from cooperating on developing superintelligence software, chip design, the Terafab, obviously robotics, mass to orbit with the Starmind constellation, and connectivity. So I think it would be a good idea.” It might seem like madness to merge a space business with an automotive business, but both SpaceX and Tesla are rapidly transitioning from their original markets into artificial intelligence. In its public financial documents, SpaceX has indicated that the bulk of its future revenues are likely to come from orbital data centers that will provide the backbone of computing for artificial intelligence on Earth. Tesla sees its future in sophisticated self-driving cars, robots, and industrial hardware. Both enterprises would require advanced computer chips and energy. SpaceX could provide the connectivity for Tesla hardware. Beyond these synergies, the merger would be attractive to Musk—already the world’s richest person and first trillionaire—because it would give him more control. Based on SpaceX documents filed as part of its IPO, Musk holds more than 82 percent of voting power in SpaceX. By contrast, Musk holds about 20 percent of voting power at Tesla. By merging SpaceX with Tesla, where he has less direct control, Musk could acquire a majority of shares eligible for voting at the combined company. This would depend on the nature of valuations and negotiated agreements at the time of the merger. At the time of Musk’s announcement, SpaceX and Tesla had market valuations of about $2 trillion and $1.5 trillion, respectively. The merger will face scrutiny by US regulators. Additionally, Morningstar reported earlier this year that valuation disagreements will likely be significant, especially among Tesla shareholders. Musk said Tuesday that 50 percent of all Tesla shareholders, not just those with voting shares, would have to approve of the merger. Such a vote would only come if the two special committees reached an agreement on merger terms. Some political leaders, already wary of Musk’s outsized power and influence and willingness to use both freely, may also object to a single Musk mega-corporation. It’s not definitive what the merger’s implications are for SpaceX’s traditional space business and its longtime goal of settling Mars, or for Tesla’s one-time aspirations to bring electric vehicles to the global market. Most probably, however, a combined Musk-universe would focus on artificial intelligence and the computing power needed to back those ambitions. Less Mars and cars—and more silicon.

Meta drops the word "train" from its commercial TOS after AI backlash

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-20

Visit Original Website
Hoping to quell criticism, Meta has dropped the word “train” from its “streamlined” commercial terms of service that kick in on October 30. Last week, Meta faced backlash after legal experts warned that the tech giant’s sweeping new commercial terms of service seemed to give Meta permission to endlessly scrape websites linked on business pages. Talking to Nieman Lab, experts suggested that Meta could possibly use the policy to avoid millions in licensing fees from news organizations by requiring all business users to share a wealth of AI training data simply for running a business page. A Meta spokesperson confirmed that Meta did not alert users to the changed language following backlash. This potentially only adds to confusion ahead of the policy rollout. In response to backlash, Meta’s spokesperson provided a statement to Ars, acknowledging Nieman Lab’s initial report but not its follow-up report reiterating experts’ concerns: “We’ve heard you and want to clarify: the crawling terms we consolidated only applied to our ads and business products, and we’ve clarified that in the terms themselves. It does not change how Meta uses web crawlers. As always, content owners can use robots.txt to signal their preferences. This is not about scraping news sites, and these updates don’t give us new rights that supersede those preferences.” The same experts who sounded the initial alarms were not impressed by Meta’s updates, which they told Nieman Lab do not go far enough to protect business page users from invasive scraping. Matt Topic, a media and intellectual property attorney at Loevy & Loevy, told Nieman Lab that “publishers should remain concerned.” “The revised version of the new terms still allows Meta to use that scraped content to develop any ‘business product,’” Topic said. “That doesn’t seem to be a defined term, and presumably Meta is developing AI business products. I think ‘develop’ is broad enough to include ‘train.’” Initially, Meta’s terms authorized Meta to use “any content” from websites posted on business pages “to provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain the integrity” of any Meta products, services, or features. The updated terms delete the word “train” and no longer refer to “any content.” Instead, they allow Meta to “periodically pull updates” from business users’ websites in order to “provide, develop, improve, research, and maintain advertising and business products,” as well as to help Meta “maintain the integrity and security” of its products. This scraping, Meta says, is for users’ benefit, feeding into Meta research into products to “help you create and deliver ads and other commercial content, provide users information about your products and services, and connect you with users who may be interested in your products and services,” the policy says. Following tweaks, it’s good that Meta added “more language about the permitted uses,” James Grimmelmann, a professor of digital and information law at Cornell Law School, told Nieman Lab. But “[the] ‘develop…advertising and business products’ [language] is still broad enough to drive a truck through,” he said. Ultimately, Grimmelmann agreed with Topic that “this does nothing to restrict the ‘associated with your products or services’ [language] that would let Meta scrape entire sites.” What would help—as University of Wisconsin law professor Benjamin Sobel told Nieman Lab—would be if Meta added specific language to the policy where the company agrees to respect scraping instructions in robots.txt files. Since complying with robots.txt is voluntary, Meta could possibly rely on the legally enforceable TOS omitting mentions of robots.txt to scrape sites that attempt to block scraping via robots.txt, experts suggested. When it comes to robots.txt, “you can still express the preference, and unless it’s a legally enforceable preference, it can still be ignored all the same,” Sobel said. Criticism goes beyond Nieman Lab’s reporting, as news groups globally urge regulators to intervene and block Meta’s controversial terms from kicking in. A chief executive for the United Kingdom News Media Association, Theo Bamber, accused Meta of a “shameless land grab” relying on the “worst kind of arrogant tech platform bullying.” Bamber urged UK regulators to “stand up for British creators and the business community” by “preventing Meta from setting this dreadful precedent.” Meanwhile, the European Publishers Council has already asked the European Commission to intervene. “Publishers must be able to decide whether and how their journalism is used, and negotiate fair payment,” the group’s executive director, Angela Mills Wad, said. “Burying such far-reaching claims in platform terms is no substitute for consent.” Similarly, Danielle Coffey, the president and CEO of the News/Media Alliance—which Nieman Lab noted is the largest news publishing trade association in the US—protested the terms as “outrageous and probably illegal.” In the US, Coffey urged regulators to recognize that Meta’s “decision to withhold access to its audience unless businesses allow Meta to scrape content from their websites is an appalling, bad-faith policy, and a clear abuse of the company’s market power.” “It cannot be allowed to be implemented on October 30,” Coffey said.

Starting on Sept. 25, a hacker set loose an army of AI agents that attempted to break into over 1,200 websites over the following few weeks—autonomously, with no additional work required of the human hacker after sending them off on their task. The agents successfully breached about 100 companies, including farm equipment maker Deere, home appliance ​company SharkNinja, and engine manufacturer Cummins, according to Reuters, which first reported the incident, discovered by cybersecurity researchers.While the hacker didn’t need to execute the attack themselves, they had to foot the bill for the AI tools. It cost about $5,000 worth of tokens from a combination of DeepSeek and Anthropic’s Claude Code models, researchers told Reuters. That’s roughly $50 per successful breach. Autonomous attacks powered by AI agents are becoming more common, Jessica Ji, a senior research analyst at Georgetown’s Center for Security and Emerging Technology (CSET), told Fortune. This is primarily happening because the tools have “gotten meaningfully better” over the past year or so, she said. Specifically, AI companies have improved their models’ coding skills, which has led to a “dramatic increase in their ability to do cybersecurity-relevant tasks like analyze large volumes of code,” she said. Also, they have become more agentic, meaning they can use other software to complete tasks, as opposed to merely outputting text. “These AI systems are now both more capable of hacking and are able to act more autonomously,” Ji said. Agents powered by large language models (LLMs) can run all day and night without stopping, at a “fraction of the cost of human attackers,” Varun Chandrasekaran, assistant professor of electrical and computer engineering at the University of Illinois’s Grainger College of Engineering, told Fortune. This “fundamentally changes the economics of exploitation,” he said, citing the attackers who spent only $50 per successful reach. Open-weight models, or those that are publicly downloadable and customizable, can also offer powerful, low-cost tools for agentic hacks, Ji said. Attackers are “not restricted to the most capable ‘closed’ models and can use similarly capable open models that are potentially more difficult to set up, but less expensive,” she said. The wide availability of low-cost, powerful open models is one reason Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has warned that open models in particular pose a risk of widespread and potentially devastating cyberattacks. It is one reason that Amodei and others have said that there may need to be government restrictions placed on the distribution and use of open models.However, others disagree and say open models benefit defenders more than attackers. For example, when AI company Hugging Face discovered it was being autonomously attacked by an agent in July, it first tried to use a closed model to defend itself, but the model refused the request owing to its security guardrails. Hugging Face then switched to an open model, GLM 5.2, made by China-based Z.ai, which it used to help fend off the attack. The same thing happened during another agentic attack this week that targeted OpenCode, an AI-powered coding platform. A hacker unleashed an autonomous AI model on OpenCode that was “nonstop trying to attack our systems for the past 48 hours,” OpenCode cofounder Dax Raad posted on X on Oct. 18. The attackers broke into certain OpenCode user accounts, affecting 1.4% of its users, according to the company. It spent their money, with some people losing as much as $1,700, Dealroom reported. The company fought back with its own AI agents, which it kept running “24/7,” according to Raad. OpenCode had been running its defense with an OpenAI model, which started to refuse its requests in the middle of the attack. This was “a bit frustrating,” Raad said. But the company was able to continue after asking OpenAI to admit it to the company’s Daybreak Blue, an application-only program that allows verified cybersecurity professionals to use its most advanced tools. After some mild online backlash to OpenAI’s models initially refusing OpenCode’s requests, OpenAI executive Dean Ball said this was required to prevent bad actors from using its models for “sustained autonomous cyberattacks.” In what appears to be a first, OpenAI refunded OpenCode users who lost money in the attack, OpenAI’s head of core products, Thibault Sottiaux, said on X. In a third agentic attack this month—the most severe one—bad actors hacked into several South Korean banks. The attackers were likely based in China, according to Reuters, and used a combination of a Chinese state–developed AI tool called Artex, built on open models, as well as Anthropic’s Claude Code. They stole data from an estimated 68,000 people, the Wall Street Journal reported. South Korean officials urged banks to harden their defenses. “Cybersecurity attacks powered by AI agents have been increasing in South Korea, and I expect they will grow in number worldwide in the future,” Mun Chong-hyun, who heads Genians Security Center, a Seoul cybersecurity analytics firm, told the Journal. © 2026 Fortune Media IP Limited. Anthropic has named Christopher Stubbs, a Harvard University astrophysicist, to its Long-Term Benefit Trust. The trust has the power to elect a majority of the AI company’s board, an unusual corporate governance arrangement that is likely to come under increased pressure if the company goes public, as it is widely expected to do as soon as next month. Stubbs’s appointment fills one of two open seats on the Trust. He is best known for his work showing that the universe’s expansion is accelerating, a counterintuitive finding that scientists speculate is the result of a previously undocumented force known as “dark energy.” Stubbs later headed the ESSENCE supernova cosmology project which gathered further evidence on the nature of dark energy. In 2015, Stubbs and other members of the High-Z Supernova Search Team, which had made the original dark energy findings, were awarded the Breakthrough Prize in Fundamental Physics.Stubbs was also the first project scientist for what is now the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile, which began a decade-long survey of the southern sky this past summer. In addition to being a professor, he was Harvard University’s Dean of Science from 2018 to 2024. And he serves as a member of JASON, the independent group of scientists that advises the U.S. government on defense and technology. Daniela Amodei, Anthropic’s cofounder and president, said Stubbs had a track record of following data and evidence “even when it went against the prevailing wisdom of the field.” She said that “as AI starts to transform how science itself is done, [Stubbs’] intellectual honesty, rigor, and commitment to the truth are the values we want shaping the future of this technology, and I’m grateful he’s bringing his deep expertise to the Trust.” Neil Buddy Shah, the CEO of the Clinton Health Access Initiative and chair of Anthropic’s Long-Term Benefit Trust, cited his experience leading “large, complex, collaborative scientific endeavors across academia, government, and the national laboratories.”Anthropic said Stubbs has recently written about the challenge of ensuring AI-assisted research remains reproducible when AI models can give different answers to the same question. “I use AI tools in my research every day and see what they make possible, and as an educator I have confronted some of the hard questions they raise,” Stubbs said in a statement. “As a grandfather, I want to help ensure that AI is developed and deployed for the long-term benefit of humanity.”Stubbs is the first physicist to be appointed to the Trust. He joins Shah; Richard Fontaine, CEO of the Center for a New American Security; and former Federal Reserve Chair Ben Bernanke, who joined in July. The remaining vacancy on the Trust opened in August, when former California Supreme Court Justice Mariano-Florentino Cuéllar left the Trust to become Anthropic’s first chief global affairs officer. Anthropic is a Public Benefit Corporation, which means it is legally required to weigh its stated mission—which it specifies as the “responsible development and maintenance of advanced AI for the long-term benefit of humanity”—alongside shareholder returns. The Trust holds a special class of stock with no economic value. That stock gives the Trust the right to elect a growing share of Anthropic’s seven-person board, and eventually a majority. Trustees are independent of management and investors. They advise leadership on major decisions, especially those involving AI’s risks and impact on society. The Trust’s power is getting new scrutiny as the IPO approaches. Anthropic confidentially filed a draft registration statement with the SEC in June. Reports suggest the IPO could value the company at more than $2 trillion. Anthropic was valued at $965 billion in a funding round in May. The company has also reportedly planned super-voting shares for its founders. Critics say this structure, together with the Trust, would leave public shareholders little say over the company’s strategic direction. Last weekend, Anthropic said it would put 5% of its shares into a new Public Benefit Fund. This is separate from the Trust. The company said the new fund would make donations to philanthropic organizations as well as possibly support people directly who have been negatively impacted by AI, for instance workers who had lost their jobs to AI-powered automation. Native to the tropical waters of the Indo-Pacific, lionfish have infiltrated the western Atlantic, Caribbean, and Gulf of Mexico, where they have become a destructive invasive species ever since the first was recorded near Miami in 1985, likely after being released from the home aquarium trade. With few predators in these new habitats, a single fish can produce more than 2 million eggs a year. Studies have found that just one lionfish can reduce the replenishment of native fish populations on some reefs by nearly 80%. In 2016, Bowman sold the first lionfish to Whole Foods in South Florida. “That was the ground-breaker,” she says, “because if Whole Foods is promoting something, then people know it’s good. We went from $2 per lb. to $10 per lb. pretty much overnight.” Now, 10 years later, through Keys Fisheries, her fish is sold in stores, and lionfish appears on restaurant menus throughout the area—and in doing so, millions of native reef fish have been saved from predation. Bowman’s work is part of a growing trend that’s not just about introducing new dishes to the local food scene; creating a market for invasive species may also bring ecological benefits for the local environment. Invasive species are considered one of the five most critical direct drivers of biodiversity loss, according to the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES), whose most recent report in 2023 notes that the global economic toll of dealing with these species cost more than $423 billion in 2019. Once established, invasive species are often impossible to eradicate because they reproduce rapidly, spread across vast, interconnected environments, and often go unchecked until they are established. And the challenge of managing invasive species is particularly acute in the ocean, says Helen Roy, a professor at the U.K. Centre for Ecology & Hydrology and the University of Exeter and a co-chair of the IPBES assessment. It’s a consequence of the openness and connectedness of marine systems, she says. A pot of green crab. Courtesy Lisa Nichols In 2004, conservation biologist Joe Roman coined the term “invasivorism” to describe the idea of eating invasive species to help control their populations. Since then, a growing movement across North America is attempting to transform ecological menaces into culinary commodities, from Florida to Hawai’i, British Columbia, Maine, and the Mississippi River basin. That movement is still expanding: in 2025, for example, 70 restaurants and fish markets across the U.S. participated in Green Crab Week, a national campaign encouraging diners to eat the invasive European green crab. The goal, says Isabelle Côté, a marine ecologist at Simon Fraser University, is often “functional eradication”: reducing an invasive population to a level where its ecological impacts are considered acceptable. That’s where our dinner tables can help, by turning the removal of the species into an economic opportunity. David Standridge’s philosophy is all about harvesting what’s plentiful and turning it into something delicious. Since 2022, the James Beard Award-winning executive chef of The Shipwright’s Daughter in Mystic, Conn., has processed more than 10,000 lbs of green crab, making it a menu mainstay. Commonly considered one of the world’s worst invasive species, the green crab has successfully invaded every continent except Antarctica. The species, native to Western Europe and Africa, has few predators, aggressively hunts its prey, and can consume a wide variety of shellfish, including oysters, clams, mussels, lobsters, and other crabs. One crab can eat more than 40 juvenile soft-shell clams in a day, while also destroying seagrass habitats. And a single green crab can produce up to 185,000 eggs per year, while its larval life stage allows young crabs to drift considerable distances in ocean currents, spreading from one invaded area to another. Rather than a nuisance, Standridge describes the green crab as tremendously flavorful, producing a beautiful stock that can be used across dishes. “We use them not to eliminate green crab, but to take some pressure off the ecosystem,” he says. Getting green crab onto the menu, however, wasn’t so straightforward. When Standridge first tried to source the species in 2020, he discovered that Connecticut, like many states, classified green crab as bait and technically couldn’t be sold for human consumption. He finally found a supplier to help in 2021, and then in 2025, a change to a single line of state code allowed green crab and other species on the bait list to be harvested and sold as food. Over five years, 36 crews removed nearly 2 million crabs, including more than 1.3 million from the Honna and Tarundl estuaries alone. The scale of the invasion has made control difficult, and harvester and marine economic development coordinator Eli Gladstone hopes a viable market for the crabs could help reduce their numbers. But he cautions that commercial demand alone won’t be enough: trapping will also need to become more efficient, and some continued public investment may be necessary. Communities across the U.S. have been grappling with the ecological and economic impacts of introduced species for decades. In the 1950s, bluestripe snapper, also known in Hawai’i by its Tahitian name, ta’ape, was introduced to O’ahu, when roughly 3,000 fish were released to bolster fishing opportunities and establish a new food source. But ta’ape, native to the South Pacific, never became a sustained part of Hawai’i’s seafood market. By the 1980s, the overabundant fish was largely considered a “trash fish,” prompting the state’s Department of Aquatic Resources to launch a market-development project in 1985 that used consumer education and supermarket promotions to try to build demand. The effort failed to gain lasting traction: supply was inconsistent, and demand tapered off after the project ended. Freediver and spearfisher Kimi Werner has seen firsthand just how dramatically the ta’ape has come to dominate the reefs. Where schools of snappers in their native ecosystems might number just a handful, she says Hawai’i’s ta’ape, with its electric-blue stripes and lemon-yellow skin, can blanket the seafloor. “You can see the power of numbers taking over these habitats where native fish belong,” she says. Since 2019, Conservation International Hawai’i, in partnership with local businesses and organizations including the nonprofit Chef Hui, has been trying to turn that abundance into an opportunity: promoting ta’ape as a food source to reduce its numbers while supporting local fishers and food security. The approach is starting to resonate across Hawai’i’s tourism industry, says Jhana Young, sustainable seafood senior manager with Conservation International Hawai’i. Courtesy Justin Turkowski But creating a market for an invasive species also raises the question of whether it can genuinely serve conservation goals—or simply become another fishery. Young argues that it can redirect existing fishing pressure toward a species whose removal benefits the ecosystem. “Fishers are always going to fish. By helping create markets for a species that didn’t exist before, it helps point fishers in the direction of a choice that is ultimately better for nature’s long-term balance.” More than 4,000 miles away, in the lower Mississippi River basin, Asian carp populations have proliferated since the species was imported to the U.S. from China in the 1970s. They were brought in to control nuisance algae in wastewater-treatment plants and aquaculture ponds, as well as for human consumption. Within a decade, they had escaped confinement and spread into the Mississippi River basin and its major tributaries, including the Illinois and Missouri rivers, disrupting aquatic food webs, harming native fish communities, and interfering with commercial and recreational fishing. The invasive Asian carp is one of the most urgent, far-reaching, and complex environmental challenges facing the U.S., according to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS). And experts now worry about their potential to reach the Great Lakes. For Angie Yu, founder of Two Rivers Fisheries in Wickliffe, Ky., the potential of invasive carp was obvious. Yu grew up in China, where carp is a dietary staple—so commonplace, she says, that it is eaten “just like chicken.” When she first read about the Asian carp problem in 2010, she saw an opportunity others in the U.S. had largely overlooked: a fish considered “trash” by many Americans but which had an established market in Asia. She began connecting the fish with Asian markets, eventually exporting to 12 countries. Before the pandemic, nearly 90% of the company’s products were destined for overseas markets. But then shipping disruptions, rising fuel costs, and steep international tariffs made exporting increasingly difficult, prompting Yu to turn her attention to building a domestic market. She has since developed a use-everything model for the fish, making meatballs, patties, dumplings, along with pet food, as well as fish meal and fertilizer from the trimmings, guts, and other waste, while finding uses for the skin and bones. Her carp is now sold in Asian supermarkets across the U.S., and her processing plant can handle up to 40,000 lbs of carp a day. “The fish is not trash,” she says, “it’s a treasure.” The question now is whether commercial demand for invasive Asian carp can grow large enough to meaningfully reduce their populations. For Peter Stoett, a professor at Ontario Tech University and co-chair of the IPBES Assessment on Invasive Alien Species and Their Control, the key is to “measure the population, not the market.” A growing commercial fishery, more restaurants serving an invasive species, or thousands of pounds harvested may signal that a market is working—but not necessarily that the ecosystem is recovering. Reductions in an invasive species’ numbers or biomass, he says, are the clearest indicators of whether management is working. But measuring that requires sustained monitoring: “Money must be spent monitoring these populations or we won’t know if targets are being met.” Stoett argues that if a market is used to encourage removal, the commercial activity should exist because the species needs to be reduced, contained, or eradicated—not because the goal is to establish a sustainable fishery for that species. In other words, the conservation objective has to come first, with the market serving as a tool rather than becoming the goal itself. As populations decline, he says, there needs to be ongoing dialogue among scientists, industry, local communities, and government about whether incentives should continue, change, or eventually end. In Kentucky, there is some evidence that removal efforts are having an effect. The Kentucky Department of Fish and Wildlife Resources has increased its fishing incentive to $0.15 per lb. for invasive carp harvested from Kentucky Lake and Barkley Lake, citing evidence that management efforts are reducing populations in both lakes. Most of the carp processed by Yu comes from those same two lakes. Meanwhile, according to a USFWS spokesperson, around 30 million lbs of invasive carp are removed from Midwestern waters each year. The idea of harvesting and eating invasive species can sound like an elegant solution: turn an ecological problem into dinner and let appetite do the work. But Roy cautions against treating consumption as a panacea for control. The ecological payoff from harvesting is rarely proportional to the share of a population removed. Taking half of a population, for example, does not necessarily mean halving its impact on the ecosystem. That does not make the markets emerging around lionfish, green crab, ta’ape, and carp meaningless. They can provide food and income, engage communities and, in some cases, redirect fishing pressure toward invasive or overabundant species whose removal can benefit ecosystems. But Roy stresses that these benefits must be considered separately from the ecological damage caused by an invasion: “There’s no simple sum here.” Creating demand could theoretically give people an incentive to keep an invasive species abundant, although Côté says she is not aware of evidence that this has happened. And while “nothing beats prevention,” says Stoett, once an aquatic or marine invasion is established and containment is the only realistic option, a market for their safe consumption could provide another tool—one whose economic benefits can be weighed against the ecological impacts of the invasion. The goal, ultimately, is not to eat our way out of an ecological disaster. It is to make sure that, when we eat an ecological problem, we are actually helping to reduce it. And in 2010, the Bureau of Prisons made a new rule that prisoners could start hormone treatment in prison if it was “medically indicated.” That means hormone treatment was the policy under the first George Bush, Bill Clinton, the second George Bush, Barack Obama, Donald Trump (the first time), and Joe Biden. In fact, it’s a straightforward application of a fundamental legal principle: If the government imprisons you, they have to provide for your basic medical needs. This isn’t charity, socialism, or some act of benevolence: Incarcerating someone makes them dependent on the government for their medical needs, so a toothache that would be just an annoyance in the outside world can, if untreated in prison, quickly become a form of torture. But here’s one example: Before I had electrolysis to remove my facial hair, I hated seeing and feeling the stubble on my face. I have other things about my appearance I’d like to change. Kingdom, “An action is arbitrary and capricious when the agency relies on inappropriate factors, fails to consider important aspects of the problem, or ignores relevant evidence.” To let the Trump policy go into effect—barring incarcerated trans people from receiving the healthcare they need—the Supreme Court is going to have to walk all over this principle, because the Trump administration’s reasons for denying people necessary medical care are no reasons at all.
