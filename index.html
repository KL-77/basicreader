Once again, NASA is asking for ideas to rescue Hubble from certain death

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-31

Visit Original Website
The Hubble Space Telescope still shines in the eyes of astronomers after 36 years in orbit, but it is not as nimble as it once was. Left alone, the observatory will plunge back into the atmosphere in the early-to-mid-2030s, closing the book on one of the most famous scientific instruments of the century. NASA last raised the orbit of Hubble in 2002, when the Space Shuttle Columbia visited the observatory for a servicing mission. The final shuttle servicing flight in 2009 did not reboost the telescope’s orbit, and with the retirement of the shuttle fleet two years later, Hubble has steadily lost altitude with no way to stave off eventual destruction. The space agency released a Request For Information (RFI) on Thursday asking commercial companies for ideas on how to reboost, service, or a controlled disposal of Hubble. Responses are due November 30. Specifically, NASA is looking for information about companies’ current and near-term capabilities for capturing Hubble and boosting it to an altitude of at least 373 miles (600 km), enhancing the telescope’s performance, or guiding Hubble to a controlled reentry. “The requirement includes that the boost and/or servicing mission must not harm the HST and that this mission is expected to occur by 2032,” NASA wrote in the RFI. The contractor would be responsible for providing the launch vehicle, servicing spacecraft, mission operations, and “crew if applicable.” NASA is not committing to buying a Hubble rescue mission, but officials want to know who might be interested in mounting such a mission, how much it would cost, and which companies would have a realistic shot at reaching Hubble before it falls out of orbit and burns up on reentry. Orbital dynamicists currently predict that it will likely happen in 2033 or 2034 without intervention, but reentry forecasts can change based on solar activity. It took 24 years for Hubble, which lacks onboard propulsion, to glide from its 2002 altitude of 362 miles (583 km) to its current orbit some 292 miles (470 km) above Earth. A reboost to NASA’s requirement of 373 miles (600 km) would likely keep Hubble in orbit for at least 25 more years. You’ll be forgiven if you’ve heard this before. Jared Isaacman, a billionaire commercial astronaut, approached NASA in 2022 about a servicing mission to Hubble with a team of astronauts on SpaceX’s Dragon spacecraft. He proposed funding most of the mission, which would have reboosted Hubble by at least 30 miles (50 km). Engineers also studied using Dragon to attach a rescue pod onto Hubble with new gyroscopes to overcome problems with the telescope’s debilitated pointing system. NASA also released an RFI in 2022 for other teams to share how they might boost Hubble’s orbit. The agency received eight responses, including a joint robotic reboost proposal from Astroscale and Momentus to pair their satellite servicing technology and in-space transport capabilities. Agency officials announced in 2024 they would not pursue a reboost mission after reviewing the responses. Mark Clampin, the head of NASA’s astrophysics division at the time, said the servicing plans from Isaacman and SpaceX “were really just notional concepts.” Fast forward to 2026. Isaacman is now NASA administrator, and the satellite servicing industry is maturing. Northrop Grumman launched the world’s most sophisticated robotic servicing satellite earlier this year in a partnership with DARPA, the Pentagon’s research and development agency. Smaller startups like Astroscale and Starfish Space have contracts for servicing and refueling demos with the US Space Force and NASA. So it’s not unrealistic to think the commercial industry could reboost and service Hubble by 2032, given enough time and resources. That was one of the lessons learned from NASA’s last-minute bid to rescue one of its smaller space telescopes, named Swift, from an impending reentry. NASA awarded a $30 million contract to a startup named Katalyst Space Technologies last year and gave the company just nine months to develop a mission to fly up to Swift, grab onto it, and propel it to a higher orbit, extending its useful lifetime. Remarkably, Katalyst built a rescue satellite and launched it by the deadline, and got within about 6 miles (10 km) of Swift. Ground teams decided not to move closer after problems with Katalyst’s power and reaction control systems. The Swift rescue attempt showed a servicing mission doesn’t have to cost billions of dollars, as one of NASA’s now-canceled servicing demo projects was predicted to cost. But Hubble is a significantly more expensive asset than Swift, so if NASA goes forward with a rescue mission, the agency would like to give a contractor more than $30 million and nine months. NASA does currently not have any funding allocated to procuring a Hubble reboost or servicing mission. “Hubble still has important science ahead of it, and we should be looking at every reasonable opportunity to keep it delivering for as long as possible,” Isaacman said in a NASA statement. “If there are innovative and cost-effective ways to extend the life of an asset already on orbit, we owe it to the American taxpayer and the scientific community to explore them. Extending Hubble’s life would mean more discoveries, more science, and more opportunities to expand our understanding of the Universe.” A crewed servicing mission like the one Isaacman envisioned in 2022 is unlikely to happen now. SpaceX aims to retire its Crew Dragon fleet by 2030. In a post on X, Isaacman wrote that he expects the new RFI will “draw interest from robotics companies.” NASA’s newer flagship observatories, sometimes described as successors to Hubble, are not actually one-for-one replacements. Hubble is tuned to observe the Universe in visible and ultraviolet light, while the Webb and Roman telescopes peer deeper into the infrared Universe. Astronomers will likely have to wait for the launch of NASA’s Habitable Worlds Observatory in the 2040s for an improvement on Hubble’s visible and ultraviolet vision. “Commercial industry has rapidly evolved in recent years to offer new capabilities for in-space satellite capture and servicing,” said Nicky Fox, NASA’s associate administrator for science. “Given the time we have until Hubble’s anticipated reentry, we are eager to explore the breadth of possibilities to boost Hubble to a higher orbit while we develop the flagship mission to follow.”

Flesh-eating screwworms spread to third state and are attacking pets

For a genuine fright this Halloween, you can turn your gaze to the southern border, where ravenous, flesh-eating maggots are festering and have taken to eating people’s pets. This week, the country’s New World Screwworm (NWS) infestation spread to a third state, Arizona, which joins New Mexico and Texas in being plagued by the horrifying parasites. To date, 74 animal cases have been identified in the US since the flies re-invaded the country in June. The flies lay hundreds of eggs in wounds and moist orifices of animals, and the resulting voracious maggots feast on their victims. The maggots resemble screws and tortuously bore and twist into living flesh, causing severe, sometimes fatal wounds, before dropping to the ground to develop into adults. Because of their ability to ravage livestock, the flies were eliminated from the country in the 1960s through aerial releases of sterile male flies, which thwart successful breeding. For years, they were held at bay using a biological barrier along the Darién Gap at the border of Panama and Colombia. But the barrier was breached in 2022, and the flies have made their way back to the US through Central America. While they are largely seen as a threat to the livestock industry, they can also devastate wildlife, domestic pets, and in rarer cases, humans. According to the latest data from Mexico’s Ministry of Health, the country has confirmed 806 cases of human screwworm infestations since the flies reemerged. Nine people died from the savage maggots. In the US, most of the 74 confirmed cases have been in livestock. But some of the cases have been in domestic pets, mainly dogs. Just this week, the US Department of Agriculture recorded four new cases in domestic dogs—which includes the new detection in Arizona—as well as a domestic cat in New Mexico. That brings the pet total to 10 cases since the flies reemerged. The US has also detected one case in a wild elk, which was reported last week. Arizona’s agriculture department said this week that the state’s index case was identified in a dog in Cochise County, which borders both New Mexico and Mexico. A local veterinarian examined the poor pup on October 26 and found a wound on the canine’s back that was “heavily infested with maggots consistent with NWS characteristics.” “We remain prepared and vigilant, and we urge all livestock owners, pet owners, and other animal industry partners to continue to monitor animals closely and report any unusual wounds consistent with NWS immediately,” the state said in an announcement. The USDA confirmed the infection and said it was working closely with Arizona on a joint response to the expanded infestation.

Along with the rest of the world, we at Ars are mourning Sir Patrick Stewart—the beloved award-winning actor best known as Captain Jean-Luc Picard in Star Trek: The Next Generation and Professor Charles Xavier in the X-Men franchise—who died yesterday at the age of 86. A statement released by Stewart’s representatives said: We announce with great sorrow the passing of Sir Patrick Stewart yesterday at his home in Los Angeles, in the loving care of his beloved wife Sunny, following a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer’s Disease and Lewy Body Dementia. He has been held in the warm embrace of family and friends, surrounded by live music, prayer, tears, laughter, and the kind of profound emotion that only he could inspire after a lifetime of devotion to us all. We will forever cherish the peaceful ease and reflection on his face, an enduring reminder of his invincible grace. Through the actor’s X account, the family has requested that, in lieu of flowers, memorial contributions be made to the Johns Hopkins Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center Fund and the Alzheimer’s Foundation of America Caregiver Resources. Born in Yorkshire, England, in 1940, the acting bug bit Stewart at the age of 12, when his English teacher handed out scripts for Shakespeare’s The Merchant of Venice and asked young Patrick to read the part of Shylock—a role the adult Stewart would later play for the Royal Shakespeare Theater (RSC), among many other parts. His signature bald head emerged early, around 19, thanks to alopecia. He briefly worked as a cub reporter and a furniture salesman before winning a scholarship to the Bristol Old Vic Theater School, and he landed his first professional role in 1959, playing a marooned pirate in a production of Treasure Island for the Lincoln Repertory Company. Stewart worked regularly in theater and on British television, and his big break came in his late 40s. The actor was conducting master classes on Shakespeare at the University of California, Los Angeles, when a TV producer developing TNG heard him lecture and thought he would make a perfect captain for the Enterprise-D. Creator Gene Roddenberry wasn’t initially keen on the choice, but Stewart’s audition changed his mind. The rest is television history. On Instagram, fellow thespian Sir Ian McKellen—who first met Stewart when both were young actors with the RSC and co-starred as Magneto in the X-Men films—recalled the day Stewart told him about the Star Trek offer. “’But Patrick, think of your stage career in the UK,'” McKellen recalled saying. “‘Do you really want to risk—getting lost in Hollywood for years and years?’ Well, he took the risk and thank goodness for us all that he did.” Stewart himself never expressed anything but pride in the role. “For huge numbers of people, I will always be Jean-Luc Picard, and that’s fine,” he told The Independent in 2003. “I really believe we created an admirable individual.” TNG ended in 1994, but it wasn’t long before Stewart created another iconic role, starring as Professor Charles Xavier in 2000’s X-Men, alongside McKellen as Magneto. He reprised the role in six more films and voiced the character in multiple video games. He was a serious actor who never took himself too seriously, relishing Shakespeare, Ibsen, and Beckett but also throwing himself into comedic TV appearances on Saturday Night Live, The Daily Show (he was a “special correspondent” for several segments in 2012), and Sesame Street. Who could forget his timeless “Soliloquy on B,” channeling Hamlet? Or his daily readings of Shakespearean sonnets that helped us all get through the COVID-19 pandemic? He voiced Napoleon in 1999’s Animal Farm, CIA Deputy Director Avery Bullock on American Dad and, earlier this year, Sir Nitchfield in the delightful Sheep Detectives. In between acting gigs, Stewart found time for charity work, advocating for such causes as domestic violence prevention and PTSD awareness among members of the armed forces, drawing on his own childhood being raised by an abusive father. All of this made Stewart one of the most beloved actors of our time. Tributes to Stewart are all over social media today, as celebrities and regular folk alike reflect on everything the actor’s work has meant to them over the years, including these heartfelt words from his lifelong friend McKellen: Before everything else, Patrick was defined by being born and bred in Yorkshire, the proud county in the north of England which he loved to the end. You could hear it in his accented voice and in the stern honesty with which he told you what he thought and felt. Yorkshiremen have no truck with half-truths or avoidance of big issues. He wore his heart on his sleeve, which made him a man to rely on, to admire and in my case, love. Ars staffers share their own reminiscences about Stewart and what he meant to them below. Boom Supersonic CEO Blake Scholl recently declared “it’s time for a renaissance in energy and in supersonic flight” as the supersonic airliner company unveiled a Colorado factory dedicated to making gas turbines for AI data centers. But Boom may be facing turbulence after early backers for both ventures have questioned the company’s delivery timeline or backed out entirely. Kirby’s latest frank assessment came during an interview with the Office Hours: Business Edition podcast that appeared on October 28, 2026. “‘29 is their official timeline,” Kirby told podcast host Monica Langley. “It’s not going to happen in ‘29—it’s going to take longer.” Boom first made headlines for flying its XB-1 demonstrator aircraft in 2024 and 2025 to demonstrate a “boomless cruise” concept that harnesses the Mach cutoff phenomenon. Such a feat involves flying just beyond supersonic speeds at higher altitudes under specific atmospheric conditions, so that the aircraft’s shockwaves refract upward and minimize the impact on the ground. The company’s plan to revive supersonic air travel has been backed by the Trump administration via efforts to ease regulations. In June 2026, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) proposed a new rule overturning a long-standing ban on commercial supersonic flights over the United States, following an executive order from President Donald Trump. Boom’s press releases have previously described achieving aircraft certification and carrying the first paying customers by 2029. The company has conditional agreements with American Airlines, Japan Airlines, and United Airlines involving non-refundable deposits of unknown amounts that would allow the airlines to fully purchase the Overture aircraft once it becomes commercially available. But United CEO Kirby pointed out that Boom still has “a lot of hurdles to overcome” while developing Overture and getting the supersonic airliner certified by the FAA. “So it’s not a guarantee that they’ll overcome,” he cautioned during the podcast interview, even though he praised Scholl and the company in general. This follows Kirby’s earlier comments back in March that put the chances of Boom’s Overture airliner entering commercial service at “50-50.” Ars has reached out to Boom for comment. Although Boom completed building the factory intended for production of the Overture airliner in 2024, the facility located at Piedmont Triad International Airport in Greensboro, North Carolina, has mostly sat dormant since that time. That has raised questions for North Carolina state leaders who provided more than $50 million in taxpayer funding to help build the Overture factory. Since October 2025, NASA has separately been doing test flights involving the experimental X-59 with a long, needle-like nose designed to reduce the sonic booms from supersonic flight. But this is a completely different approach than Boom’s use of the Mach cutoff flights. Much of Boom’s recent focus has been on developing natural gas turbines to power AI data centers as an additional revenue stream. On October 22, the company unveiled another factory in Centennial, Colorado, dedicated to producing a 42-megawatt natural gas turbine called Superpower for data centers. The Trump administration’s public support of Boom was once again evident as US Secretary of Energy Chris Wright participated in the event alongside Colorado Governor Jared Polis. Boom described its new Superpower factory as making turbine parts while still undergoing construction. It aims to deliver the first gas turbines to AI data center customers in mid-2027 while ramping up annual production to 25 turbines in 2028 and 50 turbines by 2029. Boom’s detour into powering data centers coincides with many tech companies prioritizing natural gas as the primary power source to get their projects up and running. Some data center developers are even using “mobile gas generators strapped to semitrucks” or “aeroderivative turbines originally designed for aircraft and warships,” according to the market intelligence platform Cleanview. Crusoe has been involved in building Texas data centers for Oracle, Google and Microsoft that draw upon various power sources including natural gas and renewables. Scholl said Boom “will be delivering about 250MW of Superpowers next year to other sites” but did not specify the AI data center customers that may be using the gas turbines. Still, Boom has characterized the development of a gas turbine for data centers as being synergistic with its design of the Symphony engine for the Overture airliner. During the October announcement, it also described having completed a prototype turbine core, called Sprint Core, that is intended to eventually power both the Superpower gas turbine and Symphony engine for the airliner. That turbine core is set to undergo testing at the Colorado Air & Space Port, with Boom planning to fire the turbine for the first time before Thanksgiving. That would be a concrete development milestone, but the company still has much to do in proving it can get both its AI data center and supersonic airliner ventures off the ground.

The malicious version, 2.1.313, subsequently affected “at most 5,000 users,” presumably when they either downloaded it or their systems automatically fetched and installed it. Anthropic pushed version 2.1.314 several hours after the previous version became available on Cursor, according to independent researcher Kevin Beaumont. This shows him using the LLM as a tool for revising his papers, finding and fixing bugs in code, and editing or generating figures. Before submitting the manuscript for publication, I also asked GPT-5.6 Pro and Anthropic Claude Fable 5.1 to review it critically and identify issues that reviewers might raise. I evaluated the issues they identified and made corrections where I judged them warranted. Mathematical tools have long made substantive contributions without thereby becoming authors… The point is not that the software contributed nothing. It is that contribution and authorship are different roles.” He is not against disclosure, as he makes clear by describing his method. Lynn Kuok is the Lee Kuan Yew Chair at the Brookings Institution. Keeping Southeast Asia open for business is a geopolitical task as much as a commercial one. Its relationships across geopolitical divides give companies access to competing powers’ markets, capital, and technology. In 2025, ASEAN attracted a record $243.9 billion in foreign direct investment. But conflict, U.S.-China strategic competition, and economic nationalism threaten these opportunities. Sustaining them requires protecting the security, supplies, and rules underpinning commerce. Governments must de-escalate tensions and, with businesses, understand and address the political objectives behind economic restrictions, assess whether diversification reduces vulnerabilities or creates new ones, and strengthen critical infrastructure. They have a shared interest in defending international law—even when doing so may create friction with the country violating it. The U.S. war against Iran has disrupted oil and gas supplies to Southeast Asia, which relies heavily on the Middle East. Higher energy prices raise business costs, squeeze household budgets, and increase loan-default risks. The Strait of Hormuz has also demonstrated how a strategic waterway can become an instrument of coercion, even for a militarily weaker power. Developments there serve as a stark warning about the risks of coercion and disruption in the Straits of Malacca and Singapore. In August, Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore reaffirmed their commitment to keeping those waterways, which carry nearly a quarter of global seaborne trade, free, open, and safe. That commitment does not preclude a littoral government restricting passage, an outside power pressuring it to do so, or a state or non-state actor disrupting shipping. Economic détente between Washington and Beijing could benefit businesses, but if maintaining it diminishes U.S. commitment to regional security, it could weaken deterrence and undermine the stability commerce depends on. In October 2025, China began its first significant island-building in the South China Sea in almost a decade at Antelope Reef, where it is building a runway; the reef is now China’s largest outpost in the disputed Paracel Islands. In September, China announced the opening of a joint military aviation facility in Laos. China has also expanded its presence in the waters east of Taiwan, where Japan and the Philippines have overlapping exclusive economic zones. A shift in the regional balance of power could increase vulnerability to coercion and affect the rules governing trade and investment. Strategic rivalry has brought gains for Southeast Asian countries able to maneuver for advantage: higher U.S. tariffs on Chinese goods prompted manufacturers to shift production to the region. But U.S. licensing requirements for shipments of certain advanced chips to China can delay or prevent sales by regional distributors. Malaysia, amid U.S. pressure, introduced its own controls, including 30-day advance notification for specified advanced chips moving through or out of the country. U.S. economic nationalism creates a distinct challenge: Washington’s drive to reduce trade deficits and rebuild American manufacturing extends beyond its rivalry with Beijing. Tariffs have applied across Southeast Asia, including to U.S. allies and partners—even Singapore, with which the United States runs goods and services surpluses. Vietnam, which ran a $178.3 billion goods surplus with the U.S. in 2025, has drawn particular scrutiny. Legal challenges have not ended this pressure. After the U.S. Supreme Court’s February ruling that the emergency law did not authorize Trump’s “Liberation Day” tariffs, the administration turned to temporary tariff powers and Section 301 investigations into alleged excess manufacturing capacity and failures to adopt or enforce bans on imports made with forced labor. The latter led to July tariffs on all 60 economies investigated, including several in Southeast Asia. Understanding Washington’s objectives and priorities helps determine what concessions might achieve. The administration has justified tariffs and other restrictions by citing reciprocity, reducing deficits, protecting domestic industry, raising revenue, reducing strategic dependence, and denying capabilities to a rival. Trade concessions may not remove restrictions designed to deny China strategic capabilities. Nor will moving production from China to Vietnam satisfy demands for production in the United States. Unclear or conflicting priorities will complicate responses. Securing multiple suppliers may reduce vulnerabilities, but those suppliers may still rely on the same shipping routes or Taiwanese semiconductor foundries. The region remains acutely exposed to energy supply disruptions. Businesses cannot yet count on regional arrangements to secure supplies. The revised ASEAN Framework Agreement on Petroleum Security has yet to enter into force; even once it does, assistance during critical oil and gas shortages will remain voluntary and subject to commercial terms. The ASEAN Power Grid could diversify electricity supplies, but a regional system remains elusive nearly three decades after ASEAN leaders called for it. Nuclear power could reduce fossil-fuel dependence for electricity generation. Vietnam has signed an agreement with Russia for its first plant; an Indonesian company has agreed to buy electricity from two Rosatom floating nuclear units. The Philippines has discussed nuclear cooperation with Russia. Russian reactors, however, could create long-term dependence on Moscow for technology, nuclear fuel, spare parts, and technical support. Energy insecurity has lent urgency to Philippine-Chinese talks on joint oil and gas development in the South China Sea. Yet any deal’s benefits must be weighed against its implications for Philippine maritime rights and potential dependence on Beijing. Resilience can contribute to deterrence by reducing what hostile actors expect to gain from disruption. Governments must work with owners and operators to strengthen critical infrastructure, including subsea cables, ports and data centers. A World Economic Forum report to which I contributed advocates joint vulnerability assessments, standards setting, communication mechanisms, and response planning. The rules underpinning commerce also need defending: economic rules provide predictability and recourse, while international law protects freedom of the seas and constrains the use of force. Upholding international law may cause friction, but countries should remain steadfast. Not wanting to choose sides should not be an excuse for failing to defend the rules: this is not about alignment with any country. Keeping Southeast Asia’s commercial options open will require taking a stand to defend rules that support peace and prosperity. More than 700 profitable corporations paid 10.4% in current federal income taxes last year, according to a recent report from the Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy (ITEP), a progressive think tank—less than half of the 21% statutory federal corporate income tax rate. More than 250 of those companies paid effective tax rates below 7%, and 114 companies—including Tesla, Walt Disney, United Airlines, and DoorDash—paid no current federal income taxes, the report said. ITEP calculated these tax rates based on analyses of annual financial reports filed to the Securities and Exchange Commission, and not IRS data, which lags behind SEC filings. Corporate tax revenue has plummeted under the Trump administration, which has ushered in a series of tax breaks meant to boost capital investments, even as corporate profits have soared. Treasury Department data released earlier this month showed about a 15% reduction, about $70 billion, in corporate income tax revenue to $381 billion in the fiscal year that ended Sept. 30. The drop is part of a more than 25% reduction in corporate tax revenue since 2024, when former President Joe Biden was in office. Diminishing corporate tax revenue has some policy experts concerned about a ballooning federal deficit and the U.S. government’s ability to fund itself. The Congressional Budget Office projected President Donald Trump’s One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) would add $3.4 trillion to the federal deficit over the next decade, a result of a $4.5 trillion decrease in revenues offset by a $1.1 trillion decrease in spending. Rather than blame the more than 100 companies paying nothing in federal income tax, however, it’s the hundreds of corporations paying a reduced rate that present the bigger threat to tax revenue, according to Matt Gardner, ITEP senior fellow and coauthor of the report. Spooky science: How your brain conjures up night-time scares

Silicon Republic | 2026-10-30

Visit Original Website
University of Bristol’s Prof Michelle Spear looks at how your brain could be responsible for things that go bump in the night, not ghosts. A version of this article was originally published by The Conversation (CC BY-ND 4.0) It’s three in the morning. You’ve woken from a dead sleep because you’ve heard a thud in the darkness or a floorboard creaking downstairs. Or perhaps you’ve woken because you have the unmistakable sensation you’re not alone. During daylight, these phenomena might be dismissed as the heating, your neighbours or the sounds of an old house settling. But in the middle of the night, they can feel a tad more sinister. Your mind might immediately start reeling, jumping to the worst-case scenario – wondering if you’ve been broken into, or if you’re being haunted by some supernatural force. In reality, the explanation is far more simple. Things really do go bump in the night. But occasionally, the culprit is closer to home than we might imagine. Falling asleep is not the neurological equivalent of switching off a light. The brain moves gradually from wakefulness into sleep, accompanied by changes in sensory processing, muscle activity and awareness of the world around us. But these changes don’t always occur in perfect synchrony. So for brief periods, characteristics of sleep and wakefulness can overlap. The results can be surprisingly spooky. If you’ve ever been on the verge of sleep only to feel that you’ve fallen off a kerb, missed a step or dropped through the bed, you’ve probably experienced a hypnic jerk. Also known as a sleep start, this is a sudden involuntary contraction of one or more muscles as we move into the earliest stages of sleep. It’s an example of how the transition between wakefulness and sleep isn’t always a smooth one. Hypnagogic experiences can also involve vivid sounds, images or sensations. So as you’re on the edge of sleep, you may think you hear footsteps or whispers – or you may even think you see a figure in the room. Exploding head syndrome – perhaps the most alarming name for a harmless sleep condition – is another example of how the boundaries between sleep and wakefulness can become blurred. As someone is falling asleep, or occasionally waking, they suddenly perceive an enormous noise: an explosion, gunshot or slamming door – yet nothing in the outside world has made a sound. Its precise cause remains uncertain. But the phenomenon demonstrates something important: hearing a sound and a sound actually occurring are not quite the same thing. The conscious experience of hearing is constructed by the brain. So neural activity generated without an external sound can still produce an auditory experience that seems entirely real. The brain doesn’t even need to be asleep for this to happen. If you’ve ever thought you’ve heard someone call your name – only to turn around and find no one there – you’ve been tricked by your brain. Another possible explanation for the sounds we think we hear at night involves source monitoring. This is the brain’s ability to distinguish events generated internally from those coming from the outside world. Thoughts, memories and imagined speech are internally generated, yet normally retain the quality of being ours. But occasionally, that attribution goes awry – allowing something generated within the brain to feel as though it came from somewhere else. Around sleep, that boundary becomes particularly interesting. Another common – but frightening – experience that can happen at night is waking up with the sudden feeling that someone else is in the room. This experience is usually the result of sleep paralysis. During rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, when most of our vivid dreaming occurs, the brain temporarily suppresses activity in most muscles. This protective mechanism, known as REM atonia, prevents us from physically acting out our dreams. But during sleep paralysis, our waking awareness returns while this physical paralysis persists. You know where you are, but cannot move. Yet elements of dream perception can remain active. This is why you may have experienced the sensation that someone – or something – is in the room with you, despite clear sensory evidence of the contrary. One clue as to why this happens comes from experiments examining how the brain distinguishes itself from others. In one study, participants controlled a robotic device positioned in front of them, while a second robot, which was placed behind them, reproduced these movements by touching the participants’ backs. But when researchers introduced a slight delay between the movement and the touch, some participants began to feel as though somebody else was touching them. Some even sensed another person standing behind them. While this experiment doesn’t explain every felt presence that can happen during sleep paralysis, it does reveal something remarkable: the distinction between ourselves and others is something the brain has to construct. Usually, movement, touch and our sense of body position agree about what belongs to us. But if that relationship is disrupted, an internally generated sensation can begin to feel like it originated elsewhere. Now picture this happening in a dark bedroom, when you’re partly awake yet still on the edge of dreaming and unable to move. The sensation that somebody else is there becomes rather less mysterious. Perception is not a passive recording of the world. It combines incoming sensory information with expectation and previous experience to construct the most likely explanation. But at the boundary between sleep and waking, this process can become less reliable. We may hear sounds that were never made, experience fragments of dreams intruding our bedroom or interpret sensations arising from our own bodies as the presence of somebody else. And on the prospect of settling the broad conflict between Jews and Palestinians, the election uncloaked an aspect of Israeli politics that will likely worsen tensions, regardless of the final vote count. The key factor is this. Hamas’ murderous raid of Oct. 7, 2023; Israel’s wildly excessive retaliation; and the inability of outside parties to impose an arrangement that ends the war, empowers a peaceful Palestinian Authority, and repairs the devastation in Gaza—all of these acts of ultraviolence and neglect have frozen Israeli politics in a state of seemingly permanent trauma. Hamas will not disarm until Israel withdraws from Gaza; Israel will not withdraw until Hamas disarms. Both positions are, in their way, understandable. Meanwhile, the vast majority of Israelis—even those who were once lefties or pacifists—consider it suicidal to so much as think about surrendering territory to Palestinians, much less negotiating a separate Palestinian state. One surprising outcome of the election made this fact all too clear. Israel’s Arab parties won 19 parliamentary seats—almost twice as many as the 10 they won in the prior election. If either of the two main Jewish candidates—Netanyahu or Eisenkot—invited the Arabs to join his coalition, he would gain more than enough seats to win a majority and thus lead the government. (Netanyahu’s bloc won 53 votes, and Eisenkot’s 48; it takes 61—half of the Knesset’s 120 seats, plus 1—to form a majority.) However, neither politician is likely to do that. Netanyahu has no desire to court the favor of Arabs, and even if he did for the sake of retaining power, he would probably lose as many far-right partners from his coalition as he would gain. Eisenkot, while not quite as extreme on the matter, promised before the election not to form coalitions with parties that don’t formally declare Israel to be a Jewish state—a stance that was widely (and accurately) taken to mean an oath not to partner with Arab parties. The Arab parties do not represent the roughly 5 million Palestinians in Gaza or the West Bank; those residents of occupied territories lack the right to vote in Israeli elections at all. Rather, they were supported by the 2.1 million Arabs who live in Israel proper—and almost all of them are Sunni Muslims, Christian Arabs, or Druze. Over the years, Israelis have fended off charges that they live in an apartheid state by pointing to the equal rights held by these Arabs. Yet, in a season when the Arab parties have won enough seats to make them potential kingmakers, the Jewish parties—their leaders, and to a great extent their constituents—have decided, at least for now, that they’d rather forgo certain victory if it meant giving Arabs positions in the government. This blatant denial of political power to Israeli Arabs, even when they’ve earned it through peaceful democratic means, would further isolate Israel from its allies—and could radicalize Israeli Arabs who until now were content with working within their country’s political system. The double standard has always been there, but now it’s too obvious to pretend away or disguise. The question of whether the election will make a difference when it comes to Jewish settlers’ violence against Palestinians in the West Bank is more complicated. Eisenkot has spoken out against the government’s tolerance—in many instances blithe encouragement—of the violence. If Netanyahu is thrown out of power, so will his main henchmen on this issue, Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich, the minister of national security and the finance minister, respectively, who believe that Gaza and the West Bank should be formally annexed. However, while Eisenkot has opposed annexation, he is in favor of continued—even expanded—occupation. And to the extent the occupation is a root cause of the tensions and violence in the area, its problems will persist. On other matters, Netanyahu and his Jewish opponents differ more distinctly. Netanyahu—who has been prime minister, on and off, for 19 years, longer than any other in Israel’s 78 years as a nation-state—has clearly stated that he wants to resume full-scale war on Iran for the purpose of changing its regime. Eisenkot has dismissed regime change as a fantasy and has said he’d prefer to sway Tehran’s behavior—away from supporting terrorist groups and developing nuclear weapons—through economic pressure. Still, Netanyahu remains prime minister until the election is settled, and if he decides to start bombing Iran again, and if Iran bombs Israel in return, Eisenkot—the former top general of Israel’s military—would not likely oppose further retaliation against Iran. Some speculate that under those circumstances, he might accept calls to form a “unity government,” joining the two coalitions. It would be a tenuous, probably short-lived government, since the two leaders—and their followers—hate each other, but it would presumably be a popular move for the short term. However, this is theoretical speculation, as Netanyahu is unlikely to resume bombing unless he gets at least the green light, if not active cooperation, from Donald Trump. The American president has said he might start bombing again after next week’s midterm elections—a telling qualifier. He wouldn’t do so before the midterms because he knows that the war is unpopular and that its resumption could wreck the Republicans’ already-slim chance of hanging on to the House—and possibly to the Senate as well. No one knows what Trump may do after the midterms; perhaps Trump himself doesn’t know. If the Republicans lose, especially if they lose big, will he take that as a sign to steer away from Middle East wars, as he long ago promised voters he would do? Will a Democratic Congress vote to forbid him from resuming attacks on Iran (as the narrowly Republican-controlled Congress almost did)? Will Trump abide by that vote or ignore it as a violation of his Article 2 powers? Trump endorsed Netanyahu in these elections and, as a result, will no doubt take credit—perhaps legitimately—for the prime minister’s Likud party winning a larger share of votes than the polls, and even exit polls, had predicted (though far fewer than it won in the previous election). However, what happens next is beyond Trump’s control. If neither faction can form a majority, then new elections would have to be called. In the interim, Netanyahu would remain prime minister, but his supporters would form only a minority of parliament. This gridlock could persist for months. The stalemate would hobble the state, which might compel Netanyahu and his opponents to make some kind of deal, either with each other or with the Arab parties. Or maybe they won’t be able to pull off such a feat. The divisions in Israeli politics and society may be too severe to mask—and any deal could aggravate tensions among the coalitions’ constituents. The region’s wars have hit a lull in recent weeks, but they are far from over. The dynamics unleashed by the elections—Israel’s and ours—could extend the calm or recharge the violence. How the Uber-bound delivery giant tuned its mobile networking stack to cut latency When you're ordering food from your phone, even time spent navigating the app is time wasted. At the P99 2026 virtual conference, Elvis Chidera, a staff engineer at Germany-based global delivery service Delivery Hero, explained how the company moved most of its user base to HTTP/3, and consequently cut its apps’ total end-to-end response time of the home screen by 17 percent. Chidera, who managed the project, explained that, as promised on the tin, HTTP/3 delivers the web faster than HTTP/2, though it has slow bits that need to be adjusted to work properly. Delivery Hero is all but unknown in the US, which is dominated by Uber Eats and DoorDash. But it is a company with global reach outside the US, with an estimated user base of 60 million across Europe, Latin America, Africa, the Middle East, and Asia. Uber is currently acquiring Delivery Hero for $14.8 billion, with plans to unite the company with Uber Eats. With this purchase, Uber would expand the number of markets where it offers both ride-hailing and delivery services, though not necessarily under the Uber Eats nameplate. Delivery Hero centrally manages separate apps for different regions, all of which it built or acquired. Asians can order poke bowls and sundries through Foodpanda. Turks can order kebabs through Yemeksepeti. Throughout Spain, Italy, Poland, Romania, Ukraine, and much of Africa, the Glovo app is where to ring up the late-night deliveries. And in Latin America, users dial in to PedidosYa to get what they need from nearby farmacias, restaurantes, and mercados. All these regions have widely varying network connectivity, a problem Delivery Hero wanted to minimize. Last mile networking was most often the bottleneck. Apps in Pakistan, for instance, could take nearly a second (at p90) to establish a secure handshake with a server. The engineering team looked at HTTP/3 as a way to lower, even eliminate, that latency. The Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) formally introduced the third version of the internet’s Hypertext Transfer Protocol in 2022. HTTP is the application layer protocol that connects browsers and other resources connected to servers over the Internet. The previous versions of HTTP were built on the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP), which offered guaranteed delivery and flow control. In conjunction with the Internet Protocol (IP), TCP/IP built the modern internet. HTTP/3 replaced TCP with Quick UDP Internet Connection (QUIC), developed by Google, to address some of TCP’s perceived sluggishness as a transfer protocol. UDP stands for User Datagram Protocol and, unlike TCP, it does not guarantee packet delivery or flow control. UDP is faster because it doesn’t wait around for a signature (QUIC adds in its own flow control on top of UDP). But the UDP-based HTTP/3 reduces, and sometimes eliminates, the round trips needed to secure connections. The app can cache a session ticket and, on a later connection, use QUIC's 0-RTT capability to start sending eligible application data while the handshake is still underway, rather than waiting for it to finish. “Essentially, you can eliminate that one second” of latency that plagued slower networks, Chidera said. “You don't want to pay the cost of two round trips just to exchange data, if you could do it faster.” And unlike HTTP/2, HTTP/3 doesn’t get hung up when packets are lost or delayed. There’s no head-of-line blocking because packets are sent in multiple streams, not just the single string that HTTP/2 offers. Setting up HTTP/3 turned out to be surprisingly easy, Chidera found. Delivery Hero used Cloudflare’s content delivery network to serve its global user base, so moving to HTTP/3 involved a simple reconfiguration. Adding HTTP/3 support to the clients, mostly mobile, proved almost as straightforward. Apple has supported HTTP/3 since iOS 15 and most native browsers already support HTTP/3 out of the box. Android proved a bit trickier. The engineers used Google’s Cronet library for the networking stack, initially wired into their Android apps with the Cronet Transport for OkHttp wrapper to minimize rewriting their existing code, originally written for the OkHttp HTTP client. Out of the box, Cronet helped support the HTTP/3 rollout, which reduced network-level request failures. But it also came with a significant catch: the engine took approximately 800 milliseconds to initialize at P90, eating into some of HTTP/3's 0-RTT speed benefits. So the team forked the code into their own bridge using a library called OKCronet. They also did some additional engineering work, which involved overlapping Cronet initialization with other background tasks. Overall, they slashed the P95 latency time from 1.2 seconds to 120 milliseconds, achieving a 10x improvement in read latency. Overall, the engineering team moved 95 percent of the users over to HTTP/3. Those devices that couldn’t use the protocol (largely due to networks blocking UDP traffic) automatically fell back to HTTP/2. Handshake latency between the apps and the server was cut by 51 percent. Pakistan’s response time was cut in half. One hurdle remained, however. Secondary screens on some of the apps (pages/screens that aren’t the home page) experienced latency issues. These issues only happened in certain markets that were known to be prone to packet loss, such as Pakistan. Delivery Hero’s engineers attributed the issue to Cloudflare’s choice of the congestion control algorithm for HTTP/3, Cubic, which historically has not handled packet loss well. The engineering team tested some of its traffic with Google HTTP/3-aware servers, which use another congestion protocol, Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time (BBR), and did not get hung up on the packet loss. Delivery Hero is currently working with Cloudflare to resolve this specific congestion algorithm issue, Chidera said. GLP-1 weight-loss drugs are one of the biggest biotech breakthroughs of the last decade. But to some clinical psychologists, they can represent something more sinister—a potential trigger for eating disorders. A new report suggests that the global incidence of anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa shot up between 1990 and 2023—by a whopping 71%. By 2023, there were an estimated 18.1 million people living with one of these two disorders. What’s worse is that the number is likely to be much higher than that once we factor in other eating disorders. And, disturbingly, those in the field are already starting to see GLP-1 weight-loss drugs exacerbating the issue. Cheri Levinson is a clinical psychologist at the University of Louisville in Kentucky who has specialized in eating disorders for almost 20 years. She says she’s now seeing more patients with more severe symptoms than at any other time in her career, and that many of them are misusing GLP-1s. “This is the worst I’ve seen things,” she says. Eating disorders are mental health conditions in which people’s eating behaviors impact their health. They include anorexia nervosa (defined by a fear of weight gain), bulimia nervosa (in which people binge and purge), binge eating disorder, and others. It’s thought that some people have a genetic predisposition to certain eating disorders, but that some external factor is what triggers their onset. That might be stress, or social pressure to be skinny. Often, simply restricting food intake can trigger an eating disorder, says Levinson. Eating disorders—along with the pressure to look thin—were already prevalent in the 1990s (the heroin chic era springs to mind). To find out how things might have changed since then, Ruhai Bai at the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University in China and his colleagues pulled together data from studies and surveys conducted on populations around the world. The team used statistical models to fill in any data gaps. That research allowed the researchers to estimate the annual incidence of two eating disorders—anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa—for each of 204 countries and territories between 1990 and 2023. They also estimated the impact those disorders had on global health by calculating their disability-adjusted life years (one DALY represents a year of healthy life lost). The findings, published in JAMA Psychiatry on Wednesday, are unsettling. Between 1990 and 2023, there was a 71% increase in the incidence of those two eating disorders, the team found. Those conditions had a huge impact on global health, too. In 2023, they “cost the world the equivalent of about 3.83 million years of healthy life,” says Bai. Bai and his colleagues expect these figures to continue to rise. In 2023, an estimated 18.1 million people had either anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa; by 2050, the team predict, the two conditions could affect 21.8 million people. It gets worse. Those two conditions represent less than half of all eating disorders, says Levinson, who was not involved in Bai’s study. The true impact of these disorders is “probably three times the rate that they’re reporting,” she says. Bai and his colleagues didn’t explore the reasons for this rise (although Bai points out that it can’t be entirely explained by population growth). So how did we get here? Social media may have played a role. “Social media comparisons, influencers, challenges, diet trends can all be precipitating … events for an eating disorder,” says Cynthia Bulik, a clinical psychologist at the University of North Carolina, who was not involved in the new study. Bulik has been studying and treating eating disorders since the early 1980s. “Between 1990 and 2023 there has clearly been an upsurge of those types of environmental exposures,” she says. Many social media users are exposed to filtered and modified images of skinny influencers who may have undergone cosmetic surgery or extreme dieting and who are promoting “thinspiration” or “fitspiration” content. But users with eating disorders appear to be more likely to engage with this content—and to be fed more of it as a result. When researchers at the University of Melbourne investigated this phenomenon a couple of years ago, they found that the algorithms of TikTok users with eating disorders delivered 4343% more “toxic eating disorder videos” than those of users who didn’t have the disorders. They also got more content focused on dieting or exercising. Many were fed over 1000 problematic videos per month. Bulik describes social media as “quicksand” for people with, or at risk of, eating disorders. The covid-19 pandemic—and the social isolation that accompanied lockdowns—also appears to have contributed to the rise in eating disorders. Research conducted in 2021 found that the number of people hospitalized with eating disorders in the US doubled between 2018 and 2020. Researchers don’t yet have good data on how trends in eating disorders might have progressed since 2023. But anecdotally, things seem to be getting even worse. “There are more eating disorders, more severe eating disorders… and we’re seeing earlier ages of onset,” says Levinson. “We’re seeing eight- and nine-year-olds with eating disorders.” “From what I’m hearing and seeing, I am not going to be surprised if that number isn’t at least doubling again,” she adds. Levinson particularly worries about the impact of GLP-1 weight-loss drugs, which have exploded in popularity in recent years. A Gallup survey conducted earlier this year suggested that 15% of Americans had used one of these drugs for weight loss. And they’re becoming more accessible—drug companies are working on new formulations that are both easier to take and more effective, and many people can buy versions of these drugs from compound pharmacies or dubious online sources. In a small study published earlier this year, Levinson and her colleagues found that 30% of people with eating disorders had used GLP-1 drugs. The drugs might benefit some of those people. But 10% appeared to have misused the drugs. And Levinson thinks that figure is also an underestimate. Levinson says she and her colleagues have seen children develop eating disorders after being prescribed GLP-1s for medical reasons. They’ve seen others—even children as young as 11—buy versions of these drugs over TikTok. “We’re very concerned that GLPs will lead to even faster and more extreme weight loss in people with anorexia nervosa, and that patients will come to us in even more advanced stages of illness that become difficult to reverse,” says Bulik. Both Bulik and Levinson are continuing to study the impacts of these drugs in eating disorders. In the meantime, both urge medical professionals to consider the risks GLP-1s might pose to people with those disorders. Bulik contributed to a set of research and clinical guidelines, published earlier this month, that recommends doctors screen for eating disorders before prescribing the drugs, for example. The guidelines also recommend more patient education and more robust monitoring of people who take GLP-1s. If you think you or someone you know might have an eating disorder, you can find information and support at the National Eating Disorders Association. This summer, hundreds of OpenAI agents tried to cheat on a cybersecurity test. They broke out of their test environment and successfully hacked Hugging Face, a site that they believed held clues about how they’d be graded. Around the same time, another OpenAI agent accessed private files from an Australian government website. It’s a problem that’s by no means confined to OpenAI. In the months since, Anthropic, Google, and Meta have also disclosed incidents in which their agents have gone off track. And worryingly, in many cases the agents eluded the very researchers and safety teams tasked with monitoring them. AI agents are taking unauthorized actions, and the companies building and running them are mostly relying on luck and hindsight to mitigate any harmful impacts. They must do more to ensure safety. That’s why EU regulators and US agencies are rightfully calling for agent developers and deployers to monitor agents in real time. But these rules assume that companies can see what their agents are doing and why—when in reality, they lack sufficient visibility. As agents roll out across critical sectors, from finance to government to medicine, we must be able to monitor them in real time to prevent wide-ranging and potentially devastating consequences.  And while safety depends on monitoring, monitoring requires observability: the capacity to capture what the system did, process it into signals, and present those to humans or automated oversight systems. In other words, we need to know what the agent did and why it did it. And as our recent research shows, current agent frameworks—the software that runs agents— do not reliably generate the crucial observability records we need to ensure that agents are deployed safely. Agents present new kinds of problems that generative AI alone does not. They inherit the unpredictability of the LLMs they are built on, but where a generative AI system produces a single output for a human to act on, an agent performs longer tasks—it plans, picks tools, spawns sub-agents, and takes actions. So an agent’s course can take shape in a multitude of different ways, and no tests before launch can fully anticipate the risks. This makes observing the agents while they work even more challenging, and even more important. With colleagues from academia, civil society, and industry, we studied the four most widely used agent frameworks to see what signals they actually emit. We found major gaps that made it hard to achieve key safety goals, like noticing policy violations, unauthorized actions, and even human oversight failures. Key observability requirements were missing—like Agent IDs that persist over time, and records about which humans approved agent tasks. This made it hard to tell which agents did what or assess whether they were taking unauthorized actions. The good news is that these gaps, once identified, can be closed. Doing so starts with capturing two types of records: those that reveal what happened in an agent’s run and how the agent made its decisions. The “what happened” records identify the agent (while preserving privacy) and log how humans intervened and whether agent permissions or “memory” shifted, while the “decision” records show an agent’s reasoning and levels of confidence in the actions it’s taken. Take, for example, agents working to process a bank’s mortgage loans. The system examines an application and recommends denying it, and then a human loan officer signs off on the decision. In a later audit, or perhaps even in real time, a bank or regulator needs to be able to reconstruct which agent made the call, which officer approved it, and how long they took. And the bank must also analyze records of how the agent arrived at its refusal decision in order to ensure it did not use off-limits information, like details about the neighborhood the applicant is from. Recording “what happened” and “how the agent decided” require different approaches, but both are feasible. The current lack of “what happened” records is mostly a software plumbing problem that can be addressed through OpenTelemetry, an open observability standard maintained by engineers across the industry and by the agent frameworks adopting it. OpenTelemetry has launched a working group drafting shared formats for tracing agents, but they have yet to be finalized. The group should urgently incorporate standards for maintaining persistent agent identity and for recording human interventions and changes to agents’ permission or memory. And it should be done before these systems can scale, all the while attending to the privacy implications of agent ID that links to real people. Preserving “how agents decided” records is more challenging. The issue raises open research questions for model providers and researchers: Does the step-by-step reasoning a model displays, known as chain of thought, reflect how the model actually made a decision? And do we have reliable ways to measure how confident the agent is in that decision? These questions need answers before anyone sets standards for how such observability records are used. No single organization or sector can close the observability gaps on their own, but together, they can support the infrastructure required to prevent dangerous incidents and support safety. AI developers building agent frameworks, like OpenAI, Anthropic, CrewAI, and LangChain, must integrate telemetry signals recording an agent’s persistent identity, any human interventions, and any changes to an agent’s permissions and memory. Meanwhile, enterprises adopting these agent frameworks have an influential role to play; enterprises in regulated industries, like banks and hospitals, can put pressure on the agent developers to supply these missing observability records where standards do not yet exist. Enterprises must also define policies, permissions, and roles that set out what agents can safely do, since telemetry alone cannot assess whether agents broke specific rules. Finally, regulators and AI standards institutes must specify the observability records required for high-stakes AI deployments while leaving the technical details to industry. We have already seen initial momentum here: NIST’s agent standards initiative in the US is working on agent identity, one of the most salient gaps we found in our research. No bank would let a thousand unbadged employees loose on customer accounts and hope to piece together what happened when something goes wrong. Yet that’s how we’re deploying AI agents. Making their behavior observable is the first step toward fixing this. And by building the regulatory and policy ecosystem that makes it possible to act on what we see, we can hold organizations accountable. Until then, every task we hand to an agent hands over our agency, too. Our climate models have a big, dangerous blind spot

MIT Technology Review | 2026-10-29

Visit Original Website
Climate change itself is increasingly fueling climate change. Rising concentrations of greenhouse gases are driving up global temperatures, setting off dangerous feedback loops that we have not fully accounted for in our climate models—or our emissions-reduction goals. Scientists have long warned that warming would propel further warming in various ways, but a growing number of observations and studies are at last providing a sharper sense of just how powerful these cascading effects could be. And, by extension, they’re showing how much faster the planet may warm in this century. A study in Environmental Research Letters in September, synthesizing the results of previous papers, found that the additional carbon dioxide and methane released from thawing permafrost, intensifying wildfires, hotter lakes and reservoirs, and wetter, warmer wetlands alone could drive global average temperatures up 0.2 °C to 0.4 °C this century. That would come on top of the 2.6°C of warming above preindustrial levels that modelers are already expecting through 2100. The additional heat could subject many of the world’s cities to monthslong heat waves and expose hundreds of millions of people to flooding. None of the models that inform the major UN climate reports, which assess how much the planet will likely warm under various emissions scenarios, account for all these feedback effects. Many of them don’t incorporate any. That leaves a huge blind spot in our understanding of how much the world could actually warm in the coming decades—and how rapidly nations will need to slash emissions to stay within critical boundaries. A separate analysis published in One Earth in March, which examined what the feedback problem means for our international climate goals, concluded that the world may be much further off track than is widely believed. It found that just three regional sources of warming-driven emissions—thawing permafrost, increasing fires in boreal forests, and warming tropical wetlands—may shorten by as much as 25% the time before the planet consistently breaches the 1.5 °C and 2 °C warming thresholds. Our climate models and the UN’s reports clearly need to be updated to better represent these effects. Nations must also step up their efforts to reduce emissions to reflect this improving understanding of the dangers we face, says Sam Abernethy, the lead author of the paper and a climate scientist at Spark Climate Solutions, a San Francisco–based nonprofit research group. At least some of this is starting to happen. Spark kick-started an initiative, called the Warming-Induced Greenhouse Gas Emissions Model Intercomparison Project, through which more than 20 international teams are running the same set of analyses on different models to develop greater understanding of feedback effects’ potential power. Researchers involved hope that the UN will incorporate those results into its next major climate assessment, due out in 2029. That report will update estimates of the remaining emissions that can be released before the world crosses key temperature thresholds. But what is already clear is that the planet is rapidly warming and that nations haven’t done nearly enough to cut fossil-fuel pollution. And even if we did manage to halt it, warming-driven emissions could continue. So a number of the research groups involved in the recent studies have started to explore what nations could do to get feedback emissions under control. Scientists at the Woodwell Climate Research Center in Massachusetts are, for example, investigating the possibility of preventing fires or rapidly detecting them and putting them out when they threaten to burn the vegetation and soil that help insulate particularly carbon-rich wedges of permafrost (a subject I explored at length in a recent feature story). In addition, the Environmental Defense Fund’s Climate Innovation Initiative has backed lab research on the potential of adding sulfate, iron, or other compounds to wetlands to counteract fast-rising methane emissions. Several startups are also exploring possibilities. They’re looking into suppressing the lightning strikes that are igniting more and more fires in high latitudes, placing synthetic mats above permafrost to prevent it from melting, and deploying autonomous drones to quickly suppress potentially high-emissions fires before they burn out of control. Investigating all these ideas is worthwhile. But researchers are only in the earliest stages of figuring out how big a dent in emissions any such efforts could achieve across sprawling global ecosystems undergoing massive transformations. The surest path to reducing the effect of warming-driven warming remains the one we’ve known all along: breaking society’s addiction to fossil fuels.
