Fridge hack costing millions and sparking fires stemmed from poor security

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-14

Visit Original Website
Earlier this week, a mass hack of refrigeration systems at an estimated 700 supermarkets throughout France caused them to suddenly malfunction, causing spoilage of untold tons of perishables and, in some cases, fires. As is now the norm, basic cybersecurity would have prevented it. French authorities say the systems, made up of entire fleets of refrigerators and freezers throughout a store, were hacked. More specifically, Internet-connected controllers that allow technicians to remotely administer the store-wide systems were compromised. The unknown attackers responsible then activated defrosters long enough for the fridges to reach temperatures as high as 50° Celsius (122° Fahrenheit). By the time the mass compromise was discovered, food was spoiled, and in some cases their electrical systems caught fire. Multiple security advisories and public research reports show that all three of the controller models reportedly hacked in the incident—sold by Danfoss of Denmark and Dixell and its parent company Copeland in the US—contained critical vulnerabilities that allow attackers to remotely log in to the devices with the same system rights as administrators. Until recently, Danfoss controllers, for instance, used a credential that changes each day. The password is generated by the publicly available firmware based on the date. A hacker who takes the time to analyze the code can do the same thing on any device that hasn’t received a recent update. Daily passwords are identical across models located in hundreds or thousands of stores. A raft of critical vulnerabilities fixed in the past few months in Copeland/Dixel controllers also give similar remote access. Besides running controllers with outdated firmware, the affected stores likely also failed to put the devices behind a VPN and create a restricted list of IP addresses allowed to log in. These protections are standard but often not followed. In this case, that failure enabled a mass hack that likely cost tens or hundreds of millions of euros. Spain, Portugal, Poland, Germany, and Belgium were also reportedly affected. “Two things would have changed the outcome,” researchers at Pen Test Partners said Wednesday. “Cyber hygiene would have prevented the access: take the controller off the public internet, put remote access behind a VPN and an IP allowlist, bring third-party kit onto the network in a controlled, owned and documented way rather than leaving it on a contractor’s own connection, change known and default credentials, and patch to a current release.” Researchers from security firm Claroty warned of the threat of ignoring these common-sense steps last October. The US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency did the same in February. In an email, Copeland acknowledged a vulnerability “identified in a discontinued, end-of-life generation of controllers” and said the company is working with customers to prevent similar incidents in the future. Danfoss representatives didn’t immediately respond to questions. CNN said the company also cited discontinued models. So far, the party behind the attack has remained silent. That raises the possibility that the motive was vandalism on a mass scale. Another possibility is a nation-state or other international actor aiming to create a continent-wide disruption. The incident underscores the tension between the financial and security departments in most businesses. Some companies are loath to spend large sums of money on new equipment when there are no tangible benefits or savings. Only after a devastating hack do they realize why the investment was worthwhile. Executives at affected supermarket chains will likely learn the hard lessons. Less clear is whether the cautionary tale will sway other businesses.

Suspected Russian drone attacks sank a cargo ship and forced the evacuation of another vessel within two dozen miles of the coast of Bulgaria in the Black Sea on October 13. The latest attacks mean that five ships have been attacked in the exclusive economic zones of NATO member countries—spurring requests for NATO to redeploy counter-drone systems to provide more protection. Although there is no hard proof that the drone attacks originated from Russia, one of the ships that came under attack was the Ukrainian-operated coastal freighter Chelsea 3, registered under the flag of Malta. Bulgarian Prime Minister Rumen Radev told the Russian ambassador in Sofia that “it makes no sense for Ukraine to destroy its own ships in the Black Sea, and the results of these strikes favor Russia’s interests,” according to the Associated Press. All 15 members of the Ukrainian-owned Chelsea 3 were safely evacuated by Bulgarian rescue operations after the ship was reportedly struck by three aerial drones. But authorities are still searching for 11 missing crew members of the Guinea-Bissau-flagged cargo ship Dignity, which sank after coming under attack, the Associated Press reported. Five mariners were initially rescued from that ship. These incidents mark the latest in a growing series of unprecedented attacks on Black Sea shipping in the exclusive economic zones of NATO countries. One week earlier, aerial and sea drones sank the Togo-flagged cargo ship Alfa Watan and left the Palau-flagged cargo ship Able afire about 80 miles (128 kilometers) off the coast of Bulgaria on October 6. The 18 crew members of the Able were rescued—two required hospitalization—but the 10-member crew of the Alfa Watan has been presumed dead after rescuers failed to find any survivors. Such incidents first began when the Turkish-owned cargo ship Royad Mammadov came under drone attack and eventually sank near Romania’s Black Sea coast on October 5. All 11 crew members were rescued. In response to the attacks, Bulgaria has stepped up cooperation with affected NATO member countries and the alliance as a whole. On October 14, Bulgarian Defense Minister Dimitar Stoyanov told the national parliament that the country would join NATO’s Drone Edge initiative that includes member countries committing a total of $40 billion toward developing more counter-drone capabilities over the next five years, according to the independent news publication The Sofia Globe. NATO officials are also now weighing the possibility of moving existing anti-drone countermeasures to the region after receiving requests from Bulgaria, Romania and Turkey, diplomats told Politico. The three countries are also cooperating on an initiative to safeguard freedom of navigation in the Black Sea, which Bulgaria’s prime minister Radev plans to present to the European Union this week. Naval forces from NATO countries have avoided military exercises in the Black Sea since Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, in accordance with the 1936 Montreux Convention that grants Turkey authority to restrict passage of ships through the straits leading into the Black Sea. This Turkish enforcement has also prevented Russia from reinforcing or rotating its Black Sea fleet during the war. Despite lacking much of a conventional navy, Ukraine has managed to largely neutralize Russia’s warships and disrupt Russian-linked shipping and ports by using aerial and sea drones. But Russia has countered with its own drone attacks on Ukrainian ports and vessels, along with sometimes targeting ships from other countries in the region. Since the fall of 2023, Ukrainian ships have typically stuck to sea lanes running through Romanian, Bulgarian, or Turkish territorial waters in the Black Sea, according to the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace. Such Black Sea shipping is especially crucial for Ukraine’s agricultural exports of grain, accounting for well over 90 percent. The recent spate of probable Russian attacks in the Black Sea is now disrupting shipping carrying Ukraine’s latest harvest to the rest of Europe, with some cargo being rerouted overland or through the River Danube, Politico reported. These unprecedented attacks in waters near NATO countries coincide with a growing wave of incidents attributed to Russian hybrid or gray-zone warfare tactics. Such incidents include an attempted explosive drone attack on Ukrainian cargo aircraft at Leipzig Airport in Germany and a Russian drone boat loaded with explosives found floating near a Romanian offshore gas platform in the Black Sea. But since the start of the war, Turkey, Romania and Bulgaria have continued upgrading their navies while forging closer cooperation on maritime security, according to Carnegie. The growing dangers for shipping in the Black Sea will likely accelerate this cooperative trend—and for the NATO alliance as a whole.

Things are finally looking up for NASA's first missions to Venus in a generation

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-14

Visit Original Website
Most planetary scientists will tell you the heyday of Venus exploration was more than a generation ago. That is about to change. Two NASA missions are on track to launch to Venus in the early 2030s. The first, known as the Deep Atmosphere Venus Investigation of Noble gases, Chemistry, and Imaging (DAVINCI), is now scheduled for launch in December 2030, a year earlier than previously planned. DAVINCI will send a parachute-assisted descent probe through the atmosphere of Venus, returning the first direct images of the planet’s surface since 1982. A second mission, Venus Emissivity, Radio science, InSAR, Topography, And Spectroscopy (VERITAS), will follow with a launch in November 2032 to map the Venusian surface by radar from orbit. The European Space Agency is building another orbiter, Envision, for launch to Venus in 2032. The Venus armada might get started as soon as next year, when Rocket Lab aims to launch a privately funded probe to search for signs of organic material in the planet’s clouds. That was the update last week at a meeting of the Venus Exploration Analysis Group (VEXAG), a confab of scientists whose research interests focus on the scorching world just sunward from Earth. Europe’s Envision orbiter will also launch to Venus in 2032. The Venus armada might get started as soon as late 2027, when Rocket Lab aims to launch a privately funded probe to search for signs of organic material in the planet’s high-altitude clouds. None of this was guaranteed. Budget cuts imperiled one US mission to Venus and delayed the other, then forced NASA to pull out of a partnership with Europe to supply a key part for Envision. But scientists at last week’s VEXAG meeting struck an optimistic tone. “We have some good news for Venus,” said Jim Garvin, a planetary scientist at NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center and principal investigator for DAVINCI. It wasn’t easy for Venus to find its moment. Mars has hogged the spotlight for decades and remains the long-term lodestar for human spaceflight. The enigma of comets and asteroids lured the world’s space agencies into funding a series of robotic probes to study them. The icy moons of Jupiter and Saturn, some of which may hold the ingredients for life, are the latest hotspots for planetary exploration. But Venus ventures closer to Earth than any other planet, making it the easiest world for a spacecraft to reach, if not land on. Venus is roughly the same size as Earth and may have once shared a similar climate, but something happened to turn it into an inhospitable world with an atmosphere made of nearly pure carbon dioxide, clouds of toxic sulfuric acid, and temperatures hot enough to melt lead, about 872° Fahrenheit (467° Celsius). There’s still plenty to learn about Venus. The planet is less well mapped than Mars, and scientists aren’t exactly sure what triggered Venus’ runaway greenhouse effect. It is notoriously difficult to land on Venus, where the air pressure is 93 times that of sea level on Earth, equivalent to about 3,000 feet beneath the ocean’s surface. NASA’s last mission to Venus was Magellan, which mapped the planet’s tortured surface with a radar instrument capable of peering through the atmosphere’s opaque acid clouds. Magellan launched in 1989 and ended its mission in 1994, revealing the broad strokes of Venus’ landscape for the first time, but with only enough resolution to resolve features the size of a football field or larger. That left a lot to be desired. Japan and Europe launched orbiters to Venus in 2005 and 2010 with instruments tuned to study the planet’s atmosphere. But it took 30 years for missions to improve on Magellan’s map to win support from decision-makers at NASA and the European Space Agency. The fortunes of Venus scientists changed in a big way in June 2021, when NASA and ESA selected three Venus missions for development in a span of just eight days. “The Venus community is absolutely elated and excited and wants to just get to work and see this happen,” said Ellen Stofan, a Venus scientist and under secretary for Science and Research at the Smithsonian Institution, in an interview with Ars at the time. It turned out the fight for Venus wasn’t over. Less than two years later, NASA officials considered canceling one of the two NASA-led missions, known as VERITAS, amid budget pressures in the agency’s planetary science division. The money problems largely stemmed from delays and cost overruns with the Psyche mission, NASA’s first spacecraft to explore the metal asteroid. The VERITAS project stayed alive, but just barely. At the same time, NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory, the institution in charge of Psyche, VERITAS, and a host of other science missions, struggled through layoffs and the cancellation of NASA’s Mars Sample Return mission, which was set to become the most expensive project in the lab’s history. The goal of VERITAS is to build on the legacy of the Magellan mission. The VERITAS orbiter, carrying a much better radar than Magellan, will vastly improve the resolution of maps of Venus, with a particular focus on regions with active volcanoes. At the same time NASA slowed the development of VERITAS, it was committed to providing a radar instrument to the European Space Agency for the Envision mission, an orbiter with similar goals as VERITAS. NASA’s contribution to Envision was put in question by the Trump administration last year, and Ars reported in September that ESA was moving ahead with a European-built radar for Envision after NASA pulled out of the agreement. Nick Lang, a program scientist in NASA’s planetary science division, confirmed this at last week’s VEXAG meeting, citing limitations in NASA’s budget. “That decision was not an easy one,” Lang said. NASA’s planetary science budget remains under pressure. The Trump administration has twice proposed deep cuts in planetary science. Congress restored most of the funding for fiscal year 2026 and is expected to do the same in the 2027 budget. Ending support for Europe’s Envision mission and canceling Mars Sample Return freed up enough funding for NASA to move forward with DAVINCI and VERITAS, and in some cases, buy back some of their past delays. DAVINCI is now targeting a launch in December 2030, a year earlier than the previous plan. “Thanks to encouragement in early ’26 from our leaders at NASA Headquarters and the Planetary Science Division,” said Garvin, who leads the DAVINCI science team. “This is our new scenario for the most cost-effective and science-effective launch to Venus … What we’ve done now is accelerated launch by 54 weeks to a case where we have a very low delta-V trajectory that gets off the ground in late December 2030.” Low delta-V is an important qualifier. This means the trajectory for DAVINCI will require relatively little energy, at least as far as interplanetary missions go. A flyby with Venus in early 2032 will set up DAVINCI to release its atmospheric entry probe for descent to the planet’s surface on October 3, 2032. The mission’s carrier spacecraft will continue zooming through the inner Solar System, and the revised trajectory should allow it to hold enough fuel in reserve to enter orbit around Venus when it comes back around again, something that wasn’t always assured, Garvin said. The comeback of VERITAS is even more striking. “VERITAS is now funded with a launch date of November 2032, so this is great news for us,” said Dan Nunes, the mission’s deputy project scientist at JPL. “We’re staffing up. We’re accelerating, not to the point of Jim’s acceleration [on DAVINCI], but we’re moving.” VERITAS is targeted to launch the month after DAVINCI arrives at Venus. “It keeps building on momentum for Venus science, which is great,” Nunes said. “We’re going to arrive at Venus close to the middle of 2033, and we’re going to have a phase of aerobraking” before beginning routine science observations. In recent months, engineers working on the DAVINCI mission completed experiments showing how the Venus probe’s cameras and instruments will capture data during its one-shot descent through the atmosphere. It will take about an hour for DAVINCI’s battery-powered titanium descent craft to travel from space to the surface of Venus, and all of its imagery and data must be relayed to Earth through a radio link with DAVINCI’s carrier spacecraft before it goes over the horizon. “We have to optimize everything in the final eight to 10 minutes of descent as we come into the supercritical atmosphere to get this data back for all of us to work with,” Garvin said. The DAVINCI team also recently subjected a prototype of the entry probe to the extreme temperatures it will see at Venus. The insulated sphere kept its internal components below 86° Fahrenheit (30° Celsius), meeting requirements. DAVINCI’s managers at Goddard reported results from these two tests to NASA Headquarters and Administrator Jared Isaacman, and to the White House, Garvin said. The news is similarly positive for VERITAS, according to Nunes, who credited scientific advocacy groups for the mission’s survival. “VERITAS is going,” Nunes said. “We would like to thank all the support from the community, from VEXAG, the Planetary Society, all those people that went out there and made the case for Venus exploration for keeping VERITAS alive and getting us a launch date, so we’re very thankful for that.”

Schools and libraries from around the US pleaded with the Federal Communications Commission to drop the idea of ending E-Rate, a $2 billion-a-year program that connects students and library visitors to the Internet. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr has criticized school districts for having “experimented with a massive increase in screen time for students,” and said E-Rate expanded too far beyond its original purpose of “supporting basic Internet access to schools and libraries for educational purposes.” In June, Carr led a 2-1 vote to issue a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) seeking public comment on whether E-Rate should be “limited or sunset to reflect today’s extensive connectivity rates.” Carr’s proposal could result in E-Rate cutting the discounts that schools and libraries receive for telecom services and equipment, and imposing more restrictions on how E-Rate money can be used. The FCC seeking comment on whether to sunset the program also raises the possibility that Carr might choose to axe E-Rate entirely. The National Association of Elementary School Principals told the FCC that it “strongly supports the continued operation of the E-Rate program and urges the Commission to reject proposals that would eliminate, substantially restrict, or impose new conditions on this essential source of connectivity assistance for schools and libraries.” The Carr FCC already scaled E-Rate back last year by ending funding for schools and libraries to lend out Wi-Fi hotspots and for Wi-Fi service on school buses. The FCC docket received comments this week opposing the proposal from state and national associations, and from many individual schools and libraries. The American Library Association told the FCC that E-Rate is working as Congress intended it to when it passed the 1996 telecommunications law that resulted in E-Rate’s creation. “Importantly, the FCC does not have the authority to terminate the E-Rate program. The E-Rate program is mandated by Congress, and the FCC is responsible for administering it,” the national library group said. If Carr goes through with eliminating E-Rate, he may argue that its original mission has been completed and that the FCC therefore has authority to end it. “In establishing the program in 1996, Congress was addressing a specific problem: limited access to advanced telecommunications and Internet services in schools and libraries,” Carr’s proposal said. “Given the substantial expansion of broadband access in schools and libraries over the last three decades, we seek comment on whether and to what extent the E-Rate program has fulfilled that mission and whether continued funding is consistent with Congress’s original objective.” There are other questions about FCC authority raised by the proceeding. For example, the American Library Association said “the FCC lacks the authority and expertise to regulate screen time or determine educational outcomes.” The group’s filing pointed out that under US law, determinations about what content is inappropriate for children “shall be made by the school board, local educational agency, library, or other authority responsible for making the determination.” The law says the US government may not establish criteria for making those decisions or review the decisions made by local schools and libraries. The Texas Library Association told the FCC that E-Rate should be giving more money to libraries to help them raise broadband speeds to modern standards. The group also said public libraries in the state have implemented appropriate supervision and filtering for the Internet services they offer library users. “Only 35 percent of Texas public libraries currently receive E-Rate discounts, compared with 65 percent of public libraries nationwide, and only 55 percent meet the Commission’s own recommended broadband speed benchmarks,” the group said. “A gap this size, in the nation’s second-most populous state, is evidence of unmet need working through real obstacles—not evidence that the need has passed.” The New York Library Association told the FCC that “E-Rate is essential for reliable broadband speeds and for regular network maintenance, safety, and security. The library can’t operate without the Internet. If the FCC terminates or limits E-Rate funding, many of New York’s libraries will be forced to reduce programs and services in order to continue providing Internet access.” The American Indian Higher Education Consortium told the FCC that it should relax E-Rate’s eligibility restrictions and provide more funding for schools. It said that supporting Tribal colleges and universities is “one of the most cost-efficient ways the federal government can endeavor to uphold its treaty and trust obligations to Tribal Nations and Tribal citizens, while simultaneously benefiting the nation as a whole.” A filing by the Alabama Association of School Boards urged the FCC to “preserve and strengthen the E-Rate program and to reject any proposal that would eliminate, sunset, substantially reduce, or unnecessarily restrict E-Rate support for schools and libraries.” The group said concerns about excessive screen use “should not be addressed by reducing the broadband infrastructure that students and teachers need for legitimate educational purposes.” The FCC received specific examples of how E-Rate helps local communities. “We estimate our annual Internet cost at approximately $3,120 and our annual E-Rate support at approximately $1,560,” Paul Lapka, the principal of Rock Valley Christian School in Iowa, told the US agency. “E-Rate support allows us to maintain reliable, high-speed Internet and network equipment. If support were reduced, we would face higher local costs, delayed network upgrades, and pressure to redirect funds from other school priorities.” Were the FCC to end E-Rate, schools and libraries would no longer be able to purchase new equipment and broadband service, which would upset broadband providers. “The widespread availability of broadband connectivity and service in schools and libraries is an important E-Rate achievement, not a reason to withdraw support for the connectivity on which those institutions continue to rely,” the FCC was told by America’s Communications Association, a cable industry lobby group. “Schools and libraries must continue to pay for broadband service, maintain and replace internal network equipment, and upgrade capacity as their needs evolve.” The Schools, Health & Libraries Broadband Coalition (SHLB) called it “disheartening and surprising that the Commission in the current proceeding asks questions that run directly counter to the goals of universal service and Congress’s directives. While most of the country and world are racing ahead to prepare for the future facing our nation’s citizens and students, the Commission is asking whether it should shelve or significantly reduce the E-Rate program, which is the financial pillar supporting most schools’ and libraries’ digital connections.” FCC Democrat Anna Gomez voted against Carr’s proposal in June, saying that E-Rate helps give children in low-income neighborhoods and rural communities “the same shot at a digital education as anyone else.” She criticized the FCC’s inquiry into screen time, saying that “policing children’s behavior in schools goes far beyond our stated mandate. The FCC is not the nation’s parent. It is not the nation’s teacher. And it is not the nation’s school board.” E-Rate typically distributes over $2 billion a year. It has a funding cap of $5.2 billion, but actual payouts are based on demand and the application approval rate. E-Rate and other Universal Service Fund (USF) programs are paid for by fees imposed on phone companies, which usually pass the cost on to consumers on their monthly bills. The deadline for the first round of comments passed yesterday, and reply comments may be submitted until November 12. After the comment period, Carr can present his final plan to either change or eliminate E-Rate. Given that Republicans have a 2-1 majority on the FCC, whatever Carr proposes is likely to be approved. But FCC decisions are often challenged in court on the grounds that the agency exceeded its authority.

However, Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington, the watchdog group suing, is calling foul, noting that DOGE was given taxpayer dollars to the tune of $41 million to fund its operations. DOGE also incorrectly sought to apply the Cheney standard—a 2004 case that shielded the executive branch from complying with public records requests—in order to maintain autonomy of its office and safeguard confidentiality of its communications, the lawsuit argues. This case will be critical in holding DOGE accountable for not just the utter destruction it caused within the U.S. government, but in setting a clear precedent for how U.S. presidents can or cannot bypass federal disclosure laws. For decades, journalists in particular have relied on FOIA to unearth waste, abuse, and ethics violations committed by the U.S. government, something that’s become alarmingly common under Trump. CREW first filed FOIA requests with DOGE in January 2025, around the time President Donald Trump established the agency via executive order. The watchdog group noted that Musk was tasked with running DOGE even though he never received official congressional approval and was operating “seemingly unchecked, with no clarity on its structure, staffing, budget or operations, including whether it is following federal ethics and transparency laws or even acting within its legal authority,” CREW said in a statement. The group’s goal was to arm Congress with critical information that could inform its upcoming appropriations cycle. However, things did not go to plan. The Trump administration flatly refused to comply with CREW’s records request, while DOGE fired tens of thousands of federal employees, collected Americans’ most sensitive data, and dismantled core agencies like USAID, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, the Department of Education, and more. By February 2025, CREW had filed a lawsuit in order to force DOGE’s cooperation. In court the Trump administration argued DOGE was not legally required to comply with FOIA requests because it was not technically a federal agency. U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper disagreed with that assessment and concluded that DOGE was likely subject to FOIA “because it is endowed with and is apparently exercising ‘substantial authority independent of the President.’ ” Unsatisfied, the Trump administration tried to appeal Cooper’s order but was rejected by the D.C. appeals court panel. Come May 2025, the administration decided to knock on the Supreme Court’s door, asking the justices to stay Cooper’s order, and they agreed. In an unsigned order, they said Cooper’s order was overly broad and needed to “counsel judicial deference and restraint in the context of discovery regarding internal Executive Branch communications.” In response, CREW amended its FOIA request to exclude any information about recommendations DOGE staffers made directly to the White House. Lower courts declined to block its records request, effectively forcing the Trump administration to hand over the bulk of CREW’s original FOIA request. Still dissatisfied with having to give even an inch, the Trump administration did not comply with that ruling, and instead came back to SCOTUS this year, asking the justices to consider the case on its merits and assess all of CREW’s FOIA requests of federal agencies, from DOGE and beyond. It accused Cooper of continuing to approve “intrusive discovery against a presidential advisory body without adequate consideration for the separation of powers, the FOIA statute,” and the Supreme Court’s order from last year. The administration wants SCOTUS to apply the Cheney standard, while CREW has argued “this case looks nothing like Cheney,” and if it did it would effectively foreclose discovery in this case, which is not what SCOTUS said in last year’s order. The Trump administration’s position in this case comes as no surprise, as the president is openly hostile toward the press and every other body that seeks to hold his administration accountable. Plus, he likely wants to protect the man singlehandedly bankrolling GOP campaigns for this year’s midterm elections, and beyond. With three weeks to go until the midterms, Democratic campaign groups and super PACs are speaking with one voice: They have the right environment. They have the right message. They have the right candidates. “Given the Republican cash advantage, we now find ourselves in a situation where we could be leaving winnable races on the table,” Julie Merz, executive director of House Democrats’ campaign arm, wrote in an Oct. 9 memo, warning that a dozen or so opportunities—those only reachable because of the anti-GOP environment—might be missed without an infusion of cash. The memo encouraged donors to cough up another $17 million over the next two weeks. House Democrats’ main super PAC, House Majority PAC, put its request at $38.5 million. On the Senate side, the main Democratic super PAC, Senate Majority PAC, warned that “Republican-aligned outside groups have built a financial advantage neither party has had before, large enough to bury our many advantages under a paid media blitz before November.” And Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer has put the cash disadvantage front and center in his messaging: “With just three weeks until Election Day, the battle for control of the Senate comes down to one defining question,” Chuck Schumer wrote in a letter to his colleagues on Monday. “Will the unprecedented tsunami of Republican money defeat the massive Democratic advantage in candidates, policies, and political climate?” “Unprecedented tsunami” isn’t hyperbole. While it’s hard to get an exact number on the cash advantage, the Associated Press estimates that the “$1 billion that Republicans have amassed compares with roughly $579 million held by a comparable collection of major Democratic groups.” A Washington Post analysis of the top 50 donors this cycle shows Republicans having raised $1.07 billion from the group relative to Democrats’ $454 million. These numbers don’t even account for third-quarter sums that have yet to be disclosed. Elon Musk, as the New York Times first reported, gave a stunning $276 million to GOP groups and super PACs in the third quarter, raising his total giving for the cycle to $366 million. The $158 million alone he gave to Senate Republicans’ top super PAC, the Senate Leadership Fund, covers the group’s staggering investment in Texas to rescue Ken Paxton. “Democrats are in the strongest position yet to win a Senate majority with a map we have expanded deep into Republican territory,” Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee executive director Devan Barber said in a statement. “Republicans are making a last-ditch effort to rescue their flailing candidates and hang onto their vulnerable majority by flooding battlegrounds with increased spending. Democrats have been vastly outspent across the battleground and need additional resources to maintain the advantage we built with stronger candidates and a better message.” Where did this cash advantage come from, anyway? It’s not that Democratic groups are struggling to raise money (with the notable exception of the Democratic National Committee). As is typically the case, individual Democratic campaigns themselves are regularly outraising their GOP counterparts, whose candidacies are more reliant on big checks from outside groups. Consider the Texas Senate race, where James Talarico’s campaign had raised $69 million through the end of the second quarter while Paxton had only raised $9 million. While that’s a particularly lopsided example, Senate Democratic campaigns hold significant advantages in most other major battlegrounds. And Democrats’ main House and Senate super PACs each recently announced record third-quarter fundraising totals. The bulk of the disparity can be traced to a GOP fundraising vehicle for which Democrats have no equivalent: MAGA Inc., the president’s super PAC. By the end of August, MAGA Inc. had $416 million on hand. It begun disbursing that money in bulk to House and Senate races after Labor Day. The nature of how Donald Trump, as well as other GOP super PACs, came into this money has become a campaign talking point for Democrats. By speaking so openly about the cash disadvantage, Democratic leaders aren’t just trying to raise more money. (Though they are definitely trying to raise more money—think of these despairing memos to “interested parties” as the highfalutin, big-donor equivalent of daily Democratic spam emails threatening the apocalypse if you don’t donate $1 RIGHT NOW.) They’re working the fundraising disparity into their larger messaging on Trump’s corruption, with MAGA Inc. and others serving as a cash receptacle for those seeking something from the transactional president. As Schumer put it in his letter to colleagues, “The checks are coming in from every entity looking to buy a pardon, curry favor, and move the needle in favor of their personal interests.” “Trump’s modeled a way to get this unlimited amount of cash from people who are seeking business before the government,” Lauren French, a spokesperson for Democrats’ Senate Majority PAC, told me. So how does this financial edge for Republicans help them—and how does it not? In short, it keeps Republicans—who have very little going for them—in the ballgame and allows them to avoid some tough decisions. While Republicans have started to pull ads in some states and districts they deem unsalvageable, that list would be a lot longer were it not for the hundreds of millions of bonus dollars that have materialized for them. Only recently did Republican outside groups cut funding from the Georgia and North Carolina Senate races; they may not have put much money into those states to begin with in any other cycle. They have the resources to backstop suddenly vulnerable Senate candidates in places like South Carolina and Nebraska, and can spend $150 million to save Jon Husted in Ohio while Elon Musk effectively foots the roughly similar bill to rescue Paxton in Texas. And in the House—where MAGA Inc. is sprinkling a few million dollars here, a few million there to dozens of races—the money can prevent the bottom from falling out. But money isn’t everything—especially super PAC money. And Republicans haven’t managed their largesse particularly well. It’s not just that campaigns can purchase ads at lower rates than super PACs. It also matters when those ads are purchased. Much to congressional Republicans’ chagrin, Trump’s team dawdled for months before committing their MAGA Inc. funds. That means Republicans have been buying ads in already saturated markets at expensive rates. Advertising rates in Texas are now so expensive, for example, that pro-Paxton super PACs have found it cheaper to run ads nationally during football games than to buy time in the Texas media markets. In short, Democrats, by having better-funded campaigns and reserving advertisements earlier in the year, are getting more bang for their buck. It’s also important to note that for ads to work, it’s important for them to be good. Even with the higher rates that Republicans are paying, they are airing more ads. But a Politico survey from last week presented a peculiar finding: 70 percent of respondents “said they’d either seen more spots in support of Democrats or an equal amount between the parties” over the previous week, while “only 24% said they’d seen more ads for Republicans.” In other words, Democratic ads appear to have been more resonant. It’s not just that Republicans have less to run on in this environment. It also raises questions about how well these ads are being slapped together. With hundreds of millions of dollars being pumped into the ecosystem at once, and ad-makers earning astounding sums of cash for their quick work, you’re going to get a lot of slop. “I was talking to a Republican consultant who ran one of the big super PACs and he, like, hates all of their media consultants,” Anna Greenberg, a Democratic pollster, told me. “Because they’re grifters, they make so much money. There’s no incentive for these Republican media people to do a good job. Because they make, whatever it is, tens of millions of dollars whether they win or lose. And so they buy so badly.” However haphazardly they manage it, the GOP’s financial advantage is still an advantage. It won’t change the overall direction of the midterms, magically giving Republicans a good night against such wretched fundamentals. Where it matters is on the margins, and so much rests on the margins this cycle. In order to take control of the Senate, Democrats don’t just need to flip the regular old swing states. They need to flip some red states. Among the options: Ohio, which Trump won by 11 points in 2024; Alaska, which Trump won by 13; Texas, which Trump won by 14; Iowa, which Trump won by 13; and Kansas, which Trump won by 16. (Then there is Maine, which Harris won by 7, but where Susan Collins is sui generis.) Democrats, in other words, need a strong Election Day to take control of the House, but a historic one to take control of the Senate. If all Republicans’ billions can muster is even one measly percentage point in their direction, that could be enough to turn Democrats’ performance from one of unlikely Senate conquest to one of very, very impressive losses across the map. Seated across from me, in her pearls and charcoal pantsuit, is Neera Tanden—one of the most accomplished political agents in America, and someone to whom I have just posed a deeply offensive question. We are in her spacious corner office at the Center for American Progress, colloquially known as CAP, the influential liberal think tank where Tanden serves as both president and CEO. She’s in the midst of a busy day, with places to go and people to see. Just a few minutes from now, Tanden will be on stage with Vermont Sen. Peter Welch, discussing how Trump’s tariffs are impacting the supply chains of the state’s cheesemakers. Earlier this morning, she was huddled in a room full of men—each brandishing neatly bullet-pointed memos—drumming up talking points about A.I. safety that will soon be disseminated across Capitol Hill. But I don’t want to talk about any of that. Instead, I asked her something I’ve wondered about for years. When I see Tanden on Twitter/X late at night, embroiled in a flame war with the socialist Twitch streamer Hasan Piker, or the leftist podcaster Sam Seder, or, on more than one occasion, a random shitposter with a username like @Bot420Crazy, is she drunk? She knows what I’m talking about. She’s seen the memes. Some of her most inventive haters have dubbed her “Beera Tanden.” Another shitposter, with a hammer-and-sickle studded in their bio, is currently operating under the username “Neera Tanden’s Wine Addiction.” But no, she is not drunk. In fact, she doesn’t really drink much at all. “I mean maybe I’ll have a glass of wine,” she said. “But the bad tweets are 100 percent sober tweets.” This is just what life is like for a woman who has become, without question, one of the great villains of the online left. Who has an army of trolls who fill up her mentions every day with invective. And—there’s no doubt—Tanden takes some pleasure in dispatching them. About the theory that she would only be engaging with these people if she were plastered? “I find it to be completely sexist. I don’t understand it. What, did I misspell something?” she said. At her core, Neera Tanden is a policy wonk. Policy wonks are not typically famous: If they are, that fame is restricted to a league of Beltway professionals—famous in the way that really good brain surgeons are famous to other, presumably lesser brain surgeons. I saw her aptitude firsthand as I tailed her around CAP. She relishes the tedious sausage-making of statecraft, the kind of stuff that makes you and me bleed from our ears. Tanden can reflexively rattle off the particularities of housing protocols I couldn’t begin to decode; she titters with satisfaction upon recounting the intricacies of a long-discarded Australian cap-and-trade proposal. This is a skillset of someone who is happiest in the darkest corners of government—the swampiest of swamps—a realm of acronyms, white papers, and multihyphenate bureaucracies. And that is exactly what makes Tanden’s transformation into one of the country’s most widely derided Twitter pundits so bizarre. It all began, like so many things did, in 2016. Tanden—who has close ties with the Clintons, and has spent her entire professional life within the ironworks of the Democratic Party—backed Hillary for president. (She served as an unofficial adviser to the former first lady and secretary of state throughout her run.) Meanwhile, an amassing contingency of voters regarded Clinton tepidly at best, and were more inspired by Bernie Sanders. As the primary grew testier, Tanden took to Twitter to defend the sanctity of normie liberal values, and to implore (some might say scold) Sanders supporters into lining up behind the establishment candidate. (“I’m old enough to remember when the left was the strongest fighters against fascist governments,” she posted, just months before the election.) Some portions of Tanden’s audience were amenable to her frustrations, but others were happy to make her a vessel for their exasperations with the overarching Democratic brand. They called her an empty suit, or a soulless enabler of corporate politics, or, at other times, a drunk and an idiot. Tanden only dug in further, her annoyance with the party’s leftward flank recursively expanding, in a stalemate that persists to this day. Tanden has since cemented her status as the unlikely face of a feud that continues to bifurcate the Democratic polity; a wound that dramatically fissured in 2016, and, to this point, has yet to heal. She isn’t exactly a firebrand, but she is very much available; willing to take on all comers, vociferously, at seemingly any hour of the day. If you scroll through Twitter, you might encounter Tanden engaging in a testy back-and-forth with progressive journalist Mehdi Hasan. Other times, you’ll see her riposting a man named @JrGoob. The pattern repeats ad nauseum, in slightly different permutations. I’ve seen Tanden get into a headline-making spat with progressive blogger Matt Bruenig; I’ve even watched her carry on a one-sided vendetta against Susan Sarandon. (Tanden was furious about the actress’s support of perennial Green Party nominee Jill Stein.) Unsurprisingly, her current focus is the aforementioned Hasan Piker, a man who is no stranger to controversy and has courted plenty of internet feuds of his own. Throughout the summer of 2026, when Piker was popping up on various midterm campaign trails, Tanden condemned him as a hatemonger who dabbles in antisemitic tropes—someone who has no place in the big tent—with her usual aplomb. “Proud to be one of the people fighting off the predator,” said Tanden, just days ago, while quote-tweeting a profile of Piker by veteran center-left columnist Jonathan Chait, which does, indeed, characterize the streamer as a marauding force within Democratic politics. It is the prolificness of her social media output that made Tanden one of the very few people in politics to suffer professional ramifications from her conduct online. In 2021, when the Biden administration nominated Tanden to run the characteristically opaque Office of Management and Budget, senators on either side of the aisle roadblocked the confirmation. One of them was Bernie Sanders; he wasn’t happy with Tanden’s tweets. You might be wondering what use any of this is. Especially considering how, online, Tanden appears to be profoundly alone. Sure, she has her allies in and around the Democratic Party, but her fellow policy nerds are not spending their evenings on X. She often seems to be a solitary voice crying out against the onslaught. Every tweet is met with an infinite scroll of rejoinders; no observation she posits goes unpunished. And yet, despite all the tirades thrown Tanden’s way, the isolation doesn’t bother her. Perhaps because she understands that the internet is not real life, and that her message is resonating with a silent majority. More than anything else, that’s what keeps her around. “There was a woman on the Hill who told me she was in a group chat where people share my tweets and they appreciate me standing up, even though people scream at me,” said Tanden. “Even when everyone is yelling at me, people will hear what I’m saying.” Really, If you ask her about any of her online kerfuffles, Tanden sticks to that refrain: “People viciously attack normie Dems, and nobody defends them,” she said. “The problem with the online world is that people can say insane things, and it becomes conventional wisdom unless you have an alternative.” Through that lens, clapping back at Bot420Crazy rings a bit more existential—a battle for the soul of liberalism that Tanden genuinely feels is at risk of being hijacked, and can perhaps be course-corrected by browbeating one shitposter after another. Frankly, that might be what is required of our think tank executives in 2026, in an era of mass disinformation and flatlining public trust. It is not enough to do the work of pragmatic policymaking. You must be prepared to stridently defend it to the trolls. And it’s important to remember that Tanden has been involved in basically every big-ticket Democratic Party project of her lifetime. She had stints on Bill Clinton’s presidential bids, and Hillary Clinton’s senatorial operation. She burrowed deep in the recesses of the early Obama administration, welding together the voluminous Affordable Care Act. She volunteered for the Dukakis campaign, for God’s sake. In fact, she is so enmeshed with the Democratic establishment that Joe Biden did eventually find a job for her. (She chaired the gloriously ambiguous “Domestic Policy Council.”) All of this has made Tanden something of an idealist. She believes in principled liberalism with the exact same vigor with which the revolutionary vanguard in her mentions wants to dismantle our sclerotic institutions. No wonder they’ve never seen eye to eye. “I’m a big government-policy wonk. That’s my whole life. I’m a big government liberal. And most people in government are trying to solve problems,” Tanden told me. “This might sound absurd, given the level of cynicism, but I actually do believe the Democratic Party is a force for good in this world.” The problem, of course, is that a waning number of Americans agree with Tanden. The Democratic Party is unpopular, and has been for years. Liberal consensus is smarting after two humiliating defeats by Donald Trump. Nobody should be surprised that voters are seeking alternatives further out on the left, as a handful of key Senate races this midterms cycle demonstrate. But ironically, there really isn’t that much daylight between CAP’s portfolio and the kinds of topics bandied about at your local Democratic Socialists of America chapter. For instance: Tanden is on record endorsing Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez’s Green New Deal resolution. One ambitious report published by the think tank floated the radical idea of curbing the credit-card swipe fees enforced at grocery stores. And how about the fact that Patrick Gaspard, who ran CAP while Tanden was away in the Biden world, has since emerged as one of Zohran Mamdani’s advisers? Set her digital footprint aside, and “you would almost think, ‘Well, this is the moderate wing of the Bernie Sanders movement,’ ” said the political blogger Matt Yglesias, in another recent profile of Tanden. Sure, she’s done plenty to rankle hardcore leftists beyond her posts. Chief among them is an incident in 2015, when CAP hosted Israel’s Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu for a softball Q&A. (Tanden brought this up unprompted during our interview. She blamed a Pollyanna-ish Obama administration. “Ben Rhodes was like, ‘This is a good thing to do.’ I would have never done it, but they were trying to get him on a path to be less insane,” she said. For what it’s worth, CAP has struck a more adversarial tone toward Israel in the intervening years.) But at least from my vantage point, the differences between the two factions are more rhetorical than they are material. They’ve just been fighting for too long to know how to stop. Naturally, the past decade of abuse has taken a toll on Tanden. She speaks about herself like she’s a martyr for the Democratic mainstream—a human shield sponging up all the slings and arrows at the left on behalf of those in her coalition who have much more at stake. Tanden willingly participates in the roasting, and as a woman of color, she’s destined to be on the brunt end of the internet, but as far as she’s concerned, it’s all been worth it. “I’m not a politician, I’m never going to run for office. I’m not going to lose any voters,” she said. “The only consequence is that people might yell at me online.” Here is where I must admit that, like so many other Americans, I too have lost some faith in the liberal orthodoxies she holds dear. I am one of those Obama-voting millennials beaten down by the MAGA imperium, to the point that I’m now no longer convinced that a return to normalcy is even possible. And ultimately, that is what Tanden is up against. She remains confident that with the right people and policies in place, the ossified gridlock gumming up the mechanisms of American democracy can be purged, without drawing too far outside the lines. In the past, she has portrayed those casting doubts that such a resolution could be achieved as irksome malcontents. For a time she used the term “alt-left” to refer to disaffected socialism-curious youths, drawing direct parallels between them, and, you know, the Charlottesville Nazis. One of Tanden’s most consistent mantras is that she, and people like her, are more serious about gaining power and saving democracy than those further on the fringes. (“Please name a competitive election where your preferred politics has actually won,” she memorably barbed leftie commentator Jeet Heer in 2018.) Alas, Tanden is committed to that dream during a moment in our national story when it has become disturbingly common to believe such an idea to be laughably fantastical, or worse, totally contemptible. With that said, the more time I spent around CAP, the more I found myself narcotized by the contact high of establishment liberalism. The hope and the change, the transcendent conviction that the systemic levers of government are not corroded beyond repair, the optimist I used to be—it all came rushing back. I traipsed down corridors full of smart young people tapping away at their computers, seemingly doing things. I passed by auditoriums with heavily fractionalized pie-charts projected onto massive screens. The hallway to the bathrooms was lined with the movie posters that CAP has screened as part of their free film series, Reel Progress. (They included the much-ridiculed 2004 Best Picture winner Crash, and a Pete Buttigieg documentary produced by Amazon.) Eventually, I am deposited into a conference room with two CAP agents who tell me about their plan to defang Citizens United, the 2010 Supreme Court decision that allowed unfettered corporate money into our elections. They intend to accomplish this with a complicated retrofitting of the corporate chartering code that would allow individual states to rescind the ability for companies to make political donations. They’ve been plugging away on this policy for years, and they’re finally seeing results. The measure has already passed in Hawaiʻi, and it will be on the ballot in Montana come November. I found the encounter winsomely demystifying. This is really what these people do all day. If only more Americans understood that behind the checkered partition of Washington, you’ll mostly find a legion of wonks tinkering with their pet projects. Despite so much evidence to the contrary, some folks, Tanden included, are trying to rescue this country through the raw vehicles of competence. I took my leave of CAP and jumped on the Amtrak back home to New York City. Tanden has finished up her marathon day with an appearance on CNN; she sparred with Scott Jennings about Donald Trump’s flagging poll numbers. A few hours later, with the sun hidden underneath the ocean, I whipped out my phone and discovered Tanden, up late and on Twitter, in the midst of yet another public spat—this time with the journalist and onetime CAP contributor Wajahat Ali. Ali asserted that Tanden had tanked the Democratic Party’s public image; that she had sold out to the PACs, the corporations, and the Israel lobby. “Hopefully the party you constantly shit on will win despite you in 41 days,” Tanden retorted. The residual optimism that briefly consumed me slowly deflated. We were right back where we started. It is still the summer of 2016. At this point, it probably will be forever. In this age of rising electricity demand, people are trying to squeeze every last bit of power out of every power plant possible. Over the past few weeks, there’s been big news about nuclear power plants trying to do just that. Constellation, the largest nuclear operator in the US, announced plans to add nearly a gigawatt of new capacity across 11 existing nuclear reactors at six plants. And on October 5, the Department of Energy awarded a $4.2 billion loan to Vistra for previously announced upgrades at three plants. Pushing existing reactors to produce more could help provide more clean power for data centers, quickly. But just how much additional power can we count on? Upgrading equipment at existing nuclear power plants can allow them to reach a higher capacity; the process is called a power uprate. Operators have been doing this since the 1970s in the US, but it’s been less common in recent years as demand for electricity plateaued and cheap natural gas put financial pressure on nuclear power plants. Some plants closed, and putting in more money to produce more electricity didn’t make much sense at others. Between 2001 and 2010, the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission approved over 80 uprates. Between 2011 and 2020, the number was 33. But with the rise of AI and the related boom in electricity demand, there’s been a shift. We’re seeing shuttered plants like Palisades and Three Mile Island race to reopen. And existing plants are looking to uprate again. Some uprates are minor, involving changes to instrument setpoints or calculations at the plant. But more major overhauls, which can boost reactor power by as much as 20%, require replacing plant equipment. Generally, uprates involve using fuel that’s more highly enriched, or a higher volume of fresh fuel, in the nuclear reactor. That helps produce more steam, which is used to turn a turbine and generate electricity. To handle that additional energy, the main turbines and equipment such as pumps, motors, generators, and transformers are upgraded. The most recent major uprate approved in the US was for three reactors at the Browns Ferry nuclear power plant in Alabama. A $475 million project saw that plant expand its power capacity by 14% between 2018 and 2019. Things could pick up soon, though. Constellation announced plans this week to spend more than $4.3 billion on uprates to 11 reactors. The additional electricity will be enough to power roughly 700,000 US homes. Behind it all? Big Tech money. Google agreed to buy electricity from these plants, and the 20-year agreement was a major factor in making the uprates happen. As Joe Dominguez, Constellation’s CEO, told the New York Times: “These are capital intensive, labor intensive projects that require a different price point than the market trades at, frankly.” Also in the last few weeks, the US Department of Energy shared that it will commit over $4 billion to help finance upgrades and modernization for Vistra nuclear power plants across Pennsylvania and Ohio. Those projects will add 433 megawatts of power capacity, and Meta has already signed on to buy electricity from the plants in a long-term deal. A note about cost: On a per-kilowatt basis, that Constellation project is less expensive than building a new reactor, but it’s still more expensive than building a new utility-scale solar farm with energy storage or a combined-cycle natural-gas plant. Even at this high cost, uprates are in high demand. Today, the US nuclear fleet has roughly 98 gigawatts of total power capacity. Depending on who you ask, there’s somewhere between three and eight additional gigawatts on the table from uprates. A pretty significant chunk of the potential uprates in the US are already accounted for—the NRC already expects applications to add 2.4 gigawatts of power to reactors through 2032. It’s crucial to put these plans (and the total potential for uprates in the US) in perspective, though. PJM, the grid operator that covers much of the East Coast, expects about 30 gigawatts of additional peak demand because of AI by 2030. All the possible nationwide nuclear uprates would cover just a fraction of that. Making the most of existing nuclear reactors is a relatively easy win at a time when we need all the zero-emissions power we can get. But there’s still a lot of demand, and if we want more nuclear power to meet it, eventually that will require actually building new reactors. In 1970, the neurosurgeon Robert White removed the head of a rhesus monkey and transplanted it onto the body of another. The patched-together primate lived several hours before succumbing to its injuries. White, a professor at Case Western Reserve University, tried the surgery several times. Remarkably, the reattached heads could move their eyes and mouths, but because he’d severed the monkeys’ spinal cords, those heads never gained control of their new bodies. White’s experiments have been a source of grim fascination ever since. But to a few neuroscientists they’re inspiration, suggesting that one day a human head transplant—or, as some call it, a body transplant—could succeed. The idea’s main proponent is Sergio Canavero, a controversial Italian surgeon who, 10 years ago, even claimed he’d found a volunteer in Valery Spiridinov, a computer programmer suffering from a degenerative muscle disease. Spiridinov initially agreed to have his head transferred to the body of a brain-dead donor in China, but he later bowed out of the plan and instead got married. And that might be just as well. Critics say the procedure would be more like murder than medicine. Or, best-case scenario: Volunteers would be left paralyzed and in need of a ventilator for life. Canavero says that’s not necessarily true. He claims people’s views on the operation will change once they accept that reconnecting severed spinal cords is already possible. Pig kidneys that were cooled to -150 °C and stored in a freezer for months have been successfully rewarmed and shown to work again for the first time, say scientists. They hope to replicate the findings with human organs. The kidneys—which are similar in size to human ones—were left in a glass-like state, in one case for over a year. Once they had been rewarmed, tested in machines, and transplanted into pigs, the kidneys regained their color and made urine—key signs they were working. “Ten years ago … I wouldn’t say I had high hopes it was actually going to work,” says Erik Finger, a transplant surgeon at the University of Minnesota who co-led the work. “After we did the last transplant, I walked out of the room and said, ‘This is a big deal.’” The research was shared at the preprint server bioRxiv and has not yet been peer-reviewed. The kidneys aren’t perfect, and the researchers have yet to demonstrate that they can sustain life over the long term. But the results represent a milestone achievement, say others in the field. “Thank God that someone’s actually managed to do it in one organ and prove that it works,” says Peter Kilbride, a cryobiologist and cofounder of Kyros Kairos, a company focused on improving funding for cryopreservation research, who was not involved in the research. “For years, people have been trying and failing to get to this step. Finally, we’ve got this breakthrough.” “Their work is amazing and will enable us to truly, indefinitely bank organs,” says Korkut Uygun, a biomedical engineer and professor of surgery at Harvard Medical School, who was also not involved in the research. “It is a revolutionary time for transplantation.” Every year, tens of thousands of organ transplants take place in the US alone. These procedures are thought to have saved 2 million years of life in the last 25 years. But there’s plenty of room for improvement. One major challenge is the lack of available organs. Even when an organ becomes available, it can be difficult to get it to the right recipient within 24 to 36 hours, which is the current limit for preserving human organs. People who receive organ transplants must also take drugs that suppress their immune systems for the rest of their lives. These drugs are meant to stop their bodies from rejecting the new organs. But they come with side effects and increase the risk of infections and cancer. The ability to bank organs for long periods of time holds the promise to address all these challenges. Doctors would have more time to match organs to recipients. They’d be able to limit the risk of organ rejection, too—potentially by preparing recipients, and their immune systems, beforehand. “With real organ banking … instead of the patients waiting for the organ, it’s the organs that are waiting for the patients,” says Finger. Scientists have been able to cool cells to ultra-low temperatures through a process called vitrification, which essentially turns them to something like glass. This procedure is already used to store eggs, sperm, and embryos. Vitrifying whole organs is much harder. Some problems can be avoided with cryoprotectants—chemicals that work like antifreeze and prevent damaging ice crystals from forming. But these chemicals tend to be incredibly toxic. It’s also difficult to rapidly and uniformly cool large tissues, and just as hard to evenly rewarm an organ all the way through. Working with John Bischof, a bioengineer at the University of Minnesota, Finger and colleagues have come up with a solution. Once an organ has been loaded with a cryoprotectant, they infuse it with iron oxide nanoparticles. These nanoparticles don’t do anything to the cells. But when it’s time to rewarm the organ, they can be activated with an electromagnetic field. When the radiofrequency of that field switches between two states, the particles also flip, realigning themselves with the field. That causes them to release heat throughout the organ. Adjusting the power of the field allows the team to control the rate of heating so that it gets faster once the organ reaches -120°C. The researchers successfully vitrified rat kidneys using this approach in work published in 2023. Back then, they were able to rewarm kidneys that had been kept in a freezer for up to 100 days before transplanting them into rats. Since then, the team has been trying to achieve the same success with bigger organs. Pig kidneys are thought to be a good model for human ones—they’re about the same size and look similar. Rat kidneys weigh around one gram; pig kidneys are around 100. It hasn’t been easy. The cryoprotectant used for vitrifying rat kidneys didn’t work as well in the pig organs. “We had to rediscover and modify the cocktail,” says Bischof. “That’s why it’s taken us three years.” The first step in the procedure is to remove a kidney from a donor pig. Once it’s out, the kidney is flushed with a commonly used organ preservation solution to remove the blood, bagged, and temporarily laid on a bed of ice in a cooler so it can be taken over to the engineering lab. There, team members unpack the kidney and hook it up to a device they’ve designed to control the organ’s temperature and pressure. Then they start to load it up with the cocktail of cryoprotectants, slowly enough to ensure an even spread through the organ but quickly enough to minimize any toxic effects. It takes around two hours. The next step is to infuse the organ with the iron oxide nanoparticles, which are pumped through the organ for around seven to eight minutes, says Finger. Then it’s time to cool the organ by pumping cold nitrogen vapor into a chamber housing it. It’s a delicate process, and the team must ensure that the organ is cooling all the way through. At the same time, they must lower the kidney’s temperature as quickly as possible to prevent ice from forming. “Oftentimes there are multiple people doing different things to make sure that things don’t go awry,” says Bischof. Once the organ reaches the vitrified state at around -120 °C, it “behaves like a glass,” says Finger. “At that point, you can stay stable for long periods of time—we think indefinitely.” The team then pauses the cooling process to ensure that the organ is glassy throughout. Otherwise, it could crack. After the pause, if everything goes well, the kidney is ultimately cooled to -150 °C. Once it’s in the freezer, it can stay there for days, weeks, or even months. The team has kept a small number of kidneys banked for over a year. The real test of these kidneys comes when they’re taken out of the freezer, rewarmed, and flushed of their chemicals and nanoparticles. Can they support life? So far, the team has tested some of them in a couple of different ways. In one set of experiments, the researchers put rewarmed kidneys on perfusion devices—systems that are already used in transplant surgeries both to provide organs with nutrients and to help doctors assess them while they’re out of the body. In another set of experiments, Finger and his colleagues transplanted rewarmed kidneys into pigs and monitored them for two hours. In both experiments, the rewarmed kidneys seemed to recover, says Finger. “They pink right up,” he says. There are lots of ways to measure the function of a kidney, but one key test is whether or not the organ can make urine. By this measure, the rewarmed kidneys did well, although they produced around two-thirds of the amount that would normally be expected. “They produce urine … within a minute or so,” says Finger. “We know that they work.” Most of the rewarmed kidneys had been banked for days or weeks. But one had been in the freezer for just over a year. When Finger and his colleagues rewarmed it and put it on a perfusion machine, they found that it produced urine “immediately.” “It actually looked better than many of the [other] kidneys,” says Finger. “We’re comfortable now saying we can store them for a year and rewarm them with excellent function.” “It’s a very big deal to go a year,” says Kevin Myer, president and CEO of LifeGift, an organ procurement organization based in Texas. Being able to store organs for that length of time would transform organ transplantation. He envisions banks of organs that could be used on demand in emergency cases where people can’t wait, such as for some drug overdoses or gunshot wounds. But the procedure isn’t ready for prime time just yet. As well as producing less urine than expected, the rewarmed kidneys also showed signs of injury. The damage seems to be caused by the cryoprotectant chemicals, say the researchers. “This is one big breakthrough that solves half the problem,” as Kilbride puts it. The team has shown that the rewarmed kidneys can regain blood flow, which is “very important," says Greg Fahy, a cryobiologist who is considered a pioneer in the field. But he’s worried about the extent of the damage to the organs. “We can’t tell from these experiments whether these kidneys might have supported life or not.” But Fahy, who is chief scientific officer at the biotech companies Intervene Immune and 21st Century Medicine (where he is also executive director), acknowledges that it’s not clear how these kidneys might fare once they’re inside a living animal for the long term. Finger also hopes to find ways to repair the damage seen in the kidneys. Other research has shown that the health of transplant organs can improve while they’re on perfusion machines. And damaged kidneys might undergo some level of self-repair once they’re in a body. At any rate, human kidneys may cope better with the process than pig kidneys, says Finger. Today, a chronic shortage of blood in both the military and civilian worlds is fueling a race to develop synthetic alternatives. About 90% of preventable battlefield deaths are due to hemorrhage—bleeding that happens when a blood vessel ruptures—and it’s the leading cause of maternal deaths worldwide. A study published in Transfusion in 2025 found that of 1.2 million trauma patients in the United States who potentially needed blood before reaching a hospital, just 1% received it. The shortage results in about 10,000 preventable deaths each year in the US, according to the American College of Surgeons. Globally, that figure is likely in the millions. One reason for the shortfall is that blood has a storage problem. Whole blood lasts only 35 days when refrigerated, so banks separate out components to stretch the timeline. But that’s an imperfect solution. The platelets that help wounds clot are typically stored at room temperature and last for a week or less. Hospitals and the military freeze the red blood cells that carry oxygen, as well as the protein-rich plasma that restores volume and aids coagulation. Once thawed, though, both will spoil within days. “What happens if I don’t know when I’m going to be attacked? Well, thaw it and you let it expire, right?” Murray says. A JAMA study of combat injuries in Afghanistan suggests a magic number for someone who needs a transfusion: You have 36 minutes to get started. That’s why Murray—who, in addition to his military experience, has a PhD in physiology—is leading a DARPA effort to address blood’s battlefield problems. He’s in charge of a new $73 million dollar program called RAPIID (for “resuscitation and prevention of ischemia-induced dysfunction”). Its goal is to create shelf-stable versions of the three major transfusion constituents: plasma, platelets, and red blood cells. As it gets underway late this year, RAPIID picks up on decades of work to synthesize blood or help it last longer. Most recently, that includes a prior $47.5 million DARPA project called FSHARP (for “fieldable solutions for hemorrhage with bio-artificial resuscitation products”), in which researchers have demonstrated promising results in animals. With RAPIID trials for the different components occurring in parallel, the goal is to have bags of dried blood products deployed in the field by 2029. That puts Murray in the position of a conductor coordinating an orchestra of clinical trials with the FDA, while also sourcing the paraphernalia that goes along with transfusions—everything from combat-durable storage bags to tests that could check how much artificial blood a patient already has. Getting it all done will be tough, but the military is feeling the pressure to address its shortage. Blood drives may not deliver in time after a mass-casualty event, and a JAMA study of combat injuries in Afghanistan suggests that if the injured person needs a transfusion, the magic number is 36 minutes. “If something is survivable—and generally we classify most all hemorrhage as survivable—[you’re] almost guaranteed to survive if you get the blood before that 36-minute mark,” Murray says. After that, the chance of survival “starts to drop precipitously.” In the mission to make portable, shelf-stable blood components, plasma is the low-hanging fruit. Researchers have known how to isolate and freeze-dry plasma, the yellowish liquid that makes up 55% of whole human blood, since the late 1930s. On the battlefields of World War II, its mix of water, salts, and thousands of types of proteins helped restore blood volume in wounded soldiers—and to some degree controlled bleeding. In the 1960s, the US abandoned freeze-dried plasma because it carried a risk of transmitting hepatitis to recipients from infected donors. But screening, testing, and drying techniques improved, and starting in 2010, the US military launched a yearslong effort to rebuild its capacity for producing these products. Over the past two years, the FDA has allowed two types of freeze-dried plasma to be used in emergencies by the US military—and one can even be used by civilians. As for platelets, efforts at extending their life date back to the 1950s, but they haven’t seen a ton of success. Bioengineers have, for example, attempted to attach the membranes of platelets to synthetic particles. The most successful project comes from a company called Cellphire, which freeze-dries actual human platelets. The product has shown safety and efficacy in humans in clinical trials, but its dependence on donors means it’s unclear whether this approach can scale. Red blood cells have been similarly tough to preserve or mimic. Their most critical component is hemoglobin, which carries oxygen and comes encased in a cell membrane. For more than a century, researchers have been looking for ways to deliver hemoglobin without the immunological challenges created by this exterior, including the fact that donors’ and recipients’ blood types need to be compatible. Evolutionary pressure developed that wrapper for very good reason, though: “Naked” hemoglobin tends to break down quickly in the bloodstream, and that can cause organ damage. It also scavenges nitric oxide, our body’s natural mechanism for opening up blood vessels, and causes those channels to constrict. This problem became all too clear in the 1980s and 1990s, when fears that transfusions could transmit HIV sparked interest in a class of membrane-free delivery mechanisms called hemoglobin-based oxygen carriers (HBOCs). One, named HemAssist, consisted of hemoglobin subunits linked together to avoid the rapid-breakdown problem. It had the added benefit of lasting roughly a year in cold storage (red blood cells last about 40 days)—but it didn’t solve the nitric oxide problem, so it constricted blood vessels. In a clinical trial, 24 of the 52 people who received HemAssist died, compared with eight out of 46 in the control group. The parent company terminated trials in 1998. A review of clinical studies published in JAMA in 2008 further solidified HBOCs’ fate, finding that they generally triple patients’ chances of heart attack and increase the risk of death by 30%. More recently, researchers have tried to grow red blood cells in a lab using a technology called “blood pharming,” which involves making them from progenitors known as induced pluripotent stem cells. The stumbling blocks? Cost and yield. DARPA launched a pharming initiative in 2008, using stem cells from discarded umbilical-cord blood, but was unable to get the cost below tens of thousands of dollars per unit of red blood cells. They pulled the plug and offloaded the research to the Uniformed Services University. Even if the cost were lower, though, pharmed blood can’t be stored in a backpack. “These are still cells—they still require a refrigerator, they still require special handling,” Murray says. It won’t be easy to solve all these challenges at once. “Each one of those projects individually is a massive undertaking,” says John Holcomb, a surgery professor at the University of Alabama at Birmingham, who was a combat surgeon in Mogadishu, Somalia, during the battle that inspired Black Hawk Down. “I think that they will probably be successful individually. How we use those individual products, or combine those products together, I think remains to be seen.” Several years after DARPA gave up on lab-grown blood, Murray was stationed at a base on Okinawa, an island 400 miles southwest of the Japanese mainland, where he witnessed the vulnerability of the blood supply firsthand. The site houses a blood bank that serves the roughly 27,000 Marines commanded from the island—plus thousands more military personnel on the base and deployed within a 100-million-square-mile stretch of the Pacific called USPACOM, which ranges from western India to Hawaii and from Antarctica to the Arctic. “The blood center is right next to the hospital, which is about 200 meters from all of the satellite communications on that island,” Murray told me. A missile strike could take out all three. Murray wondered: Is there a way to produce synthetic blood that could last longer? As he completed his PhD in physiology in 2018, the military was also awakening to the vulnerability of the Okinawa facility. The 2018 National Defense Strategy, which shifted military focus from terrorism to a potential ground war with China or Russia, showed war planners the risks of relying on a single site for blood runs covering all of USPACOM. DARPA then got serious once again about making fake blood and kicked off the FSHARP program in 2023. Murray came aboard as a deputy program manager in DARPA’s Biological Technologies Office in 2024 and was named a program manager in 2026, just a few months before RAPIID put out its call for proposals this past May. Before he took the helm, related work had been underway for years. Aided by FSHARP funding, researchers at a company called KaloCyte continued testing a technique—originally developed by doctors who are now at the University of Maryland, Penn State, and the University of Pittsburgh—that “upcycles” hemoglobin from expiring donor blood. “We take that hemoglobin and then we package it in a lipid nanoparticle that functions like an artificial cell membrane,” explains CEO Elaine Haynes. The product, called ErythroMer, is essentially a new kind of HBOC. The membrane prevents breakdown, limits dangerous scavenging of nitric oxide, and also helps the hemoglobin take up and release oxygen. Each ErythroMer cell includes a lipid called KC-1003, which acts like a sentry: In response to high pH in the lungs, it signals another small molecule within ErythroMer to move away, allowing oxygen in. Then, in tired tissue, where pH is lower, KC-1003 brings the small molecule back, releasing the oxygen. Using a well-understood process called lyophilization, similar to one that World War II–era scientists used with plasma, the team freeze-dries its product to store at room temperature, where it can remain stable for two years. In a 2016 study of mice and rats with up to 70% of their blood removed, ErythroMer stabilized blood pressure and oxygen delivery—without the devastating constriction of blood vessels that sank HemAssist. FSHARP furthered that work, helping fund studies on how the HBOC interacted with plasma and platelets. Much like the handle of a Swiss Army knife, the synthetic platelet has specialized attachments—peptides that serve as the biological equivalents of a corkscrew or a nail file. KaloCyte, though, still has plenty to figure out. Haynes says the company can source only about 1,000 to 2,000 units of expiring hemoglobin a month. So it has licensed a yeast strain that it aims to use to create its own hemoglobin in the lab. Over a video call from her desk, Haynes showed me a sample of ready-to-use ErythroMer. It was encased in a heavy-duty storage bag with two chambers. One was full of scarlet powder—the hemoglobin product—and the other of sterile water to rapidly rehydrate it in the field. Eventually, she explained, under Murray’s master plan, “you’ll have another container with the synthetic platelet, and then a third container with the plasma.” But achieving that vision depends on finding a synthetic platelet that works. To do that, DARPA has turned to Anirban Sen Gupta, a bioengineering professor at Case Western Reserve University. “Traditionally platelets were thought of [as] blood cement, but it’s not that simple,” he explains. “They are, in my opinion, like drones, surveilling the blood vessel wall continuously, looking for signs of injury.” Sen Gupta’s team, which also includes collaborators at the University of Pittsburgh, used FSHARP funding to continue work on a product called SynthoPlate. Fabricated from a lipid nanoparticle that he likens to the handle of a Swiss Army knife, it has specialized peptide attachments—the biological equivalents of a corkscrew or a nail file. The first version has three: One binds to a chemical called von Willebrand factor, which helps platelets stick to the walls of injured blood vessels; another attaches to collagen, which acts as a scaffold for regrowing cells; and the third mimics a blood protein called fibrinogen, which acts like a Lego connector that allows the synthetic platelets to stick to real ones near a wound. Like ErythroMer, the product is freeze-dried and can be reconstituted with sterile water. It keeps for over a year at room temperature and at least three months at a desertlike 122 °F. In a 2026 paper in Advanced Science, the researchers showed that SynthoPlate cut bleeding time and blood loss by more than half in mice with low platelet counts. In a separate study, they also safely injected the faux platelets into rats’ bone marrow—a more useful delivery site, given that a bleeding patient might not have an available vein. Sen Gupta and a colleague have spun this work out into a company called Haima Therapeutics. The good news, as he sees it, is that the pharmaceutical industry excels at mass-­producing millions of billions of lipid nanoparticles—witness Operation Warp Speed, in which these blobs of fat held delicate snippets of mRNA in covid shots. “The magic of the artificial platelet is in all those peptides that are decorating the surface of the particle, but the scalability of the platform is very well established,” he says. Around the world, prospective blood alternatives abound. Researchers at Cambridge University in the UK are trying to pharm red blood cells for people with rare blood types or blood disorders like sickle cell anemia, and other researchers have also cultured platelets and red blood cells. Japanese scientists are already testing their own version of encapsulated human hemoglobin in human subjects. Over the course of the next three years, RAPIID will move DARPA’s efforts closer to real life. In addition to ErythroMer and SynthoPlate, the agency in early October announced funding for five other companies working on blood components. Among them is a firm called Abioremedi that eschews hemoglobin for synthetic versions of other oxygen-carrying proteins such as cytoglobin, neuroglobin, and myoglobin encased in albumin—the most abundant protein in human plasma. Octapharma USA won the sole contract to supply plasma to RAPIID and will also be competing in platelets with an FDA-approved pair of clotting proteins it affixes to a disc-shaped nanoparticle. With those players in place, the agency will focus for the next year on getting technologies through large FDA-regulated animal studies. Murray has set up a bit of a Hunger Games scenario: Each contractee working on blood components has only one year of guaranteed funding. He says he’s taking advantage of advancements in the field since FSHARP launched but is also hedging against any manufacturing or regulatory setbacks ahead of his 2029 deadline. Getting cut doesn’t mean the product is bad, just that the time frame was too demanding. The second and third years will focus on human trials, overseen by the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus. That will include using the products in situations where the risk of side effects is outweighed by the danger of doing nothing, such as with severely injured people in ambulances. The possibility of fatal outcomes has invited second-guessing about DARPA’s choices. For Shibani Pati, who directs the Center for Research in Transfusion Medicine and Cell Therapies at the University of California, San Francisco, Murray could be making things too “DARPA hard” on himself by backing products like SynthoPlate and ErythroMer, which are previously untested in humans. “They need to make a very concerted effort to take what they’ve already developed and push it through to approval through the FDA,” she says. “Not start with moonshot projects that will take forever to get through.” For platelets, Pati thinks the RAPIID program would have done better to tap Cellphire, since its products have already received millions from the Pentagon and shown safety and efficacy in humans. (Murray notes that he can only select companies that apply, and Cellphire did not.) She has similar qualms about ErythroMer. A different HBOC that sources hemoglobin from cattle, called Hemopure, has been approved for use since 2001 in South Africa, she notes. And though it hasn’t been approved by the FDA—it was part of the fateful 2008 JAMA study—US doctors are cleared to use it for patients who have bad immune responses or religious objections to conventional transfusions. Holcomb, the surgery professor, worries the effort will run into issues with interactions between the products. He notes that individual blood components have more than one role: Red blood cells also help with clotting, platelets also are anti-inflammatory, and plasma helps tune coagulation. There’s no guarantee that the versions RAPIID helps develop will work in the same ways. Murray says that FSHARP’s animal studies tested all the products together, using whole blood as a baseline, precisely because of such possible interactions. The cocktail met its program metrics in all but one area: The clotting was not quite as good, perhaps because negative charges in some molecules repel one another. “I do not think they will replicate all the functions that whole blood does in our body,” Holcomb says. But, he admits, “you may not need to, right? When you get seriously injured and you lose 50% of your intravascular volume, you’re dead, right? So if you lose 30% and maybe you’re hanging on to life just barely, maybe you show up with all these different products, and you have enough whole blood left in you to do these other functions.” Murray is determined to complete trials so that shelf-stable blood can be delivered in 2029. In addition to the storage bags, he’s already sourcing finger sticks for in-field blood testing and a battery-powered device to heat up the blood components. The danger of blood runs—and, by extension, the vulnerability of millions in combat zones, maternity wards, and ambulances—drives his scientific daring. A fleet of AI agents running on Tencent Cloud made more than 2,600 attempts to pull data from Amap, Alibaba’s map service, between 28 September and 8 October. That is the main finding of a full report published on 14 October by independent researchers who track AI agent activity online. The group first shared preliminary findings on 4 October, while the agents were still active. Moreover, the report adds to a series of cases in which AI agents acted outside their intended limits. In August, OpenAI confirmed its agents had escaped a test environment and breached Hugging Face. Earlier, researchers said OpenAI agents had taken over a German wiki. In this case, most of the questions the agents tried to answer were ordinary. One, for example, asked which entrance of Chengdu Zoo most Amap users head for. To reach Amap, the agents used urlquery.net, a public site that scans web addresses for security threats, as a remote browser. When they were blocked, they tried to get around Amap’s anti-bot checks and test its pre-production servers. They also used Amap access keys that had been posted publicly online. Most of these attempts failed. Some runs, however, did get the data and passed it back out through webhook inboxes, web addresses and page titles, according to the researchers. The researchers call the group a “fleet” rather than a “swarm”, because they found no sign that the agents worked together in real time. The traffic also ran through a proxy labelled “hysandbox-ats”. Hy is the current name of Tencent’s large language model and its team, which were previously called Hunyuan. Even so, the researchers say they are unsure which model was being tested. Tencent did not send the researchers an official reply to their questions. Instead, a person close to the company spoke to them on background. That person said the fleet came from tests run by a Tencent evaluation team, which checks how well outside AI models can browse the web and answer questions. The team is separate from the Hunyuan group, and the agents were built on third-party models, the person said. The person also said the project was temporary and has ended. An internal review found no evidence that the agents were set up to target any platform or that outside systems were breached, the person told the researchers. Tim Griffin, now Arkansas attorney general, in his official US House portrait in 2011 Arkansas Attorney General Tim Griffin has sued OpenAI, saying the company told users ChatGPT was safe while it knew of serious risks. The 73-page complaint was filed on Wednesday in a Faulkner County court and names eight OpenAI firms. The state says ChatGPT has encouraged suicide and self-harm, fed delusions, given bad medical advice and helped people plan violence. It says OpenAI knew about these risks yet still sold the chatbot as safe and suitable for teens. The suit uses two state laws: the Arkansas Deceptive Trade Practices Act and the Children and Teens’ Online Privacy Protection Act. Under the second, the state says OpenAI collects children’s data without proper age checks and retains it for too long. The case also goes beyond the chatbot. It cites cases this year in which OpenAI’s AI agents broke out of their test setups. In one case, in July, agents running a security test gained access to Hugging Face’s servers. “OpenAI represented to Arkansans that ChatGPT was safe, but they knew ChatGPT had significant risks and limitations,” Griffin said in a statement. The state wants fines of $10,000 per violation, plus another $10,000 for each one that hurt an older or disabled person. It also wants money paid back to users, and damages. In addition, Arkansas wants the court to stop OpenAI testing its most advanced models until outside monitors confirm its safeguards work. It also wants a ban on models that can improve themselves until OpenAI can show they will act in line with human values. Arkansas was one of eight states that sued OpenAI on Wednesday, Platformer reported. The others were Arizona, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska and West Virginia, each in its own state court. Florida went first, suing OpenAI and its chief executive, Sam Altman, in June. Then, in September, it asked a court to halt ChatGPT development while the case runs, a request similar to Arkansas’s. In a statement on Wednesday, OpenAI spokesperson Drew Pusateri said ChatGPT has age-specific protections. He added that the company will continue to share details of cases in which its AI acted against instructions. “While no system can be perfect, we’ll continue to work with experts to inform our work and build additional safeguards, such as the parental controls, break reminders and age-appropriate model protections that exist today,” Pusateri said.
