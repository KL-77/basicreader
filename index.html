FCC chair warns media not to run the "fake polls" Trump keeps complaining about

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-21

Visit Original Website
Federal Communications Commission Chairman Brendan Carr today warned broadcasters about airing “fake polls,” amplifying one of the many complaints that President Trump has regularly made about the news media. Carr’s post on X said, “Many journalists and broadcasters have been amplifying or airing these Polls without regard to basic journalistic ethics and without informing the public that those Polls lack a scientific, accurate, or even real basis… Broadcasters are advised to avoid engaging in News Distortions or running Broadcast Hoaxes by airing Polls that would violate their regulatory obligations.” Carr urged broadcasters to “provide their viewers with additional context and information about Polls they have covered.” In fact, one small polling outfit was exposed as a hoax this week, and a similar incident occurred in August. News outlets have reported on these hoaxes, providing the very “context” that Carr said is needed. Carr’s post did not mention any specific fake polls or any specific broadcasters that aired them. The vagueness of Carr’s post may be useful for his and Trump’s ongoing battle against the news media. In August, Trump demanded that the FCC punish or rebuke an NBC journalist for saying he has “mixed results” in election endorsements, and wrote that the FCC must do something about “THE FAKE POLLS USED BY OUR CROOKED MEDIA.” Carr’s latest warning to broadcasters came as no surprise, given that he has spent much of the past two years threatening broadcast licenses and opening investigations into Trump’s least favorite news companies. Former Republican and Democratic FCC chairs and commissioners recently said Carr is seeking to censor and chill the speech of broadcasters through a proceeding that threatens the licenses of ABC-owned stations. “FCC Chair Brendan Carr’s threat against broadcasters over their reporting on political polls is yet another truly outrageous attempt to censor and control the information available to Americans as they prepare to vote… Carr is now seizing on reports about fabricated political polling as an excuse to once again try to scare broadcasters into self-censorship,” said a statement today from a group of former FCC chairs and officials. Today’s statement was attributed to former Chairman Tom Wheeler, a Democrat; former Chairman Mark Fowler, a Republican; and seven former high-ranking FCC officials. “Good-faith reporting on public polls—even ones that turn out to be made up—is not a violation of the FCC’s news distortion or hoax rules. And the FCC has zero authority to dictate editorial decisions,” they said. Wheeler, Fowler, and others previously signed a letter objecting to the ABC investigation. The letter called it “the latest attack in a sustained campaign by Chairman Carr and the Trump administration of threatening legal sanctions against licensees whose speech President Trump dislikes.” Anna Gomez, the only Democratic commissioner on the current FCC, wrote today that “Pollsters who intentionally mislead the public should answer for it, but broadcasters who report publicly available polling data should never be punished when a poll is wrong. FCC rules on news distortion or hoaxes do not apply to a station that reports this data in good faith.” An entity called Wedgewood Polls said yesterday that it published fabricated polling data “to test whether media outlets, aggregators, and commentators would report on data without verifying the source, methodology, or underlying firm legitimacy.” It claimed to be running an experiment on the modern political polling industry. “Despite having no history, formal credentials, or real data behind the vast majority of our releases, Wedgewood Polling was added to major polling aggregators, including The New York Times, FiftyPlusOne, and VoteHub, and repeatedly treated as a legitimate news source,” the statement said. Notably, the three polling aggregators mentioned in the statement are not broadcast stations or news networks whose programming is carried on local broadcast stations. That’s relevant because Carr has repeatedly said he merely uses the FCC’s regulatory authority over broadcasters with licenses to use public spectrum, and not media outlets that do not have FCC licenses. Carr’s post yesterday warned broadcasters to “comply with their public interest obligations” under “the terms of their federal licenses.” While Wedgewood Polls claimed that its data faced no scrutiny from news organizations, FiftyPlusOne said it was preparing to publish an article exposing the fraud and had sent a request for comment to Wedgewood. “They did not respond to our request, but instead posted a public statement on social media admitting that they had been fabricating data,” FiftyPlusOne wrote. FiftyPlusOne, The New York Times, and VoteHub all say they did some vetting of Wedgewood before deciding to use its polls. FiftyPlusOne wrote: Their statement alleges that FiftyPlusOne, The New York Times, and other poll aggregators did not conduct appropriate vetting of their firm prior to aggregating their polls; this is false. We reached out to them to ask who they were and confirm the methodology statement on their website. We were given two names, Jack Sims and Andrew Prater. We were able to independently confirm that Sims had previous experience in the polling industry and reviewed their methodology statement in detail. It is not atypical for a pollster to leave an existing firm, form their own, and self-fund some surveys to build reputation; we had no reason at the time to believe this case was any different. Wedgewood operated as if it were a legitimate polling firm, including collecting money from donors to conduct crowd-funded polls, and sharing raw survey data (which we now have confirmed was fraudulent) with FiftyPlusOne on Oct. 15, 2026. VoteHub Head of Data Science Zachary Donnini said that after talking to Wedgewood’s “head pollster,” Votehub “assigned them a D grade and included their polling data on our site.” Meanwhile, the New York Times updated its polling page with a statement about Wedgewood. The New York Times said it sends all new pollsters “a series of questions regarding the identities of the pollsters; the methods they were using to draw their sample, contact respondents and weight their polls; and the source of their funding.” Wedgewood answered the questions, and the NYT used 15 of its polls for state and congressional-district races. The Wedgewood “polls were never included in the tracker or average of President Trump’s approval rating or of the national polls tracking overall preference for control of Congress,” the NYT said. In the previous incident this year, a group called Median Strategies admitted it faked poll results as part of a “social experiment.” The NYT said it never included that group’s results in its trackers “because the group did not respond to our questions about its methodology.” Nate Silver removed Wedgewood from his polling site today. Wedgewood polls tended to be about 1 percentage point more favorable for Democrats than other polls, according to Silver.

Xbox wants you to pay 24 to 40 cents per hour to stream games you already bought

In September, when Microsoft severely limited the game-streaming buffet available to Game Pass subscribers, the company said non-subscribers would soon be able to “purchase cloud playtime hours through the Xbox Store and stream eligible games they own on supported devices.” Today, Microsoft rolled out those a la carte Cloud Playtime purchases to Xbox Insiders, with prices that might be a bit galling for players who have already purchased these streamable games. The “introductory launch pricing” for Xbox’s Cloud Playtime purchases is as follows: Purchased playtime “remains available until it’s used,” Microsoft writes, so you don’t have to worry about your 50-hour purchase expiring before you can use it fully. Paying for playtime directly like this evokes images of old arcades, where players would invest a quarter or two (in 20th century dollars) often for just a few minutes of gameplay. That kind of metered play has fallen heavily out of fashion, though, in favor of one-time purchases for an all-you-can-play experience at home. On the surface, paying as low as 24 cents for an hour of streaming game time doesn’t sound completely unreasonable. Remember, though, that these streaming costs come on top of whatever you paid to buy the game in the first place. Unlike an Xbox Game Pass subscription—which includes a few hours of monthly streaming access to hundreds of games as part of the subscription price—these Cloud Playtime packs “require cloud playable game purchase or, for select games, free-to-play access,” Microsoft writes. Say what you will about the old arcade model, but you didn’t have to buy the cabinet and then pay a quarter per play. Microsoft’s metered Cloud Playtime packs differ from Google’s failed Stadia streaming experiment, which offered unlimited streaming playtime across devices for games you purchased on the platform. Of course, Stadia game purchases also didn’t let you download games for local play on a PC or console, making the overall value proposition quite different. That was especially true after Stadia’s 2022 shutdown made those purchases unplayable (though Google offered full refunds for any and all Stadia purchases at that point). In 2014, Sony offered a la carte streaming game access for set rental periods as part of its new PlayStation Now service. While that system didn’t require up-front game purchases, the initial pricing was exorbitant, rising to $30 for 90 days of real-time streaming access, even for games that could be purchased outright on disc for less. Those early streaming price experiments quickly gave way to an all-you-can-stream PlayStation Now subscription plan, which has now morphed into unlimited streaming of select PlayStation games through the top-tier, $20/month (or $160/year) PlayStation Plus Premium plan. Microsoft is also reminding cloud-curious gamers that Xbox Insiders can test free, ad-supported streams of games they own without buying any playtime packs. That might be the best option for gamers who want to test out a quick Halo session on their phone, rather than maintaining regular streaming access to their Xbox libraries. But Microsoft also says that paid users will get “shorter wait times [and] high-quality streaming,” meaning a deteriorated experience for those who use the ad-supported tier. In the end, it’s a question of how much of a mass market there is for Xbox Cloud Streaming on its own, rather than as an outgrowth of Xbox Game Pass subscriptions. Last month, Microsoft said that only 4 percent of Game Pass subscribers would be affected by the subscription’s new limits of five to 15 hours of streaming playtime per month. That suggests only a small minority of Xbox users want to use cloud streaming for a significant portion of their gaming time, even when it was included as a free perk with their existing subscription. How much less interest will there be among non-subscribers who have to pay by the hour for streaming access to games they already own?

Enterprises keep buying software upgrades that IT thinks will make things worse

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-21

Visit Original Website
Enterprise-sized businesses spend, on average, 28 percent of their software budgets on unwanted upgrades, migrations, and licensing changes. Often, those firms’ IT heads think those changes are detrimental. Those results are according to a survey of 2,018 chief information officers and senior IT decision-makers at companies with at least 10,000 employees that Origina commissioned for its report, “The Software Tax.” About half of the respondents (1,011) are from the US, and the rest are from the United Kingdom. All respondents worked at companies with at least $1 billion in annual revenue. Keep in mind that Origina provides maintenance for various enterprise software. Its report points to independent software maintenance as a way for IT leaders to regain control over software budgets, illustrating its potential bias. A third-party research consultancy, Censuswide, conducted the survey. Among the report’s most concerning findings, enterprises often make software purchases that IT heads believe could hurt the organization. Ninety-five percent of respondents said “their organization has paid for a vendor-driven upgrade, migration, or licensing change in the past three years that it believed at the time would reduce performance, reliability, or business value,” the report says. Fifty-three percent of respondents “have done so more than once,” the report notes. “Some 76 percent say their organization has very little real leverage in negotiations, and 80 percent say moving away from major vendors would cause significant disruption,” the report says, referring to the share of respondents who selected “strongly agree” or “somewhat agree” to those statements. “When the alternative to an unwanted upgrade is losing support on systems the business runs on, saying yes is often a rational response,” Tomás O’Leary, one of the report’s authors and Origina’s CEO and founder, said in a statement. “That’s exactly the problem. The market has left some of the most experienced technology leaders in the world with little room to refuse, and that cost shows up in budgets, in engineering time, and in innovation that never gets funded.” The result is less funding for other areas, particularly, per the report, innovation (named by 36 percent of respondents), human resources (32 percent), security (31 percent), and “customer-facing investment” (31 percent). In a statement accompanying the survey’s announcement, Liberty Mutual’s CIO, James McGlennon, pointed out that an upgrade cycle could occupy top technical workers for “the better part of a year.” “That’s not a one-off cost, it’s a recurring drag on capacity, and every year it repeats is a year those people aren’t working on the initiatives that actually differentiate the business,” he said. The majority (91 percent) surveyed would support laws that give enterprises “protections comparable with those available to long-term residential tenants.” Only 1 percent opposed this (with the rest signaling that they neither support nor oppose the idea). CIOs should “negotiate the exit before the entry” to have more control over software budgets, Marshall Van Alstyne, the report’s co-author and an information sciences professor at Boston University, said in a statement. Origina’s report suggests “settling the terms of a potential exit,” including setting a price for migration assistance and caps on renewal and expansion prices, during initial negotiations with the software vendor. Origina’s report didn’t call out any specific vendors as forcing enterprises’ hands. However, every survey respondent currently runs “at least one of IBM, HCL, VMware/Broadcom, or SAP at meaningful scale.”

Tesla profits drop 43% in Q3

Tesla published its third-quarter 2026 financial results this afternoon. Earlier in October, it reported its sales and deliveries for the three-month period, selling fewer cars than in the same quarter in 2025, yet it still beat analyst projections. Now we can see how those sales translate to the bottom line. Tesla brought in $28 billion in revenue in Q3, almost the same as in 2025. Automotive revenues were $20.6 billion, a 3 percent decrease year over year. Perhaps more troubling is the decline in revenue from solar panels and storage batteries—this line of business had been growing, but for Q3 revenue fell 16 percent year over year to $2.9 billion. Services and other revenues, though, which include parts and Supercharger use, grew by a third to $4.6 billion. But the company—like the rest of us—has seen its expenses go up. Operating expenses rose by 39 percent to $4.8 billion, dropping the company’s operating margin to just 2.6 percent; in Q3 2025, Tesla’s margin was 5.8 percent. Net profit fell to $787 million, down 43 percent year over year. What’s more, capital expenditures were up by 110 percent to $4.7 billion, and free cash flow was down 62 percent to $1.5 billion. Tesla can no longer rely on selling regulatory credits to other automakers: these fell to $113 million this quarter, down from $417 million in Q3 2025. Now, virtually every other automaker has a portfolio of electric vehicles and no longer needs to offset its emissions by pooling with Tesla. In the US, Tesla CEO Elon Musk’s support helped elect a president who has abolished such credits entirely, with Musk’s blessing. Tesla’s letter to investors says its flat revenues were due to lower average selling prices, selling fewer cars, not receiving as much in credits, and also the decline of its energy storage business. Together with the reduced credits and battery and solar sales, a big jump in operating expenses is responsible for the diminishing profitability; some of this is Tesla spending rampantly on AI hardware, but some is also the CEO’s extravagant compensation package.

The new human-crafted Apple Music brand matters in an AI world

Apple just revealed a new brand identity for Apple Music, with the expected turn to Liquid Glass. I’m not much of a fan of their new design language in general; this is where I admit that I’m still running MacOS Sequoia and have grumpy things to say about rounded corner radii and text legibility. It’s just a trend, it will pass, and at some point I’ll probably cave and upgrade when it starts actually affecting my ability to get work done, or more importantly get security updates. Once I adjust, I’ll stop noticing it, and life will go on. As such, I wouldn’t normally find an obvious rebrand like this notable, much less feel like I had much to say about it. But then I happened to catch Apple’s behind-the-scenes video in my YouTube recommendations, and I realized I did have thoughts. It’s a short clip, respecting your time at just a little over a minute long if you want to watch for yourself. Let’s get the obvious part out of the way: It’s pretty peak Apple. More than a little pretentious, with all the resources a $5 trillion market cap can bring to bear on every problem. There’s a design agency (TBWA/Media Arts Lab), a pop hitmaker music producer (Fred again.. and yes, that’s how you spell it), a whole crewed set with cameras and lighting, and a mineral oil tank equipped with a transducer to effectively transmit audio signals into physical ripples. They shot a ton of footage of the oil (chosen for its higher viscosity) interacting with the branding printed on transparent panels. Fast Company has a nice detailed breakdown of the technical aspects if you want to dig in more. It’s a little eye-rolly with the narration, but honestly also lovely in the execution and care. And it made me realize how much I think we’re all going to value this kind of hands-on, human crafting in an era where anything can be generated out of a prompt. Like many Ars readers, I have feelings about generative AI imagery. It’s feeling increasingly impossible to avoid in daily life. My new favorite term is “slop ghouls.” One really unfortunate side effect of that persistence is a new natural tendency to be suspicious of everything you see. Was that picture you just saw hand-drawn, or did someone prompt it into existence with styles stolen from human artists? I see us increasingly place genuine value on not just human-created craft, but seeing the process behind it that shows the hands at work. This Apple video was a neat bit of promotion, but it also provided the receipts. Real people did real things in the physical world to make the final images and videos. The final effect is obviously cleaned up and post-processed. But when I look at it now, I don’t just see Liquid Glass; I see the humans and their time and energy and creativity. To me, at least, that matters. Showing your work, proving the time you put in—I appreciate it. I’m slightly less grumpy for it! Generative AI is likely here to stay. There’s no stuffing the genie back in the bottle. I probably need to find some peace with that, though I reserve the right to still yell at clouds about it. In the meantime, I think we should do more to celebrate human involvement in things and not be shy about showing the process and behind-the-scenes work.

The legendary studio behind series like Mass Effect, Dragon Age, Neverwinter Nights, and Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic is no more. BioWare announced via social media Wednesday afternoon that “after more than three decades of shared adventures, BioWare is closing and early development on the next Mass Effect project is ending.” The official announcement was posted mere minutes after a Bloomberg report detailed 400 job cuts across BioWare parent company Electronic Arts, including 50 at BioWare, based on reports from “a person familiar with the company’s plans.” “Across our portfolio, we’re making deliberate choices to align talent and investments where we see the greatest opportunities for players and long-term growth,” EA executives wrote in a message to staff, according to Bloomberg. Following its 1995 founding in Edmonton, Alberta, BioWare first came under the EA umbrella through a 2007 purchase, before Mass Effect and Dragon Age were even a thing. At the time, EA’s then-CEO, John Riccitiello, called BioWare one of “the most respected studios in the industry” that would “make a strong contribution to our strategic growth initiatives on quality, online gaming and developing new intellectual properties. We also expect this will drive long-term value for our shareholders.” Of course, EA doesn’t have public stockholders anymore after a $55 billion deal led by Silver Lake, Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund, and Jared Kushner’s Affinity Partners took the company private last year. At the time of that move, analysts told Ars to expect “layoffs, studio closures, and [selling] of IP” as the newly private and now heavily debt-ridden company put “a bigger focus on live services, more focus on annual sports franchises, [and] microtransactions” to help increase profits. In more recent years, BioWare has also been beset by a number of high-profile departures, including founders Ray Muzyka and Greg Zeschuk in 2012 and longtime producer Mac Walters in 2023. Executive producer Casey Hudson, who left BioWare in 2014, ended up returning in 2017 before leaving yet again in 2020. The studio has faced its fair share of troubles following those departures, too, including muted critical and fan receptions for games like Mass Effect Andromeda and Dragon Age: The Veilguard. Anthem, the studio’s attempt at a live-service open-world shooter, turned out more ambitious than engaging for most and was recently shuttered for good well after plans for a sweeping overhaul were aborted. “What generations of BioWare built together is something to celebrate and remember,” the company wrote in its goodbye post. “These games became so much more because you played them, debated your choices, fell in love with the characters, and made these worlds your own. From all of us at BioWare, thank you for being the most meaningful part of our story.”

The next K-pop? POPS founder Esther Nguyen hopes TikTok can make Southeast Asian music a global phenomenon

Fortune | FORTUNE | 2026-10-22

Visit Original Website
Esther Nguyen has what may be a stereotypical story for a Vietnamese-American entrepreneur working in Vietnam. Her parents fled the country when Saigon fell in 1975; Nguyen, born in Michigan and raised in the Bay Area, had long felt the pull of her family’s former home. “I knew I wanted to do something in Southeast Asia, and specifically Vietnam,” Nguyen tells Fortune. “I thought about what really resonates with the local culture, and landed on music.” In 2007, she packed her bags and moved to Ho Chi Minh City with a dream of creating the “Spotify of Vietnam,” before there was even a digital ecosystem to work in. The local music industry, she recalls, was a mess. Back then, “no one understood digital rights,” and artists were signing contracts for CDs and DVDs that said nothing about digital distribution. Nguyen responded by starting POPS Worldwide, an entertainment company which produces, licenses, distributes, and monetizes digital content across platforms like YouTube and Facebook. Nineteen years after she arrived in Ho Chi Minh City, she now manages a portfolio of over 45,000 recordings across different genres, with hubs in Vietnam, Thailand and Indonesia. In March, Primary Wave—the independent publisher whose catalogs include Prince, Bob Marley, and Britney Spears—invested in the company; a Tokyo listing is in the works. Now, Nguyen sees an opportunity for Southeast Asian music as social media shakes up the world of pop culture. “Nowadays, people are listening to music from Africa, South Korea or Mexico, and embedding it into their stories and reels—and they like it even if it isn’t in their own language,” she explains. “Our goal is to ensure that Southeast Asian music will also resonate globally.” Nguyen had a tough hill to climb when she arrived in Vietnam in 2007. Her Vietnamese, she says, was “like a two-year-old’s,” so local artists often laughed her out of the room. “They’d kind of brush me away, and I’d keep coming back, and they’d be like, ‘Well, just go take it. Our content is on the internet anyway.’” Still, she says she relied on her Silicon Valley experience to keep going. “The mentality of ‘keep moving, don’t stop’ and that it’s okay to fail—I think I’ve really embraced that part of Silicon Valley,” she says. POPS’ answer was to educate the market—“song by song, title by title,” as she puts it—slowly assembling one of Vietnam’s largest music repertoires and becoming one of YouTube’s first music partners in the region. Following its foundational years in Vietnam, POPS expanded to other markets in Southeast Asia, entering Thailand in 2016 and Indonesia in 2020. “Vietnam is our home country. Indonesia has close to 300 million people, and Thai consumers have high purchasing power,” Nguyen explains. “You put those three markets together…it’s really a powerhouse for the region.” POPS is part of a growing Vietnamese pop-culture scene. Yeah1, Vietnam’s first listed media company, reported a 60% revenue jump in 2025 thanks to formats like Call Me by Fire, a singing competition that drew crowds as large as 50,000 people a night. Corporate heavyweights are stepping in: Vingroup, No. 26 on the Southeast Asia 500, made culture a “core pillar” of its strategy last year. Even Vietnam’s government is backing the cultural industry. In January, Vietnam’s government passed Resolution 80, which set a target of growing the cultural economy by 10% a year, until it reaches 7% of GDP by 2030. Still, consumers in Southeast Asia’s emerging markets are also highly price-sensitive, which Nguyen admits made it difficult for her to grow the business financially. “Southeast Asia is a place where you need a lot of patience—it’s a difficult place to to scale in terms of monetization, but it’s really easy to scale in terms of numbers,” she says. “I’m still waiting for the two to converge together.” Apart from licensing Southeast Asian content, POPS also helps to bring foreign IPs from markets like the U.S. and Japan to the region. The firm is one of Southeast Asia’s biggest importers of Japanese anime, including series like Doraemon, Naruto, One Piece, and Detective Conan. (In 2022, Japanese broadcasting company TV TOKYO, which airs major titles like Sergeant Keroro and My Guardian Characters, was the lead investor in POPS’ Series D fundraising round.) “The secret to our success is going deep into each market, understanding the cultural nuances, and then layering on cross-border content, whether it be from Korea, U.S., or Japan,” Nguyen explains. POPS is also working toward a Tokyo listing, through the Tokyo Stock Exchange’s Asia Startup Hub program. POPS executives had previously suggested a listing might come in 2027, but Nguyen won’t put a date on it for now. “Japan is not like the U.S., where if you decide this, you can list in six months,” she says. “There’s a lot of requirements for a Japan listing.” Nguyen says the firm is “near breakeven” today, with plans for profitability in 2027 across its markets, though she declined to reveal exact figures. Generative AI has flooded the market with cheap content—AI-generated microdramas have proliferated on Chinese streaming platforms like iQIYI, while apps like Suno and Google’s Lyria can generate a song from a prompt. Creators, Nguyen says, have to experiment. “It’s about cutting costs,” she says. “AI allows creators to deliver high-quality content in a short period of time, [which is important] because the hunger for content is so prevalent.” Her own focus group is at home—she has four children. “The younger generation is very anti-AI,” she says. “They’ll criticize something as long as they can see or smell AI in it. Instead, they value content not produced with AI, which they deem as more authentic.” Platforms, she notes, are still “rewarding fully authentic, human-made content”—or at least content with a human touch. Yet POPS itself straddles that line. The firm runs AI influencers, and brands have booked them, Nguyen says, “without us having to do anything because they couldn’t tell it was AI.” She calls that “a little unnerving.” The industry’s way through, she argues, is labeling and enforcement: platforms and artists are locked in “a chicken and egg” discussion over how to flag AI content and train the systems to protect the originals. Social media, Nguyen argues, is giving lesser-known artists a chance to shine outside a music ecosystem that privileges local acts and Western superstars. “Social media has really democratized content,” she says. In 2024, “I Like You the Most,” a song by Thai R&B singer PONCHET, went viral on TikTok, after global creators and influencers adopted its “sped up” version for dance challenges and acoustic covers. Today, its official music video has over 140 million views on YouTube. That same year, “the cutest pair,” a song by Singaporean singer-songwriter Regina Song, went viral on TikTok. At its peak, the song topped Spotify’s “Viral 50” global chart, and today has over 68.5 million streams on the platform. Nguyen is certain that Southeast Asian countries like Vietnam will eventually get their time in the spotlight. “V-pop is extremely catchy,” she says. “It’s a great genre for dance, and fits well with the TikTok style.” Her dream sits on the other side of the Pacific Ocean. “I would love to see a Vietnamese artist doing a sellout concert in Mexico City,” Nguyen says. “That is definitely one of our biggest goals.” Esther Nguyen will be speaking at the Southeast Asia 500 Summit on Nov. 3 in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. The blowout news from SpaceX’s Q3 earnings wasn’t the rocket and AI giant’s outstanding performance that far exceeded even the analysts’ elevated expectations, but Elon Musk’s revelation that SpaceX and Tesla are weighing a merger. On the call, held the evening of October 20, Musk declared that his two signature holdings have each named “special committees” composed of independent directors to explore a union. Musk’s statement confirmed Wall Street’s long-held view that the union was highly likely, and now—given that Musk effectively controls both companies and loves the idea (though as a “conflicted party” can’t serve on the committees), the tie-up looks all but inevitable. The prospect of a SpaceX-Tesla combo got rave notices from the research crews covering both companies. In most cases where the acquirer is en route to paying a price amounting to a humongous PE, the acquirer’s stock falls. Not this time. This time, investors applauded both enterprises, sending Tesla up 3.4% the day following Musk’s statement and SpaceX up 7.8%, adding $155 billion in market cap. At first glance, their already tight kinship, and all their many overlapping businesses, suggest that a new SpaceX-Tesla makes sense. SpaceX buys Tesla Megapack batteries and Cybertrucks, Tesla invested $2 billion in xAI, bought by SpaceX in February, and they share ownership of the much-feted chip project Terafab. Not to mention that the deal would clinch Musk’s long-harbored vision of running a single colossus as a kind of Berkshire Hathaway of AI-driven tech. The wizard freed to blend these two great elixirs of innovation? What’s not to like? A close examination of claims made for the deal, especially assumptions on its supposed advantages in bolstering the finances of both companies, points to an extremely risky wager that must perform never-before-seen heroics to reward shareholders. It’s the ultimate, buy the leap of faith, it’s about the vision, not the numbers, never bet against Elon scenario, set against the sobering numbers. Let’s explore four frequently made arguments for what would prove the largest and, so far, probably the most universally praised, merger in the annals of capitalism. The idea is that while SpaceX profits are just starting to accelerate, adding Tesla’s earnings, generated from its bedrock EV sales, would immediately raise the former’s EPS. In fact, it’s technically true: If the pair combined now, that’s how the math would work. The problem: The addition to SpaceX’s GAAP profits per share would be minuscule, and since SpaceX’s bottom line’s growing and Tesla’s dropping, the deal if done a year or two from now would prove extremely dilutive for the Starlink provider. What stands out when you examine what SpaceX would pay versus what earnings it would get is how shockingly expensive the transaction would be. One big question: How much premium would SpaceX offer over Tesla’s current price? As Rajat Gupta of JPMorgan points out in a report on a likely merger, that number in most tech acquisitions is 30% to 35%. So let’s posit 30%. Right now, Tesla’s market cap is $1.52 trillion; tack on 30%, and SpaceX would be out $1.98 trillion. SpaceX’s valuation stands at $2.14 trillion. We’re looking at almost a merger of equals. SpaceX would need to issue the equivalent of 92% of its current shares outstanding to capture Tesla. Hence, SpaceX’s count would zoom from 13.1 billion to 25 billion shares. SpaceX would be awarding nearly half of its future earnings to Tesla’s selling shareholders, And what’s it getting in return? We don’t know what the robots and self-driving cars will deliver, but we do know that Tesla booked just $3.28 billion in GAAP net profits over the past four quarters. But SpaceX would just go from EPS that’s a minuscule negative to a tiny positive. What’s important is the amount SpaceX is paying for those earnings and puny “accretion.” It would be getting $3.28 billion in profits in exchange for stock worth $1.98 trillion. For decades now, scholars have argued over what—if anything—we should have gleaned from the Stanford Prison Experiment. That it was unethical to place two dozen male college students in a university basement repurposed as a prison, designate a few of them guards and the rest prisoners, and encourage the former to discipline the latter, was apparent by the time its mastermind, Stanford psychology professor Philip Zimbardo, ended what was supposed to be a two-week exercise after only six days. As the extent of Zimbardo’s manipulation came to light, the 1971 study lost credibility in academic circles, even as its pop-cultural legend kept growing. Fifty-five years after it was conducted, perhaps the only definitive conclusion to draw from the Stanford Prison Experiment is that the Stanford Prison Experiment is not worth repeating. But just try telling that to the reality-TV machine, let alone a platform currently at work on the third, all-celebrity season of a competition series that cheerfully (and, of course, non-lethally) recreates the horrors of its international hit anticapitalist battle royale thriller Squid Game. The New Stanford Prison Experiment, Netflix’s take on a project that was always more convincing as improvisational theater than as scientific research, isn’t even television’s first crack at Zimbardo’s study. The BBC got there first with The Experiment, in 2002, whose earnest attempts to correct for the original’s missteps yielded a more nuanced, if still inherently flawed, look at authority. That the Netflix version of what can now be best understood as a reboot of a famous franchise feels less like social psychology and more like a high-concept season of Big Brother is no surprise. What’s most perplexing is its choice to incorporate female participants in a limited way—and its failure to make any real sense out of what they bring to the experience. Aware that the televised resurrection of a notoriously inhumane experiment was bound to draw scrutiny (the mere announcement of the show’s existence touched off a wave of incredulity), the producers of the six-episode series take pains to make both their safety precautions and their high-mindedness visible. Instead of a retrofitted academic building, the inmates are sequestered in a real housing pod of a Georgia correctional facility. The 30 voluntary participants are said to come from all walks of life, though what we find out about their backgrounds is generally limited to hometown, racial identity, and line of work. According to executive producer Jane Lipsitz, each “underwent psychological screenings prior to entering the experiment.” There’s a safe word any of the participants can, and some do, use to leave the roleplay early. Without the hard edge and clarity of legal processes and consequences following this administration, America could enable antidemocratic forces to persist indefinitely. Impunity for Trump-era crimes and corruption will guarantee their return and fuel the existing cynicism throughout our society. For one thing, they must explain to voters the price Trump and his circle must pay for their brazenly unlawful conduct. They need to socialize a language of accountability that promotes rule of law, democracy, and due process while eschewing talk of retribution, retaliation, or political payback. They need to articulate how an accountability agenda will also support the pursuit of popular policies in the public interest, whether it’s affordability or long-term issues such as the concentration of oligarchic wealth, a sustainable climate, or healthcare. So what does that actually look like? What should be the plan to build high-level accountability into an American government that has, it turns out, so long been without it? At the very least, the accountability agenda will require an independent Department of Justice pursuing criminal investigation and prosecution of those responsible for the actions that most experts and officials view as manifestly unlawful. Some states, such as Illinois and Minnesota, have established commissions to begin the work of accountability already. Democracies do this as a matter of course, and we can, too, even if we modify these lessons, and learn from their shortcomings, for our own particular contexts. Some of the crimes may be amenable to state prosecutions, which the pardon power does not reach, and federal prosecutors should be open to helping those succeed. The most heinous of crimes—those constituting war crimes or crimes against humanity—may even be subject to criminal process by prosecutors outside the United States. Accountability, to be clear, is not retribution. It is leveling the system so that we can genuinely say that no person is above the law. It is promising due process and even forbearance for those who participate in genuine acts of truth-telling. But it has to happen if we are to build a democratic society. PARTNER CONTENT: AI is driving liquid cooling adoption, but hybrid architectures will help operators balance density, flexibility, and operational realities From training massive language models to powering nonstop inference, AI is fundamentally rewriting the rules of data center thermal management. AI workloads are characteristically less predictable and significantly more power-dense than traditional operations. As a result, deploying liquid cooling is no longer an experimental consideration. It has become absolute table stakes for handling AI and high-performance computing (HPC) demands. The industry conversation has shifted rapidly from “if or when” to how quickly organizations can deploy and scale. Here is a look at how AI is driving the shift toward high-density thermal management, the operational realities of adopting these systems, why the near future belongs to hybrid environments, and how data center operators can successfully navigate the transition. Historically, traditional cooling methods were sufficient for enterprise workloads. However, air cooling remains viable and efficient only up to approximately 20 to 40 kW per rack. Today’s GPU-accelerated AI racks commonly operate between 40 kW and well over 100 kW, with next-generation systems expected to push toward 200 kW or more. This GPU-driven density has made air-only cooling strategies fundamentally impractical for cutting-edge infrastructure. This extreme concentration of heat generation challenges traditional airflow-based architectures, demanding a transformation in AI data center cooling. In the ongoing evaluation of DLC vs. air (direct liquid cooling versus air cooling), the performance data is conclusive. Thermal modeling demonstrates that single-phase direct liquid cooling maintains chip-to-coolant temperature differences of roughly 17 to 20 °C under roughly 500 W processor loads, whereas comparable air-cooled systems exceed 60 °C. Liquid cooling, which is up to 3,000 times more effective at transferring heat than air, efficiently captures heat directly at the source, preventing thermal throttling and protecting infrastructure ROI. While transitioning to liquid cooling may seem like a straightforward solution, it is rarely a sudden, wholesale replacement. Most facilities are not going all-in on liquid. Instead, they are adopting hybrid cooling models that combine traditional air architectures with advanced direct-to-chip systems. Through 2027 and 2028, hybrid thermal strategies will dominate because data centers must accommodate both legacy IT equipment and next-generation AI accelerators within the same facility. The reality of brownfield retrofits, where most AI capacity is initially being deployed in existing air-cooled facilities, versus designing greenfield sites for liquid from day one means that air cooling will still play a critical role in ancillary systems and lower-density zones. The future is not strictly liquid cooling only. It is about right-sizing the cooling method to the specific workload. Operators will successfully scale by deploying mixed architectures, such as utilizing direct-to-chip cold plates and Coolant Distribution Units (CDUs) for dense AI clusters, while managing residual heat with Computer Room Air Handlers (CRAHs) or fan-assisted Rear-Door Heat Exchangers (RDHx). While the technology for liquid cooling is maturing, navigating the operational complexity of a hybrid cooling environment is the real challenge. Cooling is no longer just a facilities issue. It requires the convergence of IT, mechanical engineering, and software management. Facilities must now manage fluid chemistry, leak detection, risk mitigation, and sophisticated maintenance workflows. What’s more, the industry is facing a widening talent gap. Uptime Institute reports that nearly two-thirds of operators struggle to hire and retain qualified staff, underscoring a shortage of teams with expertise across both liquid cooling systems and integrated infrastructure. Addressing this will require new training models, deeper vendor collaboration, and greater reliance on managed services. While the industry is making strides toward standardizing interoperable designs - such as plug-and-play CDUs and manifolds that distribute coolant efficiently across racks - there is still fragmentation across vendors and approaches. Additionally, operators must navigate changing economics. While liquid infrastructure requires higher upfront CapEx, it delivers better Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) over time due to enhanced efficiency and density. Decision-makers are consequently shifting their focus from what is cheapest today to what can scale with AI demand. Navigating this hybrid cooling transition requires standardizing deployments to avoid complex, bespoke engineering for every site. A successful liquid cooling ecosystem relies on selecting the appropriate heat rejection architectures, which are defined by the heat capture method, CDU type (rack-mounted or floor-mounted), and the method of rejecting heat outdoors. A successful blueprint involves choosing among six core architectures, ranging from rejecting heat to air within the IT space via liquid-to-air (L-A) CDUs, to utilizing dedicated independent water systems with liquid-to-liquid (L-L) CDUs for massive deployments. Furthermore, liquid cooling unlocks opportunities for heat reuse, allowing operators to redirect waste heat to adjacent buildings or district heating systems. Deploying liquid cooling at scale introduces risk, particularly when sourcing racks, power, and cooling from multiple uncoordinated vendors. For example, Schneider Electric’s close collaboration with NVIDIA on validated architectures helps prevent physical infrastructure from becoming a bottleneck to GPU performance. ●  Digital Lifecycle Management: AI infrastructure must become more dynamic. Using digital twins and simulation software, like Schneider Electric’s ETAP and AVEVA platforms, teams can design, model, and validate integrated power and cooling architectures before physical construction begins, predicting issues before they arise. ● Global Manufacturing and Services: Sourcing from partners with robust global manufacturing capacity reduces supply chain risk and accelerates delivery. Post-implementation, leveraging real-time monitoring and predictive maintenance helps bridge the internal talent gap while sustaining operations. Ultimately, liquid cooling hasn’t replaced air. It has fundamentally reshaped the conversation around data center design. What was once framed as a binary debate between direct-to-chip and traditional air cooling has evolved into a more pragmatic reality: no single approach can meet the diverse and rapidly shifting demands of AI workloads. The future lies in flexible, hybrid environments that intelligently integrate both air and liquid cooling to match workload requirements, optimize efficiency, and manage risk. As AI continues to push the boundaries of power density and performance, operators must prioritize adaptability, designing infrastructure that can scale, evolve, and respond in real time. In this new era, success will not be defined by choosing one cooling method over another, but by building resilient, future-ready systems capable of supporting whatever comes next.
