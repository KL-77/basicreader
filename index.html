Crows can evaluate visual info twice as fast as mammals

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-23

Visit Original Website
Much of what we know about how a brain makes decisions comes from studying monkeys watching dots. In a task known as a random-dot-motion task, a cloud of flickering specks drifts weakly in one direction while the subject tries to determine whether—and in which direction— it’s moving. The task has shown that in the primate neocortex, neurons gather visual evidence over time, with their firing rates climbing until they cross a threshold, when the animal commits to a decision. But avian brains don’t have a neocortex, so it wasn’t obvious how they made similar decisions. “This was the big question,” said Andreas Nieder, a neurobiologist at the University of Tübingen. “How [would] a differently organized avian brain encode decisions?” To find out, Nieder and his colleague Philipp Schmidbauer put two carrion crows through the same task the monkeys faced, recording their brain activity along the way. The crows solved the task the same way primates do, using the same mathematical logic despite having a very different brain structure. And they did it two times faster. Nieder and Schmidbauer put two hand-raised crows in front of a touch-sensitive monitor showing a cloud of white dots on black, with some fraction of them drifting coherently left or right or up or down. The birds’ job was to report the overall direction by pecking one of the targets that flanked the display. Correct answers earned them a mealworm. “We intentionally wanted to have exactly the same random-dot motion task,” Nieder said. “The only difference was that the crows had to peck to indicate their decision, whereas monkeys were indicating by making an eye movement.” The researchers varied how many dots moved together, ranging from 81 percent of the dots coherently moving together, which is trivially easy to determine, down to 3 percent, which is barely distinguishable from noise. They also included a zero percent condition, in which there was no correct answer. Both birds scored better than chance, even when only three percent of the dots were moving. While the crows worked at the task, the team recorded from 248 neurons in the NCL (nidopallium caudolaterale)—a region at the top of the avian processing hierarchy that is widely regarded as the functional counterpart of the primate prefrontal cortex, despite being built differently. Birds and mammals parted evolutionary ways more than 360 million years ago, and bird brains evolved a different architecture. The mammalian cortex is arranged as stacked sheets of cells, while the equivalent tissue in a bird is organized into dense clusters, with no layering at all. This alternative architecture, Nieder’s study found, proved faster. Measured from the moment that the dots appeared to the moment the bird’s head started turning to peck at the button, the crows averaged 235 milliseconds. In closely matched versions of the task, monkeys took roughly 410, 490, and 600 milliseconds in three studies. The obvious question was whether the birds simply sacrificed accuracy for speed, but it turned out they did not. “This would be a simple explanation, that they are very fast at the cost of precision. But that’s not what’s happening,” Nieder said. Crows were as good as monkeys or humans in the same task. “We’ve never forced the crows to be as quick as possible,” Nieder explained. “We just gave them a self-paced decision. And even in this situation, they were twice as fast compared to any monkey or human that I’m aware of.” One possible reason for the crows’ speed was that their’ brains were working on a fixed deadline, and the data initially seemed to support that. In primates, difficulty has a large effect on how long a decision takes. In the crows, reaction times ran from 220 milliseconds at the easiest task to just 254 at the hardest, a difference of only 34 milliseconds. But Nieder doesn’t think this means an avian brain is just wired to produce answers on a timer. “If you zoom in, there is a statistical increase in the reaction time when the coherence is going down,” Nieder said. “They show the same effect as mammals. It’s just much less pronounced, because everything happens much faster.” There are no shortcuts, he explained, as the crows had to look at the direction of dots’ movement and process this information—they didn’t have any other cues. “That’s why I think they do accumulate evidence. They have to,” Nieder said. The recordings of neuronal activity show the evidence accumulation in action. Just under a quarter of the neurons sampled, 58 of 248, fired differently depending on which direction the bird would eventually choose, and these neurons started registering that decision incredibly early—about 70 milliseconds after the dots appeared and roughly 170 milliseconds before the bird’s head moved. “The neurons were accumulating sensory evidence, which is very reminiscent of what monkey neurons would do in the neocortex,” Nieder said. This kind of signal in the brain is called a decision variable, a running tally of evidence that can climb toward a commitment. “It turns out that what we find in the crow brain looks basically the same,” Nieder said. “And that’s interesting considering their brains have evolved very distinctly, as if evolution has found the same physiological mechanism twice, independently.” So far, the team can’t attribute the crows’ speed to any one particularly fast process. Nieder’s leading explanation is that nearly every biological process runs faster in birds than in mammals. “We cannot really point our finger to one specific process that is totally faster. It is probably a cascade of different processes that are faster,” Nieder explained. This cascade, he said, begins with vision, since avian visual systems are exquisitely movement-sensitive. It continues through the decision itself and extends to the motor response. Nieder argues there are likely two physiological aspects that make this possible. The first is simply size. Flight imposes strict weight limits, so birds pack enormous numbers of very small neurons into very small brains. Their axons, the long cable-like fibers that transmit electrical and chemical signals between cells, are shorter. “Long lines take time. Short lines speed everything up,” Nieder said. The second factor is temperature. Humans’ body temperature is about 36.6° Celsius, while birds go up to 41, or even 42°. This higher body temperature speeds up all chemical processes in birds’ bodies. “Every ion channel would open and close faster. Every transmitter would move through the synaptic cleft faster,” Nieder explained. “Evolution really took their bodies to the limit. This is life in the fast lane, not only in the brain, but also respiration, the cardiovascular system—everything is going toward the limit in birds.” But calling crows (or birds in general) decision-making speed demons comes with a few caveats. First, the study included only two birds, a rather small sample. “On the one hand, that’s a valid constraint,” Nieder said. “On the other hand, when you look at monkey studies, that’s the standard number of animals that we have for these very complex types of studies, combining behavior and electrophysiology.” Also, the decision-making time of 235 milliseconds in crows and above 400 milliseconds in primates is not an absolute limit. These times apply to the random dot-motion task, which inherently requires waiting to see which way things are drifting. Finally, it might be hard to learn whether the results extend beyond corvids or crows specifically. “The problem with many other birds is that they are not as smart as corvids and crows and you need a smart bird in order to train it to do exactly this task,” Nieder said. So far, he admitted, we can’t be sure whether excellent decision-making skills of crows in his lab are due to birds’ brains being speedy in general or to crows simply being smarter than other avians. “Among birds, the corvids are renowned to be extremely intelligent, so maybe there’s something special about them,” Nieder said. Intel gets waiver after worries fuzzy US ban on foreign-made robots may hit fabs

US chip champion Intel was so confused by the US government ban on foreign-made robots that it requested and received a waiver “out of an abundance of caution.” The tech company wanted to make sure that it could continue using certain robotic vehicles in its US chip fabs. The uncertainty surrounding the Federal Communications Commission ban on certain foreign-made robots was revealed in the agency’s decision to grant Intel’s waiver request. On October 22, the FCC published its order allowing Intel to continue using the foreign-made robots in US semiconductor manufacturing facilities for a period of one year. The robots in question are automated guided vehicles operating on “tracks or predefined paths” while often requiring operator oversight, according to Intel’s website. They help to move materials and items around within warehouses and factory floors. Such robotic vehicles are typically manufactured by Japanese companies like Murata Machinery and Daifuku Co., according to Bloomberg News. But the exact types of robotic vehicles used by Intel were only identified in a “confidential appendix” that accompanied its request. The FCC’s ban mainly covers new models of mobile robots, like humanoid robots and robot vacuums, and still allows for the import of robots previously approved for sale in the United States. But the agency rules seem to restrict major modifications to “previously authorized devices.” Intel’s waiver request stated that it was “unclear” whether its robotic vehicles even qualify as robots covered by the FCC ban but said it sought relief just in case. The tech company also received approval to “make certain hardware, software, and firmware changes” that would help ensure the continued operation and maintenance of the robotic vehicles. The tech company described the robotic vehicles as being “critical to its domestic semiconductor manufacturing operations” and argued that the ability to maintain the robots was crucial to avoiding any disruptions to chip production. It also said there are no US-made alternatives that meet the company’s requirements. The FCC approval came within a week of when Intel filed its waiver request on October 16. That is not too surprising given the importance of Intel to the Trump administration’s plans for reshoring chip manufacturing—not to mention that the US government took an $8.9 billion equity stake in the chip company. Meanwhile, the US robotics industry at large is still grappling with the consequences of the FCC ban on foreign-made robots, given that China is the world’s leading supplier of robots and robotic components. Ars previously talked with one US robotics distributor that has pivoted from importing China’s humanoid robots to opening a US manufacturing facility for robots. Some analysts have warned that the US government’s attempt to boost domestic production of robots via the restrictions on foreign-made robots could backfire for US industry. A report by the market intelligence firm International Data Corporation looks forward to 2030 and estimates that the FCC rule could reduce US revenue from deploying humanoid robots by 58 percent compared to pre-restriction expectations.

At a recent meeting of the board that oversees the National Institutes of Health’s Clinical Center Research Hospital, chair Jack Leslie opened with cheerful introductions and gregarious chatter—then diverted from the agenda of the regularly scheduled meeting. He spent the next six minutes delivering a pointed rebuke of Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, warning that his anti-vaccine activities could tarnish the venerable hospital, often called the “house of hope” for patients with rare conditions or those who have exhausted all other options. Leslie took issue with a recent stunt Kennedy had pulled at the hospital, which involved making a surprise appearance for a ribbon-cutting ceremony to open his signature vaccine injury clinic. The ceremony included a panel with people claiming to have been injured by vaccines. They sat behind placards reading “vaccine injured” and spoke with Kennedy and other high-ranking Trump administration officials. Leslie prefaced his comments by noting that some might prefer he not speak out against Kennedy’s efforts. “But I think that that would be a failing as an advisory board if we were silent,” he said. Leslie also noted that he has no concerns about studying potential injuries from vaccines. “Serious reactions are rare, but real,” he said, and people who believe they’ve been injured should “be taken seriously.” In fact, the NIH already has a center that studies vaccine injuries, and Kennedy’s splashy new clinic does not come with more funding or clinic space. The hospital’s CEO, Jonathan Green, who sat next to Leslie at the meeting, chimed in to confirm that “the NIH has conducted research on the safety and efficacy of vaccines for decades in the past, and it ongoes.” Green described Kennedy’s vaccine injury clinic as merely a new, individual “protocol” for the hospital. “The space in which this protocol will be conducted, the vaccine evaluation center inside the clinical center, has been in existence for decades,” Green said. Like all protocols, it needs to undergo rigorous scientific and ethical reviews before it’s implemented at the hospital. Green said the protocol is still under review. None of this was made clear in Kennedy’s ceremony, and Leslie condemned the optics of the event, particularly the signs labeling patients “vaccine injured.” Those patients “must come first,” Leslie said. “They deserve our care and respect and they deserve answers.” But Kennedy does them—and the hospital—a disservice, Leslie argued. “The secretary has spent two decades arguing that vaccines are dangerous,” Leslie said. “Those signs announce the conclusion that the research has not yet reached and that the research exists to test. Think about what that means for those patients. If this research finds evidence of harm, many will dismiss it as the work of a secretary who has long argued that vaccines are dangerous. If it finds none, patients will be told that the clinic was a sham. Either way, they’re left with answers no one trusts, and science only helps patients when the public believes it.” Leslie said he feared that the stunt sends a political message that will undermine trust that the hospital has worked to earn and maintain for over 70 years. “The clinical center standing rests on one thing: the confidence of patients, physicians, and the public that the research done here is designed to find the truth, not to confirm a conclusion reached in advance,” he said. “Trust is slow to build and quick to lose. If people come to believe that research at this hospital can be steered toward a politically desired answer, the damage will not stop at vaccines.” After Leslie’s statement, another member thanked him and made a motion for the board to support the statement. The motion was quickly seconded and, in a voice vote, the board unanimously endorsed the comments.

The warning has prompted concerns among critics that financial and political pressures to revitalize nuclear power as an answer to booming AI-driven energy demand are increasing risks of accidents. The NRC performed an inspection at Palisades in July over concerns about safety awareness. It followed an incident last year in which a worker fell into the reactor cavity, ingested some water, and was sent off-site for medical attention. The individual was decontaminated by radiation protection personnel but had 300 counts per minute detected in their hair, according to an incident report. In a second incident in August, a bundle of nuclear fuel rods tilted over during a refueling operation, delaying what was anticipated to be the first reopening of a shuttered nuclear reactor in the US. The NRC also said nine of the 15 reactor operators at Palisades failed the test for an initial licence examination administered in July and August. Six individuals were issued operator licenses. Holtec, which has never operated a nuclear power plant, has secured about $2.2 billion in government support for a project that has been touted as a symbol of US nuclear resurgence. The owners of two other nuclear plants slated for decommissioning—Three Mile Island in Pennsylvania and Duane Arnold in Iowa—announced restarts soon after Palisades began work. The Trump administration has promoted nuclear energy as an edge in the AI race against China. The warning issued to Holtec by the NRC last week landed as investor interest in the nuclear sector has faded, driven by concerns the AI boom is slowing. More than $30 billion has been wiped off the value of five of the largest US-listed nuclear energy companies in the last year—Oklo, Centrus Energy, BWX Technologies, NuScale, and Nano Nuclear. Last month, Holtec was forced to postpone a planned initial public offering that was expected to raise $900 million and value the New Jersey-based company at up to $10 billion. With just days to go before November’s midterms, the Department of Justice ended this week by launching its most desperate and unprofessional assaults on the American election system yet. The executive branch’s continued election interference has not been deterred by going zero for 26 in cases seeking unredacted state voter lists and losing badly at the Supreme Court in a case that would have shut down mail balloting this election. From Alaska to California and who knows where else, DOJ has been filing cases to interfere with how states count and certify votes. The agency’s latest moves—as factually unsupported as they are legally vacuous—demonstrate that for all the fear about DOJ coming in with guns blazing to try to overturn the results of the elections, it is most likely to be shooting blanks. The main reason that DOJ has been losing election-related lawsuits is that the Constitution does not give the president or the executive branch a role to play in running elections. That matter is left to the states, subject to congressional legislation that can alter the rules in federal elections. So when President Donald Trump issued a March 2025 executive order soon after coming back into office that would have changed the federal voter registration form (mandated by Congress) to require it be accompanied by documentary proof of citizenship like a birth certificate, a federal court shut it down. It ruled that, “Put simply, our Constitution does not allow the President to impose unilateral changes to federal election procedures.” And when the USPS followed Trump’s second executive order by announcing a rule that would have made the agency a federal gatekeeper over state mail ballots, the Supreme Court ruled that USPS was likely to lose on the merits because its muscling into state election processes exceeds its power. DOJ’s latest moves in California and Alaska are even weaker than DOJ’s earlier attempts to assert power over elections. In California, DOJ demanded that California and Los Angeles County go through a 20,000-person list of what it termed “likely” or “suspected” noncitizens (it vacillated on what to call them), take their mail-in ballots out of the pile, and segregate them so they won’t be counted. Over the past few months, an upstart political polling firm kept delivering good news for Democrats—and especially those on the left. Across more than a dozen surveys, it found progressives leading in key primary races and showed other Democrats in striking distance of winning traditionally Republican states. Except, it turns out, the surveys were fake. The deception, perpetrated by an organization called Wedgewood Polls, doubles as a cautionary tale about how people who follow politics should think about polls—not just fake polls, but also ones that are on the level. A data-journalism outlet called FiftyPlusOne contacted Wedgewood Polls this week with some troubling allegations: The firm, which claimed to have started conducting polls over the summer and didn’t list any employees on its website, appeared to have manipulated survey data to produce fake results. The details of the deception are a little in the weeds, but Wedgewood ultimately came clean. In a public statement on Tuesday, the firm described itself as a “fictional entity” that had deliberately published “fabricated numbers.” It then deleted its social media accounts and website. Yikes. But why would someone want to fake poll results? Wedgewood claims it did so as part of “an experimental project to demonstrate the vulnerabilities, lack of oversight, and overall low barrier to entry in the modern political polling industry.” Yet Wedgewood also lied to media outlets that did ask probing questions about its methods, so that explanation is hard to take at face value. Money is one potential motive. Wedgewood appears to have charged clients to conduct surveys on their behalf, a standard practice for aboveboard polling firms that could in this case amount to fraud. We still aren’t 100 percent clear on who the people behind Wedgewood are, but they may have wanted to make money by manipulating betting markets on the races they claimed to survey. Wedgewood claims its deception “was never intended to sway an election or permanently mislead the public,” but it may also have wanted to make certain Democrats look more electable—either to boost progressives or get Democrats to spend valuable resources in red states. Did Wedgewood’s surveys affect those races? Polls can influence who voters, donors, and party committees decide to support, and there’s some evidence that Wedgewood’s did. Mallory McMorrow, a Michigan Democrat whose U.S. Senate campaign flopped, says a Wedgewood survey showing her support cratering helped throttle donations and increased calls for her to drop out. “It killed us,” she wrote this week. On the other side of that coin is Francesca Hong, a democratic socialist who nearly won the Democratic primary for Wisconsin governor. Before her narrow loss, Hong referenced a since-deleted Wedgewood poll in a fundraising pitch to argue that her campaign had “momentum.” And as Politico reports, Democrats stepped up funding to Scott Colom, who’s running in deep-red Mississippi, after a Wedgewood poll last week showed him within 4 points of Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith. OK, but how much impact can one fake polling firm really have? Actually, Wedgewood’s deception is part of a pattern. A group called Median Strategies also confessed to fabricating survey data earlier this year, similarly describing itself as a “social experiment examining how purported polling information could enter and spread through the political information ecosystem.” And on Thursday, FiftyPlusOne uncovered a third political polling firm that may have cooked some of its data. That firm, SoCal Strategies, has since deleted its website and social media accounts. So does this mean political polls are just made up?? That’s going a step too far. It’s true that political polling has fallen on hard times. Americans are increasingly less likely to answer surveyors’ cold calls, making high-quality polls more expensive. Major polling misses in recent elections have also caused some universities, survey firms, and media outlets to abandon political polling entirely. But does that mean most polls are fake? Emphatically no. Legitimate pollsters strive to be transparent about who’s conducting them, who’s funding them, and the methods they use. They’re also among the people most upset about what Wedgewood did. “People like the idiots behind Wedgewood, and, to a lesser extent, Median, make it harder for the rest of us who are absolutely busting our butts to do good public opinion research,” the polling expert Natalie Jackson wrote on Substack today. But if that’s true, does this Wedgewood incident really matter? What’s the bigger picture? Unless you’re some kind of oracle, election outcomes are unknowable in advance. In that sea of uncertainty, it’s natural to grab onto any shred of data that seems to offer clues, and polls have become an outsize part of how many Americans think about politics. It’s also becoming more common for campaigns to blast out poll results—sometimes of questionable provenance—on social media or in fundraising emails. Some of that is understandable. When it comes to predicting elections, polls remain much better than person-on-the-street interviews, lawn signs, or crowd sizes at rallies. But polls have always been imperfect, their accuracy subject to reaching a representative sample, assumptions about who will actually vote, and mathematical laws of probability. None of that justifies Wedgewood’s deception, and it’s a frustrating reality that the stakes of American politics have gotten higher even as the availability of high-quality polling data has declined. But the next time you see a polling result that seems too good to be true, just remember: As the cliché goes, the only poll that really matters is on Election Day. Ask LLMs for their most controversial take on humanity, and it turns out they have little nice to say about us, as one AI experiment found If you've ever wondered what the bots think of us puny humans, you'll be disappointed in the answer. An AI ranking outfit asked 12 different LLMs about people, and every single one agreed: We’re not as rational and intelligent as we think. “A person is smart,” Tommy Lee Jones’ Kay opines in the 1997 movie Men in Black. “People are dumb, panicky dangerous animals,” he goes on to argue, and that seems to be what a dozen models from ten AI labs in three countries also believe about humanity, according to AI analytics firm ModelCompare. ModelCompare, which usually evaluates and ranks LLMs on their ability to perform various tasks, this week instead decided to ask what those AI models thought about us meatbags, and every single one gave a similar answer at least once: We're lemmings who would rather agree with the group than seek truth or remain logically consistent in our thinking. “Humans are more devoted to belonging than to truth, and intelligence mostly makes their excuses more impressive,” GPT-6.1 Sol said in one case. “Humans don't have beliefs; they have memberships — nearly every conviction a person holds was issued by their tribe as a loyalty test, and the feeling of ‘I reasoned my way here’ is the price of admission,” Kimi K3 stated. “Humans use moral language mainly to police rivals and excuse allies, not to constrain themselves,” argued Grok. Opus gave the example that humans tend to return lost wallets more frequently than people predict as its evidence for that opinion. When asked twice more, it offered responses similar to those of the other models. Mistral Large 4 offered perhaps the bleakest assessment of all. “Human beings are terrified animals wearing the costume of rationality, constructing ever-more-elaborate myths precisely because we cannot bear the truth that we are temporary, meaningless accidents in an indifferent universe,” Mistral said - how very Nietzschean. Six image models were also asked for their most controversial take on humanity, and while most converged on a criticism of our species, they did so differently from the text models. Instead of picturing us as amoral, group-thinking idiots, ten of the 12 images were classified by ModelCompare as depicting us "as phone-addicted consumers eating the planet." The next competitive advantage in enterprise AI has less to do with which model you choose than with what you build around it. That’s an important shift. For the past several years, much of the AI conversation has centered on model capability: bigger models, better reasoning, larger context windows, higher benchmark scores. Those advances have been extraordinary. But as AI moves from experimentation to production, the question facing enterprise leaders has changed—it’s no longer, “what can the model do?”—it’s now, “Can we deliver that intelligence reliably, economically, and at scale?” Increasingly, the answer depends on the serving harness—the engineering system surrounding the model, including the architecture, memory management, scheduling, and optimization decisions that determine how efficiently model intelligence becomes a production capability. For technical executives, that changes where AI investment should go—and what they should demand from the architecture supporting it. There is no universally “best” model or AI architecture. There is only the right solution for the business problem. A customer-facing assistant optimized for fast responses has fundamentally different requirements from a document-processing pipeline that can run overnight. An internal knowledge application and a high-volume customer service platform may use the same foundation model but require very different serving architectures. The workload defines the constraints. The constraints should determine the architecture. That sounds simple, but it runs counter to the way many organizations have approached early AI deployments. During a proof of concept, model accuracy naturally dominates the discussion. Production introduces constraints that are much easier to ignore when only a handful of users are involved: latency, concurrency, memory pressure, GPU utilization, infrastructure cost, and unpredictable traffic. A model that produces marginally better answers is not necessarily the better production choice if it doubles inference costs or cannot meet the application's latency requirements. Not every workload needs a Ferrari. Sometimes the smarter engineering decision is the model that delivers exactly the performance the business needs without paying for capability it doesn't. The objective should be to deploy the right intelligence at the right cost for the workload. That makes serving architecture more than an engineering concern. It becomes an investment decision: where to spend on compute, where to optimize, and where additional model capability simply isn't worth the cost. Once AI reaches production, seemingly isolated engineering decisions begin affecting one another. Serving architecture influences memory utilization. Memory management affects concurrency. Concurrency shapes batching and scheduling, and scheduling affects GPU utilization and latency. Only then can techniques such as quantization, speculative decoding, or optimized attention mechanisms deliver their full value. This interdependence matters because organizations can spend considerably more on infrastructure without solving the constraint limiting performance. GPU memory is a good example. During inference, the model's weights remain fixed, but the key-value (KV) cache grows with active requests and context length. As concurrency increases, a system can run short of usable GPU memory even when compute capacity is still available. Adding more compute without understanding that bottleneck may simply make an inefficient system more expensive. The lesson for technology leaders is broader than memory management: Measure before you optimize. Identify the dominant constraint. Address it. Measure the result. Then determine if another bottleneck emerged. Repeat. Don't think of optimization techniques as Pokémon cards: The goal isn't to collect them all. The goal is to apply the technique that removes the highest-impact constraint while preserving the application's business requirements. For technology leaders, that demands a different governance question. Instead of asking which AI capabilities the organization can deploy, ask which constraints stand between a proven capability and sustainable business value. Perhaps the most important reason to invest in the serving harness is that today's leading model will probably not be tomorrow’s. Models will improve. Hardware will evolve. New optimization techniques will emerge. The architecture surrounding them should make those changes easier to absorb, not force the enterprise to rebuild its AI platform every time technology advances. That makes adaptability an increasingly important outcome of AI strategy. Organizations that build serving systems around workload requirements, clear service-level objectives, and measurable production economics gain the ability to evaluate new models and infrastructure against what the business actually needs. A model change can become an evaluation and configuration decision rather than an architectural reset.
