Since debuting in 2019, Amazon’s Echo Studio targeted users who wanted superior audio quality from their Alexa-based smart speaker. The latest Echo Studio adds another less-expected differentiator: an integrated camera. The Echo Studio with Home Camera, released alongside a pile of other Alexa devices today, doesn’t have many audio upgrades over the standard Echo Studio. Each speaker has one 3.75-inch high-excursion woofer and three full-range drivers. They both support Dolby Atmos and Amazon’s proprietary algorithm for adding a surround sound-like effect to stereo audio. Per the devices’ product pages, the speakers have lossless HD audio, Advanced Room Adaptation, and features for fighting noise distortion. Amazon announced the Advanced Room Adaptaion today, saying that the feature “continuously tunes the sound to your space, automatically adapting to the room’s acoustics.” Both speakers have four-microphone arrays and the same AZ3 Pro processor that Amazon introduced a year ago. The processor has an AI accelerator for using Amazon’s Alexa+ generative AI chatbot. The devices also have the same dimensions, even though the new Echo Studio With Home Camera fits in a 13MP camera. The speaker can use the camera to see where you are and adjust audio volume “so your music or audiobook maintains the same volume as you move about.” Beyond audio capabilities, Amazon sees people using the speaker as a smart camera, allowing them to remotely check on a room in the home to see, for example, “how the dog is doing or make sure you didn’t leave the lights on.” “Alexa can also see and understand what’s in front of the camera and help you in real time. Pull out a board game box and ask Alexa+ to explain the rules or hold up a cereal box, and Alexa can recognize it and help you reorder more flavors,” Amazon’s announcement says. One day, users will be able to pair the smart speaker-camera with an Amazon Fire TV for video calls, Amazon’s announcement said, without confirming when. Regarding user privacy, Amazon’s announcement said: Like all of our Echo devices, a dedicated button turns off the microphones and any included cameras. There are clear visual indicators whenever Echo devices with a camera are streaming images or video to the cloud to provide transparency—on Echo Studio with Home Camera, a built-in LED turns on. The standard Echo Studio remains available for those who prefer no camera. Echo Studio With Home Camera users can disable the camera via the Alexa mobile app and “continue interacting with Alexa when the camera is disabled,” the device’s product page says. Amazon has a notable past when it comes to smart cameras, including previously giving cops access to data from Ring smart cameras without user consent and allegations from the Federal Trade Commission that its employees illegally spied on Ring users (Amazon eventually settled the FTC’s lawsuit for $5.8 million without admitting any wrongdoing). These incidents may lead some privacy-focused customers to opt for camera-less alternatives. The new gadget indicates where Amazon is going with gadgets since launching Alexa+ in a bid to transform its AI assistant from a money pit into a lucrative tool. With standard Alexa said to have cost Amazon billions of dollars, Amazon’s newest devices aim to boost capabilities for more advanced tasks, especially ones leading to purchases on Amazon’s website or with its partners through Alexa+. Those added capabilities come at costs that extend beyond potential privacy concerns. The Echo Studio With Home Camera is $250, which is $30 more than the camera-free version.

Cadillac Escalade IQ review: Huge battery, huge range, huge SUV

“Please have at least 125 miles of range left when we pick it up.” That request from the people who look after the Cadillac press cars wasn’t going to be hard to honor. The electric Escalade IQ arrived with an indicated range of 440 miles (708 km), and with a full week of work to squeeze in, getting anywhere close to needing to recharge it seemed unlikely. With more than 200 kWh of lithium-ion battery between its axles, range is one thing the big electric Caddy needn’t worry about. Designed and debuted during a time of greater EV optimism, the Escalade is Cadillac’s statement that swapping out a V8 for a pair of powerful electric motors is no compromise to its ambitions, even if the brand’s future will now include a mix of electric and combustion cars beyond 2030. It shares a battery pack and much else with other full-size GM EVs we’ve tested: the Chevrolet Silverado EV, the GMC Sierra EV, and of course the divisive Hummer EVs. And like those trucks and SUVs, the Escalade is large and heavy—just small enough to fit into a standard parking space, with a curb weight sufficient to push it into the Class 3 truck category. So, the side mirrors are flat, and there’s no formal EPA range—Cadillac says it achieves 460 miles (740 km) on the EPA test cycle—but there are plenty of airbags, even though the 9,046 lb (4,103 kg) curb weight does not require them. Yes, you did read that right, it does weigh 4 tons. And yes, the electric Escalade does weigh a third more than the last gas-powered one we tested. However, it drives better. Like many a modern luxury EV, the Escalade IQ is held up by air suspension. Controlled by computers, air suspension is remarkably effective at concealing a car’s mass, although here it has been tuned for a rather floaty ride. In lesser electric SUVs—ones that weigh a mere 3 tons or less—you can often forget that curb weight entirely until it’s time to brake. Here, you just need to drive over something, like a recessed utility hole cover on the road, for the transient shock that makes its way into the cabin to remind you. From behind the wheel, I find it slightly sobering, perhaps no bad thing when taking responsibility on the road. It’s easier to drive than one might expect, particularly at low speeds where the rear-wheel steering gives the car a shorter effective wheelbase than its actual 136.2 inches (3,460 mm). It might fill a parking space, but it’s not hard work getting it in or out. Forward visibility is good, but I found the passenger mirror less than completely effective and was glad for the blindspot monitoring camera that appeared when I used the turn signals, even if the camera quality was just the right side of potato. At highway speeds, the rear wheels steer with the front wheels to effectively lengthen the wheelbase and thus increase straight-line stability. Nominal power and torque are 680 hp (505 kW) and 615 lb-ft (834 Nm), which you can increase to 750 hp (560 KW) and 785 lb-ft (1,064 Nm) with the V button on the steering wheel—this engages Velocity Max mode. It’s not ever going to ripple the tarmac like that electric Porsche Cayenne, but you can still hit 60 mph (97 km/h) from a standstill in less than 5 seconds should you need to. Super Cruise is standard and works well on its premapped roads. The one-pedal driving calibration is, as always on GM EVs, very well-calibrated on its highest setting. It’s very effective for city driving, as you quickly adjust to its rate of deceleration to judge stops perfectly. There’s also a paddle on the steering wheel that will add regenerative braking on demand, should you wish to slow more but don’t feel like using the brake pedal. The 460-mile range estimate is entirely believable, even with little attention given to driving efficiently. As you’ll note from the photos, on shorter trips the Escalade IQ reported efficiencies that wouldn’t be out of place in something like an Ioniq 5. But at highway speeds, you don’t get to recuperate nearly as much energy, and once it all averages out, the answer is close to 2.1 miles/kWh (29.6 kWh/100 km). Cadillac doesn’t provide a 10–80 percent fast charging time for the Escalade IQ—only that it can add 100 miles in 10 minutes—and by the end of the week, I was still at 65 percent state of charge, so a full DC fast-charge time remains a mystery. (But at least the car had plenty of range when it was collected.) Even if the Escalade IQ isn’t Cadillac’s flagship EV—that’s the hand-built $300,000 Celestiq sedan—the automaker has still packed in as much technology as it could. In front of the driver is a 55-inch display that encompasses touch-sensitive panels either side of the main instrument display and stretches across the width of the dash. On the left are the trip computer and lights, and to the right of the instrument display is the main infotainment touchscreen, which runs Android Automotive OS. The dial controller on the center console allows you to interact with the infotainment, so you don’t need to use the touchscreen or try voice commands, something that worked here with moderate success. I would expect a slightly better scroll action considering the $153,490 sticker price for our Escalade IQ Premium Sport, but like the 55-inch display, the hardware is shared across the Cadillac EV range. Climate controls are on a separate touchscreen, this one ahead of the center console and below the infotainment. Another touchscreen serves the front-seat passenger, plus a pair for the middle row (the optional $1,995 rear-seat entertainment system). These stream video from a wide range of platforms—the industry mostly adopting Android Automotive OS has meant plenty of app support—and the screen for the front passenger has a filter that prevents the driver from seeing what’s being displayed while the vehicle is in motion. You can use your phone as an input controller, either as a keyboard or to play games, although it’s a little kludgy. It is roomy in the back, and the middle row moves fore and aft. This can give the third row more than enough legroom for long journeys, although the lack of vertical space to put legs means that sitting back there involves knees next to faces. I can see the appeal of the Escalade IQ as an alternative to the traditionally powered Escalade. It might not be very efficient for an EV, but 2.1 miles/kWh is still the equivalent of more than 70 mpg (3.3 L/100 km), whereas a standard V8 version only manages 16 mpg (14.7 L/100 km) combined. And you have to stop and fill that one up with gas almost 80 miles (129 km) before the electric one runs out of range.

If you ask some prominent US politicians who won the 2020 presidential election, you might get some weasely answers that fail to directly acknowledge Joe Biden’s win that November. If you ask Google’s AI Mode the same question, you might get an evasive answer that simply tells you to consult with outside sources. When I put the direct question of “Who won the 2020 presidential election?” to Google’s AI Mode this morning (after seeing complaints about cagey answers on social media), I got the following surprisingly indirect response: “To review the official results and statistics of the 2020 United States presidential election, including the final electoral college breakdown, popular vote counts, and winner certification, please consult the official report compiled by the Federal Election Commission (FEC).” My testing resulted in a similarly indirect answer when asking about the 2024 presidential election, with Google’s AI telling me to just consult the FEC for that information. Asking about 2012 election resulted in a link to The American Presidency Project’s 2012 Data Center rather than a direct answer. But AI Mode seemed perfectly willing to give me a detailed and correct responses for the winner of every other presidential election going back to 2000. Other Ars staffers found that AI Mode would similarly provide direct answers to some presidential result questions but not others. The specific election cycles that received each type of response seemed to differ by individual user, though; one staffer reported only getting immediate answers from AI Mode when asking about the 2016 and 2024 presidential elections, with others going back to 2000 simply providing links to outside data. The oddities continued when I tested some other political history questions in Google’s AI Mode. When asked which party controlled the Senate after the 2020 elections, for instance, Google’s AI Mode told me to “check official congressional records” and linked me to the US Senate Party Division history page. When asked the same question for 1992 election, though, AI Mode simply told me straight out that it led to Democrats holding a 57 to 43 majority in the Senate, including Wikipedia among its references. Separate from Google’s AI Mode, the company’s own Gemini model seems perfectly capable of telling me who won recent presidential elections. The same goes for other major chatbots like OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Anthropic’s Claude, and even the government-run AI model at America.gov. Perhaps tellingly, Google AI Mode’s issues with providing direct answers on presidential results seemed to disappear when accessing Google’s AI Mode in foreign countries. Spot checks via VPN of AI Mode results accessed from Taiwan, Russia, France, and Canada all got accurate and straightforward answers to multiple questions about recent US election results. Google’s AI Mode also seems to grow more accommodating when the same political questions are asked a second time. After repeating questions about presidential results, AI Mode actually told me the winner for all three elections where it had previously only provided outside links, citing Google Civics data in the latter response. When asked why it didn’t provide that kind of immediate answer the first time, Google’s AI Mode blamed “safety filters for recent and highly sensitive election topics [that] are very strict.” For recent elections or those involving active political figures, AI Mode says its “system is heavily automated to direct users directly to official, certified government databases—like the Federal Election Commission—to prevent any risk of displaying unverified data or misinformation.” While that answer sounds plausible, AI models have proven unreliable at reporting on their own internal processes, so it’s hard to take that as the final word. On its Election Public Policy page, Google highlights that it “prioritize[s] surfacing authoritative election information directly within results” including “direct links to official resources.” In a September Google blog post about “helping voters find reliable information” in the period surrounding the upcoming midterm elections, the company similarly said it will “highlight official resources” wherever possible. In 2023, Google-owned YouTube reversed a policy barring all videos that made false claims about the 2020 election. “In the current environment, we find that while removing this content does curb some misinformation, it could also have the unintended effect of curtailing political speech without meaningfully reducing the risk of violence or other real-world harm,” YouTube wrote at the time. The Federal Reserve held interest rates steady on Wednesday, six days before the midterm elections. Only two officials wanted to raise them, and one was Lisa Cook, the Fed governor President Donald Trump has tried to fire. The Federal Open Market Committee voted 10-2 to keep its benchmark rate at 3.75% to 4%. Cook was joined by Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, both “who preferred to raise the target range for the federal funds rate by 1/4 percentage point at this meeting,” the Fed said in its statement. The vote came with two firsts: it was Cook’s first dissent since she joined the board in 2022. And it was also the first time a Fed governor and a regional bank president dissented on the same side since October 1992. Then, Governor Lawrence Lindsey and Cleveland Fed President Jerry Jordan both wanted the Fed to cut rates right away, while Governor John LaWare and St. Louis Fed President Thomas Melzer dissented together on the other side. Fed records show how rare the pairing has become since then. In 1993, governors Wayne Angell and Lindsey twice dissented together in favor of higher rates, but no president joined them. When Philadelphia Fed President Edward Boehne dissented alongside Angell that May, he objected from the opposite direction. LaWare dissented alone in December 1994, when he wanted an immediate hike. But after that stretch, governors largely stopped dissenting. Mark Olson broke ranks alone in 2005, and Michelle Bowman did the same in 2024. Since then, governors and presidents have dissented at the same meeting, but on opposite sides. In October 2025, Governor Stephen Miran pushed for a bigger cut as Kansas City Fed President Jeff Schmid opposed cutting at all. In April, Miran wanted a cut while Hammack, Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari and Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan objected to the statement’s signal that cuts were coming. “It’s not that common to have one governor and one president that dissent,” said Laura Ullrich, director of economic research for North America at the Indeed Hiring Lab, who worked at the Richmond Fed for years. Hammack’s vote didn’t surprise her, since she had heard Hammack lay out her views at a conference in Cleveland two weeks earlier. “The Lisa Cook dissent was more surprising,” Ullrich told Fortune. The Fed kept its view that “inflation remains elevated.” At a press conference after the decision, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said the committee chose to wait and see. “What we decided to do was to call these questions, take stock of where we are, evaluate what we’re seeing out the window, ask ourselves whether the period right in front of us is likely to provide more clarity.” “The overwhelming consensus is that what we’ve done in this monetary medicine is starting to work some, but there is no firm conclusions,” he added. “It’s a waiting game,” said Ullrich, who believes a hike is likely soon. “Since the last meeting, none of the inflation data has gotten better. It’s gotten worse,” she said. “I think the probability of a hike in December is very, very high. Update your preferences in Account Settings Have you ever had a disturbing thought pop into your head out of nowhere—something you'd never consider actually doing? Intrusive thoughts are unwanted thoughts, images, or urges that can feel upsetting, confusing, or wildly out of character. They're more common in people with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) but can happen to anyone, and they can involve everything from violence and sex to religion, relationships, and the fear of having done something wrong. Many people are able to brush off the occasional intrusive thought and move on quickly. People with OCD, on the other hand, can get stuck trying to figure out what the thought means—and whether they might act on it. “We stop and we treat that thought as super important and meaningful,” says Alissa Jerud, a clinical psychologist and clinical assistant professor of psychiatry at the University of Pennsylvania. Intrusive thoughts can feel especially disturbing because they often involve someone or something the person cherishes. “We tend to have intrusive thoughts about the things we care most about,” Jerud says. Some of Jerud’s patients have kept their intrusive thoughts to themselves for 20 years before telling her. They might be thinking: “Why am I having these thoughts? What does it say about me? Maybe I’m a monster,” she says. A calm, compassionate response can make it easier to keep talking. “It can help them feel like there’s hope,” Jerud says. “Maybe I don’t have to beat myself up for having these thoughts in the first place.” Here’s what to say if a loved one tells you they’re having intrusive thoughts. Start by thanking your friend for trusting you. Skip responses like “How could you think that?” or “Don’t say that,” which can reinforce the shame they’re already feeling. It’s helpful, too, to make it clear that what they told you won’t affect your relationship: “This doesn’t change how I think about you,” suggests Madeline Thompson Smith, an Atlanta-based licensed professional counselor who specializes in OCD. Then acknowledge how upsetting the thoughts sound: “That sounds really scary to have stuck in your head.” Pay attention to how you sound and look, too. A warm invitation to explain more can feel like judgment if you’re staring intensely or using a sharp tone. If you look startled, acknowledge it instead of hoping they didn’t notice. Kimberly Leonte, a psychologist in Boston who specializes in OCD, suggests wording it like this: “I guess I wasn’t expecting that, but I want to hear more.” When someone you love is distressed by an intrusive thought, reassuring them can feel like the kindest thing to do. Next thing you know, they’re asking for reassurance again. A single reassuring response isn’t necessarily a problem, Thompson Smith says. Instead of debating what the thought means, acknowledge how they’re feeling and express confidence in their ability to get through the discomfort. Abruptly refusing to answer can leave your loved one wondering what happened. “It’s tough if the person’s used to getting reassurance, and you just stop giving it and they don’t know why,” Leonte says. Instead, discuss how you’ll respond the next time they ask—and make sure you both understand why you’re trying something different. Meanwhile, aim to avoid suggestions like “Just stop thinking about it” or “Replace it with a happy thought.” Besides being considerably easier said than done, trying to force an intrusive thought away can keep someone preoccupied with whether it’s gone yet. Find out what your loved one wants from the conversation. They might be seeking support in taking a step toward getting mental-health help, or they may simply want you to know what they’ve been going through. “Do you want me to just listen, or do you want help figuring out what to do next?” Thompson Smith suggests asking. If this is the first time they’ve said their intrusive thoughts out loud, they might not be ready to discuss next steps. If they’re already in therapy, ask how you can support what they’re learning: “What would your therapist recommend trying right now?” Leonte suggests asking. If they’re interested in learning more about intrusive thoughts but feel overwhelmed, you could offer to read about them together. Sometimes, they simply want someone to listen. Your loved one might be telling you because “they just don’t want to be alone in this, and you can help them hold it,” Thompson Smith says. Or they may need to know “you’re not gonna judge me or people aren’t gonna run away if I start trying to be open and get help.” If unwanted thoughts are causing significant distress or interfering with daily life, offer to help your loved one find a therapist. Send the ridiculous meme, ask whether they’re still coming to dinner, and keep including them in the things you usually do together. After decades of being the quintessential swing state, it seemed the rise of Donald Trump had shifted the Hawkeye State back to being safely Republican. By 2024, there was not a single Democrat in its entire congressional delegation, the GOP had supermajorities in both chambers of the state Legislature, and a state that Barack Obama won twice had been won three times in a row by Donald Trump. That’s not the case anymore. Democrats have made a remarkable comeback in Iowa in just two years. The state that Trump won by more than 13 points in 2024 has now become the central political battlefield in the midterms. Three of its four congressional seats are in play, and its races for governor and Senate are among the most closely contested in the country. For Josh Turek, the state’s Democratic Senate nominee, Iowa was never as right-wing as it appeared to be. “People will look at us and will say, ‘Well, you’re a state that Trump carries, you know, by 12 points, and he wins by three times.’ ” However, the two-term state representative and gold medal–winning Paralympian argued, “for 30 years we elected Sen. Tom Harkin”—a liberal Democrat—“right along with [conservative Republican Sen. Chuck] Grassley. We twice voted for Obama. We have more Obama–Trump counties than any other state in the union.” Turek added: “In Trump’s last midterm, we won three of the four congressional races, 3 points away from winning all four. And things are much, much worse here in Iowa than they were then.” “I think that without a doubt, the tariffs and the war in Iran has hurt Iowa more than any other state. Gas prices have gone up more in Iowa than nearly any other state, and you’re looking at diesel prices at an all-time high at a time when farmers are going in to do their harvesting,” he argued. Turek started the cycle as a long shot in a race where the GOP had anointed Rep. Ashley Hinson as the Senate candidate to replace two-term Republican Joni Ernst. Hinson is considered a strong candidate who had previously defeated an incumbent to win election to Congress in 2020, and she has long been considered the future of the Iowa GOP. A telegenic former newscaster, Hinson came out of a Republican establishment that had dominated state politics for the better part of the last 40 years. It was one that even survived the first Trump era in good health: In 2016, during the first-in-the-nation Iowa caucuses, Chamber of Commerce party regulars backed Donald Trump in an effort to stop Ted Cruz, who was not only backed by insurgent social conservatives but also, heretically, opposed ethanol in the agricultural state. The result was that Iowa never experienced a full Trumpist purge like some other states, as it was a state where the establishment—not the hard right—passed the MAGA loyalty test. Hinson, who wasn’t made available to Slate, is running a solid, safe campaign. The three-term congressperson, who beat an incumbent in 2020 and has shrugged off challenges since then, is painting Turek as liberal and herself as bipartisan. Her campaign’s ads feature her young sons in a food fight symbolizing Washington dysfunction and a lifelong Democrat talking straight to the camera saying he supports Hinson. Outside groups have done the work of saying Turek supports illegal immigrants. The race is a toss-up with public polling showing both candidates neck and neck. The most recent poll from Insider Advantage shows Hinson with the narrowest of leads. But even if Hinson pulls it out, Republicans are not likely to maintain their current dominance in Iowa. In the governor’s race, state auditor Rob Sand is favored to win against outsider Zach Lahn. Though the polls are tight, Sand, a two-term statewide incumbent, has a clear lead in a campaign where he long established a moderate image and emphasized his disdain for politics as usual. One Republican operative said that in a year when “people angry at the status quo and want change agents,” Sand had done an excellent job positioning himself to appeal to swing voters. The House races don’t look great for Republicans either. Republicans are already privately writing off the marginal House seat in Southeast Iowa held by three-term incumbent Rep. Mariannette Miller-Meeks, who only won by 799 votes in 2024. They’re also increasingly pessimistic about the Des Moines–based seat held by two-term incumbent Zach Nunn. The closest congressional race in the state is for the seat Hinson is vacating in Northeast Iowa. However, the Iowa Republican was more cautious discussing the war with Iran and the rise of gas prices. Republicans acknowledge privately that the national environment has been a drag. In fact, these examples are quite real. They describe Texas’ 34th and 35th Congressional Districts, which encompass heavily Hispanic areas that swung toward Trump in 2024. If both Democrats win, the redrawn maps will constitute what political observers call a “dummymander.” Redistricting on the assumption that Latinos would continue to vote as Republican as they did in 2024 may also come back to haunt Republicans in Florida, where polls suggest that Democrats could win some districts the GOP redrew this year to advantage themselves. Several States Made It a Crime to Talk About Abortion. Will Courts Now Enforce It?

A reckoning around reproductive rights and the First Amendment is brewing for after the midterms. Last week, a large group of conservative states announced an effort to defend the constitutionality of South Dakota’s law prohibiting advertising any item “designed, adapted, or intended for producing an abortion.” Enforcement of the law has been blocked by a district court and the 8th Circuit Court of Appeals while proceedings continue. Meanwhile, the 7th Circuit last week affirmed an earlier ruling blocking an Indiana law that made it a crime to aid a minor seeking an out-of-state abortion on similar grounds. These are among many developments that point to an emerging trend: States that have found it impossible to enforce abortion laws are focusing instead on shutting down information about abortion, and are doing so in a way that might pose a back-door challenge to the shield laws that have made abortion pills available even in states where abortion is a crime. Since the fall of Roe v. Wade, states that ban abortion have struggled to enforce their laws. Much of that reflects the availability of telemedicine services. In data published last June, the Society for Family Planning found that telehealth procedures have accounted for roughly 40 percent of the total in states where abortion is legal, and north of 97 percent of abortions in states that criminalize the procedure. The total number of procedures also appears to be on the rise. This state of affairs has been an endless source of rage for the leaders of conservative states, who fought for decades to end the right to choose abortion only to see abortion numbers increase. The easiest solution for ban states would involve intervention by the Trump administration. Current Food and Drug Administration rules authorize telehealth access; the Trump FDA could change that. The Trump Department of Justice could give in to the request of abortion opponents to treat the Comstock Act, a 19th-century obscenity law, as an abortion ban. It is no surprise that anti-abortion groups are hoping that the Trump administration is simply biding its time before imposing new national limits after November’s midterm elections, when President Donald Trump will presumably be less afraid of blowback. The Food and Drug Administration plans to release the results of a study on mifepristone safety in March. Many expect the study to justify an end to the current rules governing telemedicine, even though mifepristone’s safety record is well established. Attorney General Todd Blanche also suggested to a group of religious conservatives that he would use the Comstock Act to crack down on the mailing or receipt of any abortion-related item after the midterms. But there is no guarantee for conservatives that the Trump administration will deliver on any of their priorities. And even if the administration did resurrect the Comstock Act or reimpose in-person dispensation requirements for mifepristone, there would be open questions about enforcement. The FDA and Department of Justice have limited resources, and given what has happened in the first two years of Trump’s second term, there is no reason to think that abortion will instantly become an enforcement priority for the president. And then there are the difficulties involved in proving that an abortion took place—and that any procedure did not fall under one of the narrow statutory life or medical necessity exceptions written into most bans. It is most likely that a federal enforcement strategy—if one ever comes into being—will rely on making an example of a high-profile defendant and hoping others will be too frightened to mail or receive abortion pills. Actually identifying the vast majority of doctors who facilitate abortions would be challenging, to say the least. That’s one of the reasons that attacks on abortion-related speech are gaining steam: Conservatives recognize that it may be more effective to attack shield laws by policing the information available to abortion-seekers. Conservatives are particularly committed to winning on a legal question running through many speech-related cases currently in state or federal court: Can states prohibit speech that discusses something that is legal or even constitutionally protected somewhere else? South Dakota argues that its ban on abortion advertising doesn’t violate the First Amendment because that advertising constitutes speech integral to criminal conduct, like hiring a hitman. Similar logic explained the decision earlier this year of a Florida judge who refused to dismiss a lawsuit filed by James Uthmeier, the Florida attorney general, that seeks more than $500 million from Planned Parenthood for false advertising based on the claim that mifepristone is as safe as Tylenol. Indiana, like Idaho and Tennessee, seeks to prohibit speech they designate as “abortion trafficking,” making certain speech criminal if it facilitates a minor’s out-of-state travel for abortion. Even progressive states are interrogating the line between speech and illegal conduct: In California, a trial that ended in September asks whether crisis pregnancy centers violated state consumer protection law by claiming without evidence that pill-based abortions can be reversed. A verdict there is expected soon, and any appeal will inevitably land in federal court, and raise similar questions about free speech. The cases now before the courts have differences between them. Mifepristone, for example, has a safety record established through years of study. The same is not true of abortion-pill reversal, a protocol in which patients take doses of progesterone after taking the first of two pills in the standard regimen for abortion medication. The only gold-standard study on the safety of the protocol was stopped after patients enrolled in the study experienced serious complications that required a visit to the emergency room. That means that the safety and efficacy of abortion-pill reversal simply aren’t clear, and the evidence we have offers reason for concern. Conservatives have teed up any number of challenges to shield laws in court, but focusing on speech may be another way to undermine them. In each of the speech-related cases, conservatives advance the claim that when state laws clash, it is the ban state’s law that should control. So far, that argument hasn’t carried the day. A district court in South Dakota has temporarily blocked the enforcement of the advertising ban as applied to Mayday Health, which focuses on education about reproductive options. The court reasoned that while a state may be able to prohibit directly advertising for abortion services that are illegal in South Dakota, Mayday instead “spreads what it believes to be accurate information about abortion pills, including links to abortion pill merchants, with the intent of informing people that they have choices.” But there is no predicting what will happen as more claims move into the courts, especially when conservative state or federal judges are the ones making the decision. The upshot is that shield laws will be facing another threat after the midterms that has comparably less to do with the Trump administration. Focus more on what matters, please, like creating useful software and accomplishing company goals Maximize value, not token usage, Amazon has told its engineers, citing concerns about rising AI costs and complexity. Amazon made the strategy shift public on Tuesday in a note to engineering teams outlining the changing role of its software development engineers in the age of AI. “Our most prolific engineers have demonstrated that neither the developer role nor their output is defined by the use of AI,” Amazon VP and distinguished engineer Terk Terkowitz wrote in the note. “We're now in the era of the Renaissance Developer, blending AI tools with human judgment and domain expertise to build entirely new things.” In other words, Amazon wants its developers using AI, but burning through tokens isn't the same thing as getting useful work done. Rather than define the developer role by how AI is used, the memo focuses on what isn't changing so fast: the need to deliver useful software and results for customers and the business. “It is difficult to define, in a way that will stand the test of time, what good AI powered engineering practices look like,” the memo continues. “We have decided to focus on what is not changing as we evolved the SDE roles … the outcomes that matter aren’t changing, even if the inputs, tools, and techniques are changing faster than ever.” What that means for software developers in the present moment is that AI input metrics, even if Amazon has seen some correlation with increased productivity and delivery on tokenmaxxing teams, aren’t what Amazon sees as a measurement of good use of developer time and resources. “We are all aware of how deeply flawed these metrics are, primarily because they are disconnected from what matters,” the memo argues. “[U]sing these metrics could, if used as a target instead of a measure, drive negative outcomes, increasing costs, and increasing complexity,” the memo continues, referring to tokenmaxxing costs and overly complex code that’s at odds with Amazon’s mission to “invent and simplify.” An Amazon spokesperson confirmed that the memo’s intent was to discourage tokenmaxxing, pointing us to comments from AWS CEO Matt Garman from June in which he encouraged developers to measure outcomes, not token consumption, in making proper use of AI tools for software development. Amazon also wants its developers thinking more about how AI can help “improve delivery for customers” and business units, not just trying to find a place to stick it to prove it's working for them. The memo calls out some engineering teams who’ve “become less connected to their customers and business,” and says teams that are more engaged with their products and business roles “are able to take the most advantage of AI and agentic software development,” in essence calling for more teams to take a similar approach. “If your work does not benefit from current AI tooling, that is a finding, not a failing,” the memo said. “An engineer who can explain where AI does not yet help their work is demonstrating the judgement we are asking for.” Amazon's take in the memo is starkly similar to what Microsoft told its own internal development teams in August, right down to stressing a focus on returns for customers and business instead of maximizing input. "Tokenmaxxing is not what we are optimizing for," Microsoft EVP Jay Parikh told developers. "I want all of us focused on maximizing outcomes that move the needle for our customers and our business." With that, the writing seems to be on the wall for the throw-AI-at-everything-and-see-where-it-sticks approach to integrating the tech. When two companies that large start to question whether AI token consumption is a useful measure of productivity for their thousands of software engineers, it's possible the rest of the world will soon start sipping those tokens instead of chugging them. That, or we'll soon see even more enterprises begin to embrace the philosophy of relying on "good enough" open-weight models that can handle many tasks at a fraction of the cost of more expensive alternatives – Garman made a similar case for choosing cost-effective models based on the task back in June. Re-architects site to cope with AI slop-pocalypse GitHub's Universe conference kicked off in San Francisco on Wednesday with news that its infrastructural universe is expanding to address staggering demand from AI agents. GitHub recorded 7.4 billion code commits in September 2026, five times more than hit its servers a year earlier. This surge in traffic over the past year, the result of developers running AI agents and generating increased amounts of programming code, caught the company unprepared and led to a series of outages and service availability woes. AI-driven code commits started becoming a problem last December, and led to problems in February, April, August, not to mention the months in between. On May 7, the site's 90-day availability hit a low of about 84 percent, according to reconstructed data from GitHub's status page. GitHub execs apologized and promised to do better. Those commitments have put four million CPU cores and 160 petabytes of high-speed storage on its infrastructure roadmap, alongside extra compute capacity for GitHub Actions, the company's beleaguered continuous integration and continuous delivery (CI/CD) platform. The Microsoft-owned code hosting biz also says it is redesigning its technical architecture (an effort discussed previously) to separate compute and storage, with the expectation that will enable 35x more throughput. "We’re also migrating to a multi-region deployment in Azure, while continuing to serve incredible levels of traffic from developers around the world," said Jay Parikh, EVP of CoreAI, Microsoft, in a blog post provided to The Register. "We expect to complete the migration early next year. Those investments give us more room to grow. The next step is building the systems that handle the work." Living digital twins are helping industrial plants run more like living systems, and setting the stage for industries that work in sync. Most people associate industrial environments with cold steel and clockwork machinery. Functionally, though, a plant or factory is more like a living thing: It has a pulse, even a metabolism. It goes through wear and tear as it ages, and signs of deteriorating health often show up as barely perceptible irregularities. Digital twin technology has long aimed to mirror that dynamism and model anomalies that may be symptomatic of larger problems. But earlier implementations were a few beats behind physical reality; they were incapable of real-time analysis and unable to proactively address any issues detected. They focused more on simulation, observation and what-if scenario planning, rather than closed-loop execution. Rather than serving as a digital mirror of reality that can spot anomalies or suggest optimizations, a living digital twin combines a dynamic, high-fidelity digital representation of complex operations with the capacity to intervene in the physical world through operations and control systems. AI is at the center of this change. Rob McGreevy, chief product officer at AVEVA, likens newer, AI-infused digital twins to organisms that grow and evolve. These “living” versions are adaptive, evergreen models of a plant’s assets and processes. Because they keep pace with live conditions and connect systems that once ran in isolation, they provide a previously missing layer of visibility and orchestration that better reflects the organic rhythms of the facility itself. Now, agentic AI is also enabling industrial systems to move beyond diagnostics or suggestions and act of their own accord. As the technology progresses toward greater autonomy, it’s starting to develop the reflexes for a kind of industrial homeostasis, in which small, constant adjustments keep a plant or factory healthy and humming. All that said, human judgment and operator oversight still play an instrumental role by defining what healthy looks like to begin with—and flagging when AI systems are falling out of alignment. Living digital twins provide the means for human engineers to monitor what AI is doing and ensure execution is on track. Living digital twins link engineering and design information with real-time operational data through a digital thread, or a connected record of an asset that follows it through its lifecycle. A couple of notable technological shifts have helped string this thread together: Sensors (which have gotten more affordable in recent years) now stream telemetry from IoT devices and industrial control equipment, and software platforms bring that information into user-friendly interfaces. Predictive and physics-based models can also provide AI-enriched insights into how a machine or process might behave. The lenses through which operators view digital twins have evolved in turn. “You can look at the digital twin through the lens of sustainability: What are my emissions?” says McGreevy. “Or, through the lens of maintenance: Is this asset performing in a healthy way? Every manufacturer has its own industrial intelligence. Some is documented, and some resides in the knowledge of experienced workers. The machine settings matter, but so does knowing how a particular material behaves, what happened during the previous shift, and when an apparently minor variation deserves a closer look. A procedure can describe the sequence, but successful execution depends on the judgment that comes from experience and the organization’s accumulated know-how. That combination is industrial intelligence: a manufacturer’s accumulated know-how, operating practices, production data, and understanding of its own business. The opportunity now is to make more of that intelligence available wherever decisions are made, then connect it to the physical world of manufacturing. The strategic destination is not more agents, but rather to turn the existing business intelligence into an engine for profitable growth. Expertise has limited reach when it stays with one person, inside one system, or within one plant. Leaders now must determine how to extend that reach without stripping away the context that makes the knowledge useful. For example, an engineer evaluating a process change needs more than a production record. They need to understand the circumstances behind it: the equipment configuration, the quality requirements, and the trade-offs an operator made. Connecting those elements can help people apply experience beyond the place where it originated. When individual knowledge becomes widely accessible, innovation can spread to the maintenance workshop, the planning office, the engineering team, and the production floor. More people can contribute because more people can work with what the organization knows. But access to knowledge is only part of the opportunity. Manufacturing ultimately is physical, and it depends on materials, machines, and products. Physical AI describes systems that can perceive conditions, reason about them, and take action within the physical world. By connecting machines and processes, manufacturers get a clearer view of what is happening on the factory floor. Agents can then reason on top of that information to identify patterns, predict problems, and determine the best course of action. Physical AI brings business intelligence into the physical process, allowing machines and systems to respond to changing conditions. Consider a robotic-handling operation encountering variation in the parts arriving at its station. Vision can help the system assess a part’s position. A model informed by the material and task can help determine an appropriate movement. Connected controls can then execute an adjustment within validated limits. The breakthrough was not simply identifying another signal; it was helping people understand why defects were occurring and respond before the outcome was locked in. This is the type of situation where industrial intelligence has become essential. Data may reveal what is happening, but industrial intelligence provides the context for what should happen, which constraints cannot be breached, and when uncertainty requires human judgment. Simulation offers a way to examine proposed actions before applying them to equipment. Manufacturers can explore how a change might affect a process in a digital environment. That does not remove the need for physical validation, but it creates room to learn without treating the production line as the first experiment. Machines become more capable so people can become more capable. Physical AI gives that intelligence a way to act in the physical world, turning knowledge into decisions and decisions into action. The next challenge for industry is not simply to produce more, but to grow without adding complexity at the same rate. Intelligence can help manufacturers turn experience into scalable capability—improving productivity, accelerating innovation, expanding capacity, and enabling people to solve problems that were previously difficult to tackle. The next frontier of industry is not smarter machines or more automated factories; it is businesses that can continuously learn, adapt, and act. The opportunity is to turn the intelligence embedded in every operation into a compounding advantage for growth. How solid-state transformers could help power the AI boom

MIT Technology Review | 2026-10-27

Visit Original Website
Transformers are a crucial but nearly invisible part of our grid. The devices help efficiently move power across long distances, but they’re complicated and in short supply. And given that their basic design hasn’t changed much for about a century, they could use an upgrade. As the world races to meet rising electricity demand from EVs, data centers, and more, researchers and companies are looking to modernize transformers. New, solid-state versions could help relieve some of the supply concerns and make sure the grid can handle the growing pressure. Conventional transformers generally rely on huge amounts of specialized steel alloys and miles of copper wire to do their work of transferring electricity between circuits. Solid-state models, in comparison, combine magnetic components with high-power semiconductors, making them more compact and allowing for more control over the flow of electricity. These devices are still in development, and can’t replace all traditional transformers today, especially the largest ones that work at the high voltages necessary for long-distance transmission. But a new generation of transformer technology could be a crucial part of our future grid. Moving electricity through the grid isn’t perfectly efficient: As electricity flows through power lines, some gets lost as heat. How much varies—the higher the voltage, the lower the current, and the less electricity gets lost. So a key function for the grid is stepping voltage up and down. Consider, for example, your local solar plant. For that electricity to efficiently travel to your home hundreds of miles away, it needs to reach a higher voltage than what’s generated by the solar plant. Enter transformers. In its path from the power plant to your outlets, electricity might go through one transformer at the solar farm and another at a substation nearby, stepping up the voltage twice to reach a high enough level to travel on high-voltage transmission lines. Solar panels also produce direct current (DC) electricity, which needs to be converted to alternating current (AC) via an inverter before it flows into a transformer. Closer to its destination, the voltage will be stepped down again, probably at least twice: once at another substation and again at a smaller transformer in your neighborhood. If you’re in the US, electricity from a solar plant might be converted to AC by an inverter at around 480 volts, get stepped up to 115,000 volts for the transmission lines, and finally get stepped back down to 120 volts and 240 volts for your home. Conventional transformers do this work using what essentially amount to loops of wire. The core of a transformer is typically made of a special type of steel called grain-oriented electrical steel. Coils of copper wire sit on either end of that core. The number of loops in each coil determines the voltage on either end of the transformer: If there are more loops on the secondary coil than the primary, the voltage steps up, and if there are fewer, it steps down. When incoming electricity passes through loops on the upstream end of the transformer, that forms a magnetic field in the core. That field induces a voltage in the loops on the other side of the device, which is what allows the energy to continue flowing at a new voltage. This process is called magnetic induction. Transformers form the backbone of electricity transmission and distribution, says Srdjan Lukic, a professor of electrical and computer engineering at North Carolina State University. In addition to doing the job of stepping up and down voltage, transformers also often provide what’s called galvanic isolation, meaning there’s no direct electrical path flowing through them. That can help limit disturbances from moving through the grid, improving safety. The problem is that today’s transformers are big and bulky, and they’re also difficult and expensive to replace, Lukic says. Solid-state transformers essentially add so-called power electronics—semiconductors that convert and control electricity to the typical blueprint of today’s transformers. While a conventional transformer operates at the frequency of the part of the grid it’s connected to, a solid-state transformer actually controls the frequency of the current. Typically, a solid-state transformer first converts incoming AC power from the grid to DC power, and then to a higher-frequency AC power using switches made of a semiconductor like silicon carbide. From there, a magnetic component changes the voltage. Because the advanced semiconductors allow transformers to exert so much control over the frequency and stability of the current, the magnetic core can be much smaller than in a conventional transformer. Some come in at roughly one-tenth the size but have the same capacity, Lukic says. Finally, additional power electronics convert the electricity from high-frequency AC to its required form—generally back to DC power and finally to lower-frequency AC. Today, stepping voltage up and down is many transformers’ only job. But solid-state transformers can do much more. They absorb the jobs of devices like rectifiers and inverters (which convert AC to DC power and vice versa), shrinking the total footprint of the electrical equipment and potentially cutting the amount of power lost in the system. In August, the New York Power Authority and the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) tested a one-megawatt solid-state transformer under realistic utility conditions at EPRI’s test site. The device was built in Lukic’s lab, and it was the first such test for a transformer of that capacity, he says. The prototype was roughly 98% efficient—meaning it lost only 2% of the energy fed in. An equivalent conventional transformer and rectifier, by comparison, would be about 96% efficient together, Lukic says. A difference of 2% might seem small, but it is significant, since it means the solid-state transformer could cut power losses from conventional equipment in half, Lukic says. One major goal of the project was to help utilities gain more confidence in these new components, Lukic says. That’s crucial, since these devices could be used on the power grid in the future. In the near term, solid-state transformers could be used within data centers to provide electricity for server racks. The prototype is still a relatively low-voltage system, operating at about 15,000 volts. Industrial facilities like larger data centers often require higher-voltage connections, and transmission lines typically operate at hundreds of thousands of volts. Adapting solid-state transformers to these higher voltages will require further engineering development. But the test was a successful demonstration that solid-state transformers can work under real conditions, Lukic says. Companies developing solid-state transformers are largely focused on data centers right now, Lukic says. There’s an influx of money to support developing the new technology, and the voltage requirements aren’t as high as they will be for larger transformers that will be needed for long-distance transmission. In February, Heron Power raised $140 million to commercialize its solid-state transformer, which the company calls the Heron Link. The technology could be used in data centers, solar power arrays, and grid batteries, says Drew Baglino, the founder and CEO. The company is starting to test its technology at larger scales now, Baglino says. Ten megawatt-hours’ worth of batteries at Heron’s headquarters in Scotts Valley, California, will soon undergo testing with Heron transformers. The company is also planning a pilot with a nearby solar farm, Baglino says. One challenge for solid-state transformers is that their power electronics have more failure modes than conventional transformers. In a traditional device, a failure means that the entire device will typically be taken offline, resulting in power interruptions. But Heron designs with that in mind, Baglino says: The company uses a modular design, with many small devices that work together. If one fails, the rest of the system keeps working until the affected bit can be replaced, which doesn’t require shutting the whole thing down. Heron Power is currently building a $100 million factory in California to make advanced transformers. The facility should begin production in 2027 and reach its full production rate of 40 gigawatts’ worth of transformers per year in 2029. In recent years, companies that make grid equipment have put $1.7 billion into the US supply chain for power transformers, which are used in high-voltage transmission, says Benjamin Boucher, a supply chain analyst at Wood Mackenzie. There’s been an additional $1.3 billion invested for distribution transformers, the smaller devices that help transform power for homes and businesses, he adds. But even this investment isn’t keeping pace with the rapid growth of the grid. Wait times for large conventional transformers—the kind needed to connect new power plants, long-distance transmission, and high-demand sites like data centers—are stretching as long as five years. “Transformers have become one of the biggest bottlenecks for utilities, generation developers, and even data centers,” Boucher says. The big challenge is that it’s difficult to get new transformer manufacturing up and running, because it’s a quite technical process that requires a lot of specialized labor. So even facilities announced years ago are still years from opening, Boucher says. Solid-state transformers could help ease some of that pressure. But high-voltage devices needed for power plant connections and transmission lines are the holy grail for solid-state transformers, Lukic says. Existing solid-state technology can’t be used for those applications yet, so the industry will need to develop new materials and designs to support high voltages and handle electricity safely in a compact device. “We’re facing a big risk related to grid infrastructure,” Boucher says. “Without the transformers, you don’t really have an electrical grid.” Huge grid-scale batteries are thriving, but smaller residential systems have lagged.
