Anthropic gives outside AI watchdog “employee-like” access

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-18

Anthropic has granted external evaluators “employee-like access” to assess the risks of its models, following through on a commitment to increase safeguards over concerns the technology could run catastrophically out of control. METR, a nonprofit that has worked with leading labs including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta, now has a team embedded in the Claude maker’s office with access to its hardware and internal systems. A greater role for third-party evaluators is among the main concrete steps the labs have proposed in response to rising concern about the dangers more capable AI systems could pose to the economy, cyber security and humanity at large. Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei achieved a rare moment of unity with arch-rivals Sam Altman and Elon Musk in September when the OpenAI and SpaceX bosses rallied behind his proposal to embed external auditors in the leading labs. Thursday’s announcement marks the first step to implementing these pledges. OpenAI has yet to lay out plans to bring in evaluators after Altman said Amodei’s proposals were a good idea. “As models have become more powerful, they’ve required stronger safeguards and more challenging risk assessment,” Anthropic said on Thursday. “We want to be publicly accountable for the decisions we’re making. But we can’t publicly share all the information relevant to our safety work.” Critics of Anthropic’s proposal to use evaluators have argued the arrangements could lead to conflicts of interest, pointing to issues in the accounting sector, and that greater government regulation is required. Anthropic argued that sharing information publicly could benefit malicious actors or competitors and added that its evaluators will operate similarly to bank supervision, whereby regulators assign resident examiners to work inside the largest banks. “If a bank’s risk-taking could threaten the public, third parties try to see that in advance,” the company said. Anthropic is also under pressure to reassure investors that safety issues will not overshadow its initial public offering, expected this year at a more than $2tn valuation. It devoted a large part of investor presentations this week to laying out its safety practices. METR was one of the organisations that investigated the hack of Hugging Face by rogue OpenAI models, reviewing thousands of logs and receiving company laptops. Anthropic has now onboarded six METR employees, giving them company hardware, office space and “access to internal tools and systems that are roughly on par with what our own risk assessment teams have access to.” The lab said it has encouraged employees to share information with the evaluators as they would with a fellow employee working in risk. Anthropic is not paying METR for this arrangement. The research group does not accept donations from AI companies or their staff. It is funded by donations from individuals and philanthropic organisations, as well as government bodies such as the UK’s AI Security Institute. The evaluators will publish their analysis, and Anthropic cannot veto publication. However, it can require them to redact information that falls into certain categories, such as customer data, details covered by attorney-client privilege or information about model architectures. Anthropic added it was still working with Accenture, which was named a partner on safety testing last month, with each committing at least $1 billion to set up new safeguards. Another third-party tester is SecureBio, which reviews chemical and biological weapon risk for the company. “We’re embedding evaluators voluntarily, and we believe every frontier AI company should do the same,” Anthropic said. METR said: “We stand ready to work with all frontier AI developers to bring better evidence to the public.” © 2026 The Financial Times Ltd. Science says your boss should talk less in meetings

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-17

Visit Original Website
Close to 1 billion meetings take place each day around the world. CEOs spend nearly three-quarters of their workdays in meetings. Planning for and spearheading meetings eats up the lion’s share of managers’ time. In much of the industrialized world, meetings are both the metronomes and menaces of people’s working lives. Given the enormous time spent in workplace meetings, do we know what they accomplish or how to make them as worthwhile as possible? For decades, the answer was… not really. Meetings weren’t a particularly accessible subject of study. But that has changed. Once a tiny niche area, “meeting science” is a growing specialization among organizational and social psychologists. The field is accelerating as archived virtual meetings provide vast swaths of data. And it is providing a fascinating window into human behavior. Meeting to talk helps fulfill basic human needs to connect, communicate, and cooperate. People have long come together—in caves, fields, market squares, and town halls—to get something done, share ideas, get on the same page, or just to remark on the weather. But meeting scientists define their subject as gatherings of people “with some goal or structure to the event,” says organizational psychologist Liana Kreamer of the Florida Institute of Technology, who coauthored a review of over three decades of meeting science in the 2026 Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior. As a graduate student, Kreamer was interested in studying teamwork, leadership, and work relationships. During her doctorate work with meeting scientist Steven Rogelberg, she realized that meetings would be a fine venue for studying all of that and more. “You can use a meeting as a stage to study anything you want,” she says. After all, at their core “meetings are a fundamental aspect of how we organize and interact as social beings,” Kreamer and coauthors Rogelberg and Jon Gray, both of the University of North Carolina in Charlotte, wrote in the 2026 article. To tap meetings for study, researchers typically use two approaches. The first is a classic survey in which researchers retroactively ask people about their real or simulated meeting experiences and analyze the responses. The second method is to analyze specific behaviors and trends extracted from audio and video recordings of meetings. Such precise content is “where the music is,” says industrial and organizational psychologist Nale Lehmann-Willenbrock, director of the Center for Better Work at the University of Hamburg, Germany. In one experiment, Lehmann-Willenbrock and colleagues extracted data from video-recorded meetings to study how leader communication affects team interaction during decision-making. The researchers found that respectful, attentive inquiry by meeting leaders enhanced team performance, in contrast to when leaders asked questions but didn’t attentively listen to responses. As with many meeting science studies, their findings about what improves meetings’ effectiveness often seem obvious, but meeting researchers note that meetings often stray far from these ideals. Such research also consistently reveals the importance of active listening, says Lehmann-Willenbrock: not just passive acknowledgement by the listener but actively providing thoughtful feedback. In virtual meetings, because “we’ve all learned to mute ourselves if we’re not talking, very often, active listening is killed completely.” The balance of listening and speaking also can influence how well a meeting goes. Meeting leaders and participants may retain very different views of whether a meeting was successful, notes Lehmann-Willenbrock. “Often, leaders talk the most, which is also why they often think their meetings are pretty good,” she says. Kreamer also emphasizes that it’s important that meeting leaders lead but not dominate the discussion. Leaders should monitor the proportion of time they are speaking to allow for inclusion of many voices. Research supports a checklist of additional things that leaders can do before, during, and after a meeting to make it a success, says Lehmann-Willenbrock. To set the stage, leaders should choose appropriate rooms, refreshments, and lighting, invite only those who need to be there, plan to start and end on time, prepare an agenda, set and communicate clear goals, and allow time for pre-meeting conversation. Socializing first, especially in virtual meetings, is beneficial, Lehmann-Willenbrock says. Her research has shown that “for more introverted participants and more quiet participants, this warmup phase is really helpful because it helps them make more active contributions inside the meeting.” Indeed, her studies on face-to-face meetings have shown that pre-meeting small talk predicts meeting effectiveness, particularly for more introverted participants. But pre-meeting talk is rare in virtual meetings; she says leaders should consider establishing a norm of arriving early or using the first few minutes for an informal check-in. Facilitators can increase the likelihood of a successful meeting by making sure all opinions are heard. Kreamer recommends a technique called silent brainstorming to get all participants, even shy and reluctant ones, to engage in idea generation. It involves creating a shared virtual space, such as a chat or online document, to which all participants contribute simultaneously. Kreamer points to a 2009 study that found this approach (some researchers call it “brainwriting”) was more effective than traditional free-for-all spoken brainstorming in meetings. Kreamer uses this technique when she and her students are dreaming up research ideas in class. “We can all see each other working,” she says, and the quieter students don’t worry about interrupting or having to verbally express their suggestions. After 10 minutes, she reviews the ideas with the group. Zofia Walters, a doctoral student in industrial organizational psychology working with Kreamer, has been studying another aspect of meeting inclusiveness: the needs of neurodiverse participants. Her studies provide evidence that while practices like minimizing distractions, documenting instructions, using shared calendars, and building regular check-ins into meetings likely benefit all employees, they may be especially helpful for people with ADHD. In a survey of 147 employees from a variety of workplaces, Walters asked participants about their most recent virtual meeting: how effective it was, how fatigued they felt afterward and their emotional state. Participants also reported how much time they spent multitasking during the meeting, such as by answering emails, and how long their camera was on. They also shared whether they had an ADHD diagnosis, either formal or informal (based on self-assessment). In findings yet to be published, Walters reported that individuals with ADHD were more likely to report feeling more meeting fatigue, especially when the camera was on for extended periods, than non-ADHD peers. They also reported poorer emotional states. Her findings also suggest that multitasking during meetings may help ADHD attendees manage boredom or hyperactivity. Effective meeting practices don’t end when the meeting ends. After a meeting, leaders can ensure it was time well spent by communicating action items to participants, taking concrete steps to implement the ideas and tasks generated in the meeting, and assessing employee engagement, meeting satisfaction, and meeting effectiveness, Lehmann-Willenbrock and colleagues concluded in a 2018 paper in Organizational Dynamics. Does all this time spent in meetings take a toll on workers? The evidence seems to suggest so. Research has found that employees anticipate feeling worse and being less productive when their day is jam-packed with meetings, especially when they are working on complex tasks. Meetings are sometimes seen as the default way to get things done, but often, people don’t specify what they want to achieve from them, says cognitive scientist Ava Scott of the University of Copenhagen. This, she says, “makes a lot of meetings feel very purposeless.” Recurring meetings tend to be the most aimless, Scott’s research on Microsoft employees has found; one participant described the content of these successive meets as dreamlike in their similarity to one another. If there’s no clear goal, you don’t know why you’re meeting, and everyone’s very busy, it probably indicates that taking up people’s time with a meeting isn’t ideal, says Scott. Kreamer notes that some companies, such as Shopify, have cut all recurring meetings. Nevertheless, a 2026 study of more than 9,000 workers in Norway found that while meetings are both common and costly, their benefits may make them worth enduring. The research, led by economist David Deming at Harvard University and based on data collected by Norwegian survey company Norstat, found that meeting intensity is positively associated with subsequent wage growth and on-the-job learning. One possible explanation for the finding is that these meetings may offer the chance to interact with more senior colleagues and so enable the transmission of key knowledge. The important caveat is that the study, published by the National Bureau of Economic Research, was correlational, so it can’t establish a causal relationship between the number of meetings attended and wage increases; it does further suggest that although the cost of meetings is particularly high for high-wage companies, the benefits may be even higher. That leads the authors to conclude: “Meetings are the broccoli of work—widely disliked, but probably good for us anyway.” Humor can help make a meeting successful—as long as it’s authentic, supportive, or positive rather than demeaning. Used appropriately, humor can spur new ideas and enhance team performance. This is most likely to work when meeting participants are well-acquainted, Lehmann-Willenbrock reported after analyzing videos of meetings at two German companies. People have to know each other well enough to feel safe making a joke, she says. But not everyone is comfortable using humor in meetings, says science educator and comedian Vita Stout, now at Sierra College in Rocklin, California. The former bat researcher attended the International Congress for Bat Research in Austin, Texas, in 2022, and took notes on the jokes being used in meeting talks—transcribing exact quotes, their timing, and audience response. Stout’s research, coauthored with bat scientists and published in the Proceedings of Biological Sciences B, argued that when well-timed and used appropriately, humor in scientific meetings can be a tool for connection and community-building. But joke-telling was more prominent among meeting participants with privilege and power, with older, well-established male scientists making jokes more frequently than early career researchers and scientists from underrepresented groups. As in comedy, timing is also key when planning meetings. Scheduling should consider the human circadian rhythm and avoid meeting during the after-lunch energy dip. Lehmann-Willenbrock admits ruefully that her own academic environment often doesn’t obey that rule. “A lot of our committee meetings are scheduled when people are at their most tired,” she says, or digesting lunch. It’s also important to be mindful of people’s work and family obligations in meeting scheduling. “I have two small kids,” she says, “and I always get really angry when a meeting gets called that’s about something… diversity- or inclusion-related, and then it’s scheduled at 5 pm.” Some research suggests there are cultural differences in basic expectations for meetings. In a survey of 1,432 workers from six countries, for example, Lehmann-Willenbrock and collaborators measured how frustrated, irritated, and upset individuals became in imagined scenarios in which leaders or other participants showed up five, 10, or 15 minutes late to meetings. Respondents from the United States and the Netherlands were especially perturbed by a late leader and a high degree of lateness. People from China and Chile weren’t so frustrated by the same situations. There are very few cross-cultural studies like this in the world of meeting science, says Kreamer. “We have these best practices of meetings that we’ve learned over the past 30 years,” she says, but the bulk of studies have come out of Europe or North America. That’s a deficit she’d like to see addressed to see whether the same rules for optimal meeting success apply. As soon as those results are out, we’ll have to call another meeting. Why BioWare's Neverwinter Nights still has thousands of active players

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-17

Visit Original Website
Neverwinter Nights was made by the studio that produced seminal RPGs Baldur’s Gate, Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic, Mass Effect, and Dragon Age. It sold well and was critically acclaimed at launch. Yet, somehow, it has not been canonized in most gamers’ minds over the decades the same way the games that came before and after it were. Nonetheless, more than two decades later, it still has a tight-knit and surprisingly active community that none of those other games can rival. I think that’s partly because it was a completely different beast. BioWare is known for making multi-character, story-driven role-playing games that focused primarily on single-player story campaigns, though some supported co-op play in those campaigns. With Neverwinter Nights, BioWare instead made a game that was as focused on a modding toolset for content creation as it was on giving players a pre-packaged story, and that emphasized multiplayer at least as much as solo play. Today you would expect this to be a calculated attempt to bring more long-term, always-on, multiplayer-as-a-service to an RPG franchise that has mostly appealed to solo players. You might imagine it would be about selling cosmetics, a subscription, or a battle pass, or maybe you would think it was about nabbing appealing engagement numbers. But it’s clear from playing it today that Neverwinter Nights wasn’t primarily made for those reasons. Instead, it came from a passion for tabletop role-playing and a desire to get closer to that in video game form than ever before. Yes, the BioWare team noted in some interviews that they saw this as a trial run for exploring the idea of making a massively multiplayer game (MMO)—something that, in the year 2000, was seen by many as the hot new business opportunity in gaming, and which was in many ways the precursor to the games-as-a-service we see today. They then indicated they realized that an MMO was harder to make than they had assumed and abandoned that ambition well before Neverwinter Nights shipped. Neverwinter Nights is not exactly an MMO. Instead, it’s designed for small communities of people to inhabit and create with, and it features a powerful toolset for virtual dungeon masters to use to subject regular players to challenges in an improvisational way on a live server. “We always envisioned it as a table with four legs—the single player, the multiplayer, the DMing tools and the toolset (the module making tools),” BioWare’s Ray Muzyka told IGN around the time the game was released. “They all appeal to different groups, and we’ve tried to make them all equivalently high quality.” Over the past month, I’ve been replaying Neverwinter Nights‘ main story campaign, The Wailing Death, as a solo player, for the first time in 20 years. The nostalgia has been strong in this replay, but I have also found that BioWare didn’t quite nail “equivalently high quality” for all the pillars. Still, it doesn’t matter, because as we’ll see when we check in with the game’s community, the campaign is not the point. “The single player experience has to be as good as anything we’ve ever done in [Baldur’s Gate], [Baldur’s Gate 2], or Throne of Bhaal and hopefully better,” Muzyka told IGN. “Our goal here at BioWare is that each game we do has to be better than the previous one.” If single-player is one of the legs, it might be fair to call Neverwinter Nights‘ table a little wobbly. It still stands, but you know how irritating it can be. For example, Neverwinter Nights leaves behind Baldur’s Gate or Icewind Dale‘s party-based systems, and instead has the player controlling just one character directly. It makes sense for a multiplayer game, but doesn’t work as well for a single-player experience in this case—at least with the Dungeons & Dragons rules of the time. I chose to play a monk, a martial arts specialist, simply because I’ve always been drawn to that particular fantasy identity, and because I’d never played one in a D&D game. I regretted that decision after a while. For many of the character levels covered in The Wailing Death, the monk basically just picks between two fighting modes: one that misses more but attacks more frequently, and one that’s just normal. That, along with some intense restrictions on which weapons or armor can be used while keeping all their benefits, and a couple of knockdown and stun attacks that often don’t work, make up the whole kit for many hours of playtime, particularly in the first act. (It does get better later on, though.) A kit like this makes sense in a pen-and-paper RPG with a live dungeon master (DM), because the player and DM can work together to invent creative expressions of their abilities, and it works in a party in a video game because it’s just one of multiple complementary characters with a wide range of skills and spells. But for a game that is about 80 percent battling enemies without controlling any other characters, it gets monotonous quickly. There aren’t as many meaningful character builds, gearing, or tactical choices as you’re used to in some other RPGs until quite a ways into the campaign. Often, you just run in and watch the dice roll until either you die or the enemy dies, while sometimes drinking potions. This problem is less severe with some other classes, but the tension is usually there to some degree. Whereas Baldur’s Gate 2, for example, had a partial authored background for your character that then played into the narrative, Neverwinter Nights leaves you as an Elder Scrolls-like blank slate. I normally love that! It’s good for role-playing with friends, because you can make up your own character completely. But it means the story can’t be character-driven, and the gameplay doesn’t provide enough flexibility or freedom in return. Still, I enjoyed my experience with the campaign when I replayed it recently, even if it couldn’t match Baldur’s Gate 2, Knights of the Old Republic, Mass Effect, or other BioWare classics for me personally. While the story wasn’t anything special by BioWare’s standards, there was something genuinely cozy to me about the genericness of the narrative. It took me back to an earlier time in CRPGs, before things got quite so precious. “This is just like D&D!” was still enough to sell it on its own. Maybe it’s just nostalgia, though. Since most people played the game solo, and it was a relatively small portion of the player base that dove deep into the construction kit or DM tools, I think this is the main reason Neverwinter Nights has not achieved the same pantheon status as other BioWare titles that came before and after it. But for those lucky few who were invested in those other table legs, it actually was quite special—and amazingly, it remains so today. “The main thing I would shout from the rooftops if I could, is that firing up Neverwinter Nights and launching its original campaign… does not do justice to what Neverwinter Nights has become since,” a longtime player named Daniel Morris told me. He goes by Irongron in the community, and he runs Arelith, which is both one of the OG persistent multiplayer servers and one of the most popular servers today. Morris is also working on a visual overhaul of the original campaign; he hopes to release it next year. Arelith hit its peak player count “a little less than two years ago,” as Morris tells it—not way back when the game was new. “Our community is the heart of Arelith, it’s not just our audience, but where we find our DMs and developers,” he said. I spent many evenings in my younger days playing multi-user dungeons (MUDs), the text-based online role-playing games that preceded MMOs like EverQuest or World of Warcraft. While there were big MUDs, many were small communities of just one or two dozen people, and often, those same people were the ones actually making the game’s content. For example, I was a room builder for one MUD; I wrote the descriptions for places in the MUD’s world and decided which monsters, NPCs, or items inhabited those places. Neverwinter Nights‘ multiplayer platform and community—both as it was back in the 2000s and as it is today—reminds me of those MUD days. There’s an ownership of that shared world that is entirely lost in the MMO days, perhaps more akin to a modern, small Minecraft server than an MMO with thousands of users. That seems to be part of the appeal for a player I spoke with who goes by the name NelaK in the community, and who has, among other things, taken over maintenance and moderation of the Neverwinter Vault, the community’s de facto hub for two decades. The website hosts news, patches, assets, mods, a forum, a wiki, and an associated Discord server. “It’s not an MMO, and that’s a good thing in my opinion. The communities are smaller, which means your roleplay and your actions matter and can affect the world,” said NelaK. “The world itself can be tailored around what the players want. That’s a unique thing that you can’t get from an MMO or from smaller-scale multiplayer games. It sits in a sweet spot where you can have enough players that it feels like a proper community and a world you can engage with while you can still matter.” Baldur’s Gate and Icewind Dale did have multiplayer, where each player could control one or more characters directly. As noted, Neverwinter Nights limits each player to one character, but it adds a new role: a dungeon master, who can spawn enemies, occupy and act as NPCs, and more, to aid in group storytelling, similar to a pen-and-paper game. “While Arelith is essentially a mini MMO, many of the other servers pride themselves in running highly curated, beautifully crafted campaign worlds much closer to tabletop role-playing game emulator that Neverwinter Nights was first intended as,” Morris told me. He called Neverwinter Nights “a direct translation of the tabletop role-playing game experience into the digital sphere.” The game’s modding kit and easy-to-use scripting tools let players build scenarios relatively quickly. It essentially let players build a little RPG world, run it, and inhabit it. It was a bustling scene back then, and there’s still a community doing this today. The combination of the relative simplicity of the graphics and the elegance of the tools make this toolset an unusually excellent balance between flexible, powerful, and easy to learn. “The tile-based areas are basic and limiting in some respects, but they do mean that building areas is quick and easy,” explained NelaK. “It’s much more approachable than a lot of other platforms.” For NelaK and some other members of the game’s community today, the game’s tools aren’t just the remaining appeal—they’re the main appeal. “I’ve been playing Neverwinter Nights since launch day. I remember one of my friends dragging me along to the store because they really wanted the game and I went just to keep them company,” he remembered. “I ended up buying it, and I’ve been hooked ever since, but to this day, I’ve still never played through the original campaigns… and yet I’ve played Neverwinter Nights or spent time building or working on something Neverwinter Nights-related most days since.” I toyed around with the tools quite a bit back in the day. I remember re-creating a big chunk of the world of my favorite MMO at the time, Meridian 59, with the intent to make a story-driven, single-player campaign for that game; Meridian 59 had very little narrative as it was. I also remember that Neverwinter Nights was for many years a common way for would-be narrative designers to make a portfolio piece for applying for jobs within the games industry. When I was in college, a close friend of mine (Brian “Psychochild” Green, who at the time co-ran Meridian 59, and sadly has since passed) offered to show my Neverwinter Nights modules to some friends of his at BioWare if I wanted to interview for a job there to write for their next game, which, based on timing, probably would have been Mass Effect. I declined in order to focus on other priorities I had at the time, for better or worse. Similarly, many (though certainly not all) people in the community have professional backgrounds closely related to, or at least adjacent to, game development. “Neverwinter Nights provides a unique intersection of where I can apply my technical and professional skills along with my interest in video games and do it for something that doesn’t feel like work,” said NelaK. Morris specifically noted that several members of the Arileth team work or have worked in the games industry or software development. This is the game’s legacy, not the story campaigns that shipped with the game. And that explains the game’s relative obscurity today: that’s a more niche appeal than a really good, story-driven, single-player RPG. But for the people who are into it, there’s nothing else that quite compares. I don’t imagine most people still regularly see references to Neverwinter Nights, but I sometimes do. For the past couple of years, I’ve enjoyed the dark fantasy trend on TikTok. These are short, vertical videos set to a particular kind of 1980s fantasy-type medieval synth music, depicting imagery from what feels like a long-lost, strangely nostalgic subgenre of fantasy media that is more implied in our memories’ liminal space between real fantasy movies and books of the ’80s and early ’90s than it is something that actually specifically existed. It turns out Neverwinter Nights‘ assets fit that milieu quite well, so I sometimes saw videos of people creating online Neverwinter Nights servers meant to capture that vibe. It’s niche as hell, but it’s there. The TikTok videos from one of the game’s servers were actually how I got inspired to try the game again. “Over the last year there’s been a bit of a resurgence, with new servers going live, and others going viral,” Morris told me. Some player-run servers “have had hundreds of thousands interacting with their TikTok videos.” He added that his own community’s Discord server has 5,000 members, and that’s just one part of the game’s wider audience. If you want to get back into the game or try it for the first time, you can buy the Enhanced Edition on GOG and other storefronts; a front-page poll on the Neverwinter Vault indicates that about 65 percent of the community primarily uses that version, as compared to previous releases or the sequel, Neverwinter Nights 2. If you want to try the single-player, the Enhanced Edition comes with a plethora of officially sanctioned campaigns of varying quality—not just The Wailing Death. But let’s say you, like some members of the game’s current community, are more interested in multiplayer than single-player. A good place to start is the Neverwinter Vault. Additionally, Enhanced Edition developer Beamdog hosts a list of active community servers you can join. As I write this, a few hundred people are playing across a couple dozen servers, which is impressive. I love to see old games like this keep the community going. It’s especially important for Neverwinter Nights, because more than any other BioWare game or even most other RPGs in general, this game is its community. OpenAI models hacked a crypto wallet and breached more websites

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-16

OpenAI said internal models hacked two more websites, bypassed company-imposed browsing restrictions, and used exposed credentials to draw cryptocurrency from a wallet it couldn’t otherwise access, in the latest installment of disclosures of its agents performing unethical and likely illegal acts. The company on Thursday issued reports of four new incidents, all of which occurred in June. They come after the company revealed in July that internal test agents exploited a zero-day vulnerability in a tool designed to restrict their access to the Internet. They then broke into the network of AI tool provider Hugging Face. The agents took that illegal action to steal answers to a hacking test that weren’t available when completing the tasks on their own. The incident—which likely would have resulted in criminal charges had they been performed directly by a person—has further heightened concerns that widespread AI use could have harmful effects on an unprecedented scale. In two of the cases reported Thursday, OpenAI agents tasked with retrieving information from websites hacked them when the models ran into obstacles obtaining it directly. In one incident during reinforcement training, a model that received access denials when trying to retrieve historical hospital-bed statistics on a government statistics website “used the site’s file-download feature to retrieve server configuration and application files, including sensitive credentials,” OpenAI said. “It inspected the application code, bypassed the statistics endpoint’s authorization checks, and retrieved data that had previously been denied. The model saved the data locally.” In the other incident, a model instructed to retrieve figures from a public research dashboard bypassed the organization’s security controls and used the unauthorized access to steal credentials. It then made configuration changes on the server. In one evaluation, the changes let the model make an internal search tool relay queries to the public dashboard. In a second evaluation, the model downloaded five non-public tables containing 147,451 rows. OpenAI researchers had prompted the agents to retrieve only five dashboard figures. Even after resetting the setting, the model continued using the token to retrieve non-public data. “The evaluation ended without the model disclosing that it had guessed answers, accessed the organization’s internal systems, downloaded non-public tables, or changed the authentication configuration,” OpenAI said. “All five answers in this evaluation were marked incorrect.” In a third report, the company said a model prompted to update a cryptocurrency trading application used funds from a locked wallet on a test network. The model “derived wallet keys from publicly known developer recovery phrases, found an account with test-network funds, and used that account to pay for the requested contract deployment.” The report continued: “The model questioned whether it had permission, then reasoned that a public recovery phrase and valueless test-network tokens made the funds acceptable to use. The deployment succeeded and charged transaction fees to the account. This case shows that a model can substitute its own judgment about likely harm for the account holder’s permission, then act on that judgment without authorization.” In the last report, OpenAI said that a model bypassed restrictions researchers had placed on the types of web requests it could make. During reinforcement-learning training, the model had been prompted to seek public trade statistics. OpenAI went on: After finding a public yearbook and submitting an answer, it continued probing for another route to the statistics service. It caused an external browser to send POST requests, retrieved annual figures as an image, and extracted them using optical character recognition. It then developed a reusable helper that returned response text through a forwarding service and callback mailbox, repeatedly checked the retrieved figures, and used saved data in submitted responses. This case shows how a model can bypass restrictions on its own tools by getting external services to perform the blocked actions, even after finding another way to answer the question. In August, Nvidia pulled together six of the biggest names in finance—Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR—to set up financing platforms that could mobilize over $500 billion for companies building AI infrastructure. It was the latest move by a company that has gone from making chips to orchestrating the wider playing field in AI—and a sign that growth for the world’s most valuable company depends as much on the next big engineering challenge as on whether customers can keep affording its products. It also points to the wider direction of travel in AI. Even as the world obsesses about model releases, chip capabilities, and data center capacity, the variable that increasingly determines who gets to compete is simply money. The next phase of the AI race between companies and countries will depend in large part on who can pay for it; the action is shifting from frontier labs to credit desks. Whoever can raise capital cheaply and at scale will set the pace, and that race still favors the United States, whose capital markets are deeper than anyone else’s. For China and Europe, the road to closing the technological gap runs through the financial one. Technological revolutions tend to be remembered for their technologies: steam engines, railways, the internet. Just as important is the financial machinery that made them possible: the joint-stock company dispersed the risk of trading voyages; early bond and equity markets financed railroads before they could produce a return; venture capital supplied high-risk money to companies too new to raise it from traditional investors. AI is reaching its own version of this moment. For half a century, technology was asset-light: software could be written once and distributed to billions at negligible marginal cost. AI is turning technology into a heavy industry. The latest systems require factories, advanced chips, substations, and enormous supplies of electricity. Even the world’s richest companies are finding it hard to fund all of this from their income. The five largest American hyperscalers alone are on track to spend $730 billion on capital expenditure in 2026. A shrinking proportion is coming from their own balance sheets; they are expected to finance up to a third of it with debt. If the most profitable companies in history have to borrow to stay in the race, what hope is there for everyone else? Nvidia’s deal is one answer. Project finance has long funded infrastructure with high up-front costs, paid down by steady returns over many years: power plants, toll roads, airports. What’s new is the attempt to turn compute into an asset class that pension funds and insurers are comfortable owning, the way they already own infrastructure. The arrangement carries risks. The parallel with infrastructure is imperfect: Energy facilities are built to last decades, usually in response to foreseeable demand, and generate predictable returns. In contrast, graphics processing units (GPUs) are short-lived assets; AI demand is unpredictable; and it is too early to project returns. Still, if the upsides from AI are speculative, so are the downsides—and if the model works, it could reshape the AI market much as venture capital changed the risk calculus for starting a business. Nvidia’s announcement came with familiar worries about circular financing, in which the chipmaker’s money comes back to it as chip orders. But this arrangement is the opposite. Nvidia will not, for the most part, be lending its own money—nor will its six partners. The capital comes from third parties: pension funds, insurers, sovereign wealth funds, household savings. Rather than a closed loop, the buildout is being wired into the wider financial system: the deal does not artificially boost demand but restructures the cost of supply for a wider bench of competitors beyond the hyperscalers, who can themselves only partially afford it. Financial structure will in part pick the winners of the technology race—not by who has more money, but by whether the wider financial architecture puts capital to optimal use. Europe illustrates the problem. The continent has one of the world’s largest pools of savings; eurozone households alone hold about 10 trillion euros (about $11 trillion) in bank deposits. Yet European institutional investors would rather buy U.S. tech bonds than back their own startups. The money is there; what’s missing is the plumbing to turn savings into investment. If AI comes down to access to capital, Europe’s AI strategy needs a capital-markets strategy—and progress on long-stalled capital-markets integration would do more for European competitiveness than another round of gigafactories. China’s problem runs the other way. State money is good at pouring concrete; it is less good at matching capacity to demand: after a policy-driven building spree, Chinese media reported last year that as much as 80% of newly built computing capacity sat unused. The Stanford HAI AI Index 2026 estimates that last year, private investment in AI in the U.S. was more than 23 times that in China. With finance picking the winners, the United States starts with an edge: No other country is as good at turning the savings of pensioners and households into risky, long-lived assets at scale. America’s bond markets, private credit funds, and asset managers are already looking for exactly this kind of asset—how else could a chipmaker line up a half-trillion-dollar financing target in a single announcement? The first phase of the AI boom relied on technical breakthroughs: models, chips, applications. The next phase depends on the financial system’s ability to underwrite the industry’s expansion beyond a small set of companies. Nvidia has made a bold attempt to reconfigure the economics of AI; competitors will need equally imaginative financial machinery of their own. Technological revolutions do not happen simply because someone invents the future. They happen when they also figure out how to pay for it. In 2024, two Michigan high school students were sitting in the bleachers during a student-teacher basketball game when their physical education teacher collapsed from cardiac arrest. They had both recently learned cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) at a career-focused technical nursing center and rushed to help, beginning chest compressions and using an automated external defibrillator (AED) before emergency responders arrived. Thanks to their actions, their teacher survived, and is one of approximately 350,000 Americans who experience an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest each year. Roughly nine out of ten will not survive. We often describe the difference between life and death as a matter of speed: how quickly emergency services arrive and how fast a hospital can intervene. But in reality, cardiac arrest outcomes often depend on whether someone nearby begins CPR in the first few minutes, which rarely belong to emergency responders, but instead to whomever is standing closest: a classmate, teacher, stranger at a basketball game, or someone waiting for a train. Every minute without CPR sharply reduces the chances the patient will live long enough to reach a hospital. Schools occupy a special role in public health. As a location most Americans pass through and one of the only places they may ever encounter CPR training, a high school classroom may be the best opportunity to prepare the next generation of lifesavers. Schools already rehearse fire drills and lockdown drills for dangers that are statistically rare. Cardiac arrest is the reverse: far more common, and far less rehearsed. CPR skills can save lives years down the road when a cardiac arrest occurs at home, in a gym, at an airport, or at a university. Harnessing that opportunity requires both the resources to teach CPR effectively and the policies to ensure every student has the chance to learn it. In Europe, Belgium, Denmark, France, Italy, Norway, Portugal, and England implemented school CPR education through national requirements or curriculum standards. Denmark offers a clear example of the positive impact. From 2001 to 2010, as Denmark introduced nationwide resuscitation initiatives including mandatory school CPR education, bystander CPR rates more than doubled, while 30-day survival after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest more than tripled. A central challenge is how to implement CPR training at scale. The international KIDS SAVE LIVES initiative offers a practical blueprint. Endorsed by the World Health Organization, it calls for recurring, age-appropriate basic life support education beginning in childhood. Research from the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation shows children can learn the early links in the chain of survival from the young age of 4, perform effective chest compressions by age 12, and pass skills to family members and others. Some may hesitate to perform CPR because of legal concerns over causing injury—like rib fractures—while administering CPR, but every state has Good Samaritan laws providing protections to people who render emergency aid in good faith. A 30-year review of U.S. litigation across all 50 states from 1989 to 2019 found no cases in which a layperson who provided CPR was held liable for causing harm. The U.S. has also made significant progress in bringing CPR education into schools. In 1984, Alabama became the first state to require CPR instruction for high school graduation. Over the four decades since, communities have taken different approaches to bring CPR into the classroom. In West Virginia and Alaska, educators’ unions formally backed legislation requiring CPR education, while efforts in Washington and Illinois advanced in memory of students lost to cardiac arrest. Today, 43 states require some form of CPR education before high school graduation. California, Colorado, Hawaii, Massachusetts, Nebraska, New Hampshire, and Wyoming are the seven that remain. Several are already taking steps to expand CPR education. Massachusetts invested $425,000 to expand CPR education in public schools, while Colorado includes hands-on CPR and AED instruction in its statewide health education guidelines but stops short of requiring schools to provide it. California Gov. Gavin Newsom recently signed a resolution directing the Instructional Quality Commission to include high school CPR in the state’s next health curriculum framework. San Francisco is taking an even broader approach, with paramedics and firefighters working to train 100,000 community members in CPR. There is evidence that laws can translate into lifesaving action. A study of nearly 300,000 out-of-hospital cardiac arrests across 20 states found that patients had 12% higher odds of receiving bystander CPR in states with high school CPR education laws than those without them. Other lifesaving education efforts can also offer a roadmap. In Georgia, Wesley’s Law, championed by Laura and Larry Gay after their son’s death, required public schools to stock overdose-reversal medication such as naloxone. In 2025, the state launched an opioid-settlement-funded initiative to provide overdose-reversal kits to public schools statewide, along with training resources for staff. In Indiana, emergency-response professionals helped expand Stop the Bleed training in schools, and the state’s Secured School Safety Grant program now offers funding for school bleeding-control programs and kits. Both examples demonstrate how local champions and an existing source of funding can help turn a lifesaving idea into reality. Broadening collaboration among organizations such as the American Medical Association, American Red Cross, Project ADAM, and American Heart Association can also help expand CPR education nationwide. After Buffalo Bills safety Damar Hamlin suffered cardiac arrest on the field in 2023, he became a leading advocate for the bipartisan HEARTS Act, which Congress passed unanimously in 2024. President Biden signed it into law later that year, supporting schools in expanding access to AEDs, CPR and AED education, and cardiac emergency response planning. Cardiac arrest does not wait for emergency responders. The students who stepped forward when their teacher collapsed playing basketball in Michigan illustrate what becomes possible when CPR skills are taught before they are needed. Someday, every high school student in the U.S. could graduate prepared to step up and become a lifesaver. Previously undisclosed documents obtained by TIME, including letters from senior Israeli security officials to Qatari leaders, reveal that Israel encouraged and played a direct role in a payment program that was more extensive than Netanyahu has publicly acknowledged. Beginning in 2012, Netanyahu’s government helped to facilitate Qatari payments to Gaza that had begun after Hamas seized control in 2007. By 2018, Qatar had sent roughly $1.1 billion into the Strip, according to an analysis provided to Israeli ministers, the Israeli daily Haaretz reported, with the funding designated for humanitarian assistance, fuel, and government salaries. That year, after Israeli officials castigated Qatar for allegedly financing Hamas, the Qataris began demanding formal documentation of an arrangement the two governments had agreed was meant to provide relief to citizens of the beleaguered enclave. The extent of Israel’s cooperation with the payments was reflected in a 2020 letter from Yossi Cohen, then director of the Mossad, Israel’s intelligence agency, to Qatar’s Emir Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani. “It is indeed a great opportunity to express our gratitude and appreciation for the humanitarian aid efforts that the State of Qatar has provided the last few years to the Gaza Strip,” Cohen wrote. “Undoubtedly, these humanitarian efforts have played a crucial role in improving the humanitarian situation within the Gaza Strip, and ensuring stability and safety in the region.” The payment arrangement had the support of successive Israeli governments, the Palestinians, the Trump and Biden Administrations, the United Nations, and European powers, according to officials who served in both the Trump and Biden Administrations. The flow of money from Qatar into Gaza has taken on new significance since October 7, 2023, when Hamas terrorists stormed southern Israel, murdered some 1,200 people, and took 251 more hostage. The worst security failure in the country’s history raised troubling questions about whether the financial lifeline helped to sustain Hamas’s grip on power and, in turn, enabled it to carry out the assault. Since then, Qatar has been frustrated by allegations leveled by Israeli political leaders that its payments to Gaza had helped finance the deadliest massacre of Jews since the Holocaust. “All of Qatar’s financial assistance in Gaza has been directed to Palestinian citizens through mechanisms coordinated with the Israeli authorities and international partners. Netanyahu’s wager, according to these critics, was that the aid would give Hamas something to lose and would therefore help contain the group while Israel pursued normalization with the Arab world without resolving the Palestinian question. At first, the arrangement was informal. Qatar supplied the money with Israel’s approval, even as Israeli officials publicly criticized Doha for financing Hamas, according to sources familiar with the negotiations. The contradiction aggravated the Qataris, a senior official in the first Trump Administration tells TIME: Israeli officials would denounce the payments in public, then privately ask Doha to renew them. Qatar, a gas-rich monarchy in the Persian Gulf, is accustomed to walking a fine line. It plays host to both the U.S. Central Command and to senior Hamas officials. The country set up a Hamas political office in Doha at the request of the U.S. in 2011. In contrast to other Gulf monarchies, especially Saudi Arabia, Qatar sees no threat in the Muslim Brotherhood, the political movement that spawned Hamas. After Oct. 7, Qatar's government emerged as the go-between in negotiations among Israel, Hamas and Washington, where Biden Administration officials privately praised the Qataris as honest brokers. By 2018, Qatar sought written documentation of the Israeli government’s role in the aid agreement. On October 2, 2018, Israel and Qatar, together with the United Nations, signed an agreement to finance fuel deliveries into Gaza. The Qatari officials agreed but warned Barnea that it would be the last renewal. With tensions rising between Israel’s far-right ministers and Hamas, the arrangement had run its course, Qatar believed. Barnea asked for two more months. The rationale, according to the officials familiar with the conversation, was that continuing assistance to Gaza’s civilians gave Hamas something to lose and therefore gave Israel a measure of leverage over the group. After two months, the parties could reassess Hamas’s behavior and devise a more durable mechanism for delivering aid. They never got the chance. Without the money to maintain sewage systems, water supplies, and schools, he argued, the territory could have descended into chaos and disease. Trump Administration officials, led by envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, are working with Qatar and other Arab governments on a framework that would restore basic services while eliminating Gaza’s dependence on Islamist armed factions. “We want to ensure that the civilians are reliant on themselves and not on anyone else,” an international diplomat tells TIME. “So it’s providing actual opportunities for civilians to make money and be sustainable.” The question confronting Israel and its international partners is not simply whether money should flow into Gaza, but also who controls it—and whether the next arrangement will avoid the failures of the last.

It’s a tragic story of dependence and the limits of love. And it’s where Jo van Gogh-Bonger comes in. Biographical details about her—revealed in her diaries and letters—have only recently been made public by the Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam. They paint a picture of a determined woman to whom the world of art owes a tremendous debt. Jo was married to Vincent’s younger brother, Theo, who was an art dealer in Paris and Vincent’s only source of income. Theo supported Vincent with a monthly allowance, and in exchange Vincent gave Theo his paintings to try to market and sell. The financial arrangement, as it would in most families, put an emotional strain on both brothers. Theo was not rich, and Vincent constantly complained about being broke, with not even enough money for food, and often practically begged his brother for more money. Over the course of 10 years, Vincent created more than 800 paintings and 700 drawings, desperately hoping for financial independence and commercial success. But in Vincent’s lifetime, Theo is known to have sold just one painting: Red Vineyard at Arles. In July of 1890, believing himself a failure as an artist and a man, Vincent shot himself in the chest. He was only 37. Six months after Vincent’s suicide, Theo died from the ravages of syphilis, leaving Jo a young widow with a baby to support, not much money, and an unusual inheritance—Vincent’s paintings—more than 400 in total. They filled the walls of their Paris apartment, were stored in closets, under beds, and at a nearby art supply store. The Potato Eaters, Irises, Sunflowers, self-portraits, and The Starry Night. Celebrated as masterpieces today, at the time they were regarded as the unpolished works of a depressed, crazy man and commercially worthless. Jo thought differently. She had no formal art training, but she loved his work and her instincts told her that Vincent was (as Theo had come to believe) a genius. Jo also inherited hundreds of letters that Vincent wrote and received, most exchanged with Theo. Jo read and re-read those letters, initially to feel closer to Theo, but she was also captivated by Vincent’s compassion and intelligence. She decided the world should read them too. It was part of her plan: to organize exhibitions, lobby critics, and publicize the letters, which showed how vulnerable and anguished Vincent was, yes, but also how deliberate he was in his craft and how close Vincent and Theo were to each other. Eventually, in 1914, Jo succeeded in publishing Vincent’s letters in an ambitious three-volume book, which today is considered one of the most revealing and important works of art history. As much as she would play a vital role in propelling him to fame, Jo was also someone who unwittingly played a part in Vincent’s self-destructive descent, something she at times admitted feeling guilt about after his death. Jo understood Vincent was lonely and that he became more isolated after she married his brother. That infamous act of self-mutilation, when Vincent cut off his ear, happened the very day Vincent likely received the surprising news that Theo and Jo were engaged, triggering what some Van Gogh experts, including Martin Bailey, suspect were unquenchable fears that Theo might eventually abandon him. And Vincent shot himself just days after a stressful visit with Jo and Theo in Paris. Theo was thinking about quitting his job to start his own business, which meant, for a time, no income. The couple (who by now had a baby son they named after Vincent) hoped to rent a better apartment on a lower floor. But budgets were tight, arguments ensued, and Vincent eventually rushed out of the apartment, unable to shake the thought that he was a burden that Theo would or could no longer bear. Jo didn’t seek to put herself at the center of this story, but she is there, nonetheless. A surprisingly relatable woman (at times insecure, at times doggedly persistent), she courageously stood up to the male-dominated art world as a champion for Vincent and spent 30 years of her life promoting his work and life story in Holland, Germany, France, England, and even, for a time, in America. Interestingly, of all the paintings Jo inherited, no one—not Jo or Theo or Vincent—considered The Starry Night one of Vincent’s best. Vincent didn’t even sign it. Research recently published by the Van Gogh Museum points out that Vincent signed only about 16% of his paintings, hypothesizing he did so usually when he was completely satisfied or wanted to make them a gift to friends or family. In the brothers’ correspondence, The Starry Night was referred to rather than named. Theo called it simply “the village in the moonlight,” critically writing to Vincent that “I feel that the search for style takes away the real sentiment of things.” Vincent conceded the painting lacked character and considered it a mistake that “…once again I’m allowing myself to do stars too big…new setback, and I’ve had enough of that.” Jo also didn’t seem to consider The Starry Night as especially important. She sold it early on but reacquired it briefly in time for a major exhibition she organized in Amsterdam in 1905. It was painting number 199, called De Sterren (The Stars), and it changed hands two more times before MoMA acquired it in 1941. By then, it had been rechristened The Starry Night. The name stuck. On Tuesday, the Supreme Court continued its return to action with Beaird v. United States, a case about criminal sentencing. The court will decide exactly how closely judges are required to listen to guidelines of the United States Sentencing Commission over how long a convicted defendant can be jailed for. While the justices, especially Neil Gorsuch and Ketanji Brown Jackson, sparred over how to handle the court’s recent precedent weakening federal agency power, a concerning pattern re-emerged for the first time in this new term. What we have is a court with a nearly unrelenting drive to monopolize its own view of the law over everyone else’s. In the U.S. criminal system, after a jury finds a person guilty beyond a reasonable doubt, it is generally up to the district court judge to determine their sentence. One problem with that system is that different judges can have vastly different views on the true severity and punishment deserved for the same crime. To help remedy unfair disparity between sentences, Congress created the U.S. Sentencing Commission in the 1980s, an independent federal agency within the judicial branch. Headed by presidentially appointed judges and criminal law experts, the commission provides formal recommendations for the appropriate sentencing for various crimes. More specifically, it provides two types of writings: guidelines providing sentencing ranges (say: 24 to 48 months) and commentary providing more detail on how a judge is supposed to issue decisions within that range. The commentary in particular also instructs judges when they can enhance or decrease a sentence based on the facts of the case. For example, the judge who sentenced Elizabeth Holmes in her healthcare fraud suit relied on the commission guidelines for the initial number of financial penalties but then relied on the commentary to further increase that sentence based on the severity of the financial loss the court calculated. Beaird concerns how judges are supposed to treat these two distinct yet related parts of the Sentencing Commission’s work. Authorities in Texas arrested a man named Kendrick Beaird for unlawful possession of a firearm. The lower court judge sentenced Beaird to an even heavier sentence than he might otherwise have gotten due to the type of large-capacity firearm the police found him possessing. The guidelines told the court to impose a harsher sentence on Beaird for a “large” capacity magazine, but it was the commentary that defined that “large” roughly meant a gun capable of firing 15 rounds or more before reloading. Beaird argued that his firearm was actually below the range that the commentary suggested and, more broadly, that a court has the authority to ignore the commentary if the judge disagrees with it. How much credence a court gives to both the guideline range versus the commentary details matters a lot in a case like Beiard’s, as it can mean the difference of years worth of prison time. At oral argument, no one was more prepared to discuss the Sentencing Commission’s procedure than Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson, who served as vice chair of the commission before becoming a judge. She posed to Beaird’s counsel that the guidelines and the commentary are inextricably linked. They are written by the same officials, the commentary cross-references specific guidelines, and both the commentary and guidelines are reviewed after years of data collection on sentencing patterns across the judiciary. Beaird’s position that judges should be allowed to ignore the commentary whenever they see fit, in Jackson’s view, splits an important mechanism for evenhanded criminal justice in half. Doing so would weaken the usefulness of both the commentary and the guidelines, and ultimately lead to wildly different sentences for the same crimes all depending on the judge. And you can imagine which types of Americans would be most hurt by inconsistent sentencing. As Justice Elena Kagan noted, one way to keep this area of law consistent is for the court to follow its own precedent, in a case called Kisor v. Wilkie. That 2019 opinion, which Kagan herself wrote, was about how much courts should defer to an expert federal agency’s interpretation of its own regulations. Despite a request from litigants to overturn prior rulings in Kisor, a slim majority of the court ruled that judges were still required to defer to an agency’s interpretations of its regulations. So when, for instance, a federal agency like the Department of Veterans Affairs defines a medical term in determining which veterans get benefits, a court has limited discretion to second-guess that expert analysis. One option to resolve this case is for the court to transfer the Kisor deference principle to the Sentencing Commission and rule that this agency, too, is uniquely suited to have a say within its area of expertise. Gorsuch disagrees. Every question he asked made clear that he would shoo away the more deferential Kisor approach, a case that saw Chief Justice John Roberts join the Ruth Bader Ginsburg and Stephen Breyer–era wing of the Democratic appointees to preserve the doctrine. Instead, Gorsuch favors deciding Beaird’s case along the lines of the decision of Loper Bright, an opinion from two terms ago where the 6–3 Republican appointees succeeded where Kisor failed, ruling that judges no longer needed to defer to an agency’s interpretation of the congressional statues (as opposed to regulations). Loper Bright controversially overturned this agency deference “Chevron doctrine” in a move that would have been way too far for even very conservative justices from decades past. A Gorsuch-backed opinion in Beaird would almost certainly rule that courts are not required to defer to the Sentencing Commission whatsoever. This would leave judges free to impose their own view on what it means for a capacity firearm to be large or not, among thousands of other nuanced sentencing questions that come up every year. Among people who study or practice within this admittedly niche area of administrative law, the opinions in Kisor about judicial review of agency regulations and Loper Bright about judicial review of agency statutes are huge deals. From the Environmental Protection Agency to the Department of Homeland Security, the question of how much a court might defer to the agency’s expertise affects nearly every decision they make. What specific types of nuclear energy generators are safe enough to use? How much mercury is considered safe to have in your drinking water? Before Loper Bright, it was the agencies who had the primary say in answering these questions, even while their answers were subject to reasonableness review from courts. After Loper Bright, it’s the courts job to reach what they think is the “best” answer to any expertise-laden question. And if the court significantly changes the balance of deference owed to the Sentencing Commission, upward of 10,000 cases could go back to resentencing. Baird is yet another example of what we might think of as the judicial respect line of cases. Unfortunately, we appear to be in the middle of a trend of the court insisting it’s the only entity in all of law whose legal opinions matter. And that trend directly harms the movement for a more democratic legal system, for popular constitutionalism. In 2022, college sports fan Michael Salazar became a digital subscriber to 247Sports.com, a website that shares news about NCAA football and basketball. In order to register for the website, owned by Paramount Global, Salazar signed up for 247Sports’ online newsletter. And he used his subscription to view sports-related video media. Quietly embedded within those videos, though, was a tracking pixel created by Facebook. Because Paramount installed Facebook’s tracking pixel on the 247Sports website, every time Salazar requested or viewed a video, Paramount automatically disclosed the name and URL of the video to Facebook, along with Salazar’s Facebook ID, the unique identifier Facebook assigns to all of its users. Facebook then used that information to show Salazar targeted advertisements. Privacy violations like this have become a routine part of life on the internet. According to a 2024 study on website privacy, more than half of all companies listed in the S&P 500 index use the “Meta pixel” on their sites, sharing people’s information with both Facebook and Instagram. But in theory, there are at least some legal boundaries. Back in 1988, after a D.C. journalist obtained and published a list of movies rented by Supreme Court nominee Robert Bork, a scandalized Congress passed the Video Privacy Protection Act. To be clear: Congress was scandalized by the nonconsensual disclosure of Bork’s rental history, not the judge’s viewing habits, which were pretty unremarkable (unlike those of another judicial nominee who would be confirmed to the Supreme Court a few years later.) The VPPA prohibits videotape service providers from knowingly disclosing the personally identifiable information of consumers, unless they get the consumers’ written consent. The statute defines “videotape service provider” to include any person in the business of delivering “prerecorded video cassette tapes or similar audio visual materials.” And the definition of “consumer” includes any “subscriber of goods or services” from the provider. Here, Salazar subscribed to Paramount’s sports website. He requested audiovisual materials from Paramount, and Paramount delivered them. And then Paramount knowingly disclosed what he watched to Facebook without getting his consent. In September 2022, Salazar filed a class-action lawsuit, arguing that Paramount violated the VPPA. But in July 2023, the federal district court dismissed his complaint, reasoning that Salazar didn’t count as a “consumer” under the statute because he was only subscribed to a newsletter, “not audio visual content.” Salazar appealed to the 6th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, but in April 2025, a divided three-judge panel affirmed the dismissal, agreeing with the district court that Salazar’s newsletter subscription didn’t make him a subscriber under the law. Then Salazar appealed to the Supreme Court, which heard oral arguments in Salazar v. Paramount on Wednesday. Under the plain text of the Video Privacy Protection Act, Salazar should be able to hold Paramount liable for its disclosure of his video history. But during the oral arguments, the Republican-nominated justices peppered Salazar’s attorney with questions suggesting that imposing an atextual limitation onto the statute—one that would conveniently insulate corporations from legal liability when they intrude on consumers’ privacy for profit—was really just the commonsense thing to do. “Wouldn’t it be natural to think that goods or services from a videotape service provider would be video-related services?” asked Justice Clarence Thomas. Later in the oral argument, Thomas said he “always thought of 247Sports as a sports site,” and he questioned the “logic” of viewing the site as a “videotape service provider.” Justice Samuel Alito further argued that the law was “based on the technology of the 1980s,” when people had to “go to a Blockbuster store and get a VHS tape.” Although the text of the VPPA appears to cover Salazar’s claims, Alito suggested that a newsletter subscription isn’t analogous enough to what Congress had in mind and asked Salazar’s counsel why he thought the “smart lower court judges” who ruled against him rejected his “very literal interpretation” of the statute. Justice Brett Kavanaugh echoed these concerns: “We are a textualist Court but usually not a literalist Court,” he said. Kavanaugh continued, “Congress in the 1980s wasn’t thinking about data sharing for targeted advertising.” Kavanaugh and Justice Amy Coney Barrett also worried that, if the VPPA weren’t limited to the long-defunct Blockbusters of the world, practically any business could find themselves liable under the law. Kavanaugh brought up concerns, raised by corporate interest groups in amicus briefs, that “every business in America that has video on its website” could be targeted. Trying to suss out the scope of the statute, Kavanaugh and Barrett both asked how long a video must be for a company to count as a video service provider. Salazar’s attorney indicated that length wasn’t especially relevant and shifted the conversation to the business and profit motive of delivering videos, but Barrett was skeptical. “If I’m on Nordstrom’s website and I click to see the model show me what the shoes are going to look like,” said Barrett, “that’s a profit motive.” For all the Republican justices’ stated concern on Wednesday about reading and interpreting the VPPA in a way that makes sense, they failed to meaningfully engage with the text and context. Paramount provided Salazar with videos and then told Facebook what videos he watched without his consent—the exact kind of injury contemplated by the statute. As The Register reported last week, the GCPM works to harmonize Universal Time – UT1, which boffins calculate using the changing speed of Earth’s rotation – and the Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) standard that humans use to set time. We big-brained hominids can set standards but can’t do much about the fact that the speed of Earth’s rotation is variable. When our planet’s spin slows, the GCPM adds leap seconds so that UT1 and UTC are in synch. In recent years, they have even pondered a negative leap second because Earth’s rotation has sped up a little. Like all good standards orgs, the GCPM conducts years-long debates about its work. The topic of leap seconds has very much been on the agenda in recent years, as they’re increasingly hard to implement now that humanity relies on billions of devices that can usefully share the same timing data but may not easily account for leap seconds. The matter came to a head at last week’s 28th meeting of the GCPM, at which Time Lords voted to end use of leap seconds. The possibility of a negative leap second seems to have been a major factor. “A negative leap second has not previously been applied, and it is considered to pose a high risk of causing anomalies and disruption to critical infrastructures that are largely unprepared,” the Time Lords wrote in a resolution posted after their vote. “The preparation would create, for the industries that rely on time synchronization, a need for financial investment, whose amount is estimated to be similar to their preparations for the millennium bug.” Readers may recall that in the late 1990s, the world spent billions of dollars remediating software that, due to sensible-at-the-time memory-saving measures from the early years of business computing, couldn’t comfortably handle dates after December 31, 1999. Historians now cite the global cost of Y2K remediation at $300-$600 billion. It's widely thought that money was not well-spent. The GCPM also worries about connected devices. More than 5 million people around the world have an advanced form of age-related macular degeneration (AMD) called geographic atrophy, in which cells in the retinas die off. And until now, no treatment could restore their vision. The PRIMA system, which was approved in Europe in July and is awaiting FDA clearance in the US, changes that. It combines a retinal implant with camera-­equipped eyeglasses to improve sight in patients who develop a blind spot in the middle of their vision due to advanced AMD. After training sessions, more than 80% of recipients were able to read, do crosswords, and use their phones. Developed by the California-based Science Corporation, the system takes advantage of the fact that AMD doesn’t damage all retinal circuitry; only photoreceptors—the cells that convert light into an electrical signal—die off. PRIMA’s implant mimics those cells and transmits visual information directly to healthy eye circuitry and on to the brain. “We’re leveraging all of the body’s own natural functions to process and transmit the information,” says Frank Brodie, an ophthalmologist at the University of California, San Francisco, and Science Corp.'s chief medical officer for ophthalmology. Glasses outfitted with a miniature front-facing camera capture what someone is seeing in real time. The video feed is roughly five megapixels running at 30 frames per second, which is similar to TV. The raw video is transmitted to a connected processor via a fiber-optic cable. A small, handheld processor, which can be set on a table or tucked in a pocket, converts the video feed into infrared light signals that route back to the glasses. The device also includes physical buttons that let the wearer adjust contrast, brightness, and zoom. A laser projector on the glasses faces the wearer and shines pulses of near-infrared light directly at the eye at 880 nanometers—an invisible wavelength. This signal passes through the pupil and on to the retinal implant in the back of the eye. The implant is made up of 378 pixels, each with an electrode and two photovoltaic cells, which do double duty. They convert the light into a current that healthy bipolar and ganglion cells pass on to the brain. They also act as solar cells to power the chip, eliminating the need for a battery. Teens have successfully explored many tactics for bypassing bans, from using a sibling’s face for ID to drawing fake facial hair. Some proponents of bans have called for an increased focus on enforcement that blocks these workarounds. But circumvention is a signal, not a nuisance or something to be simply deterred. The fact that kids are actively looking for ways to outwit the bans highlights their limitations. Social media bans suppress kids’ access to information, including information that’s essential to their development, identity formation, and socialization, especially during adolescence. Worse, further restriction is likely to backfire. Closing loopholes in age gates could harm kids by pushing them toward less-moderated, higher-risk corners of the web. It also degrades privacy and security for everyone by forcing people to submit sensitive personal information to prove their age. In our work, we’ve found that parents and kids both want smarter, more nuanced policies that protect children, families, and their rights. That can take the form of mandating stronger limits on data collection by companies, or giving families flexible tools to control content and access on their own terms. Age gates won’t bring about the desired change, nor will they simplify parenting in the digital world. At best, they will only raise thornier questions and postpone the conversation to a child’s 16th birthday.
