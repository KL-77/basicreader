Seabirds racing home over the ocean can get jet-lagged

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-31

Visit Original Website
Flying across several time zones often causes jet lag, a feeling when the body insists it’s the middle of the night while the clock says it’s lunchtime. Now it turns out this nuisance is not exclusive to humans. A new Science study on Manx shearwaters, seabirds that can fly hundreds of kilometers a day over the North Atlantic, shows they jet-lag themselves during their excursions. And because these birds navigate by the Sun, that jet lag leaves a measurable mark—it skews their compass. Patrick Lewin is a behavioral and sensory ecologist at the University of Oxford and lead author of the study. “Manx shearwaters are medium-sized birds, about the same size as a pigeon,” Lewin said. “They are white underneath and black on top, but they have very long wings, and they are closely related to albatross.” Manx shearwaters nest in burrows on coastal islands around the North Atlantic. Most breed around the UK and Ireland, with about half of their population living in Wales. From there, they head out to feed anywhere from a few dozen to more than 1,000 kilometers into the Atlantic. To get back, shearwaters are thought to work out where they are located using a map sense, possibly based on smells, and then use the Sun as a compass to set their direction. The trouble with navigating by the Sun, though, is that it moves across the sky. “You can imagine an animal or a human that wants to travel south all day. At sunrise, when the Sun’s in the east, it needs to be traveling 90 degrees clockwise of the Sun, but by midday it needs to be traveling straight towards the Sun, and by sunset it needs to be traveling 90 degrees anticlockwise of the Sun,” Lewin said. Effective navigation by the Sun requires a clock that tells you what time of day it is. Birds, like nearly all animals, have such a clock built in. But the clock in Manx shearwaters is not perfect. The birds recalibrate their internal clock at every dusk and dawn. This means that for every degree of longitude a bird covers between sunrise and sunset, its clock falls four minutes out of sync with local time. In consequence, a Manx shearwater misjudges where the Sun should be and veers off course clockwise when it flies east, or anticlockwise when it flies west. Scientists had already shown something similar in other birds by catching penguins and quickly moving them across lines of longitude, but before Lewin’s study no one had clearly demonstrated it in wild animals moving on their own, mostly because the effect is small, and so requires a lot of data to become apparent. But Lewin’s team happened to have just the right database. Tim Guilford, Lewin’s supervisor at Oxford, and Chris Perrins, a long-time Oxford shearwater researcher, started fitting shearwaters with GPS tags in 2004, when the devices first became small enough for a bird this size. Two decades later, the team had tracks from 3,688 foraging trips made by 1,045 birds from colonies along the UK’s western coasts. Those trips last from one day up to two weeks. From this dataset, Lewin and his colleagues separated homeward flights, which the scientists say was rather simple. Foraging shearwaters make short, wiggly flights between dives. “When they are deciding to go back to their colony, they kind of switch into this mode where they are flying as fast as they can in pretty much a straight line continuously without stopping for really long periods,” Lewin said. For each homing flight, the researchers worked out where the bird last saw the sunrise, at which point its clock should have matched local time. Then, at every GPS fix, they calculated how far east or west it had traveled since, and how much that would bend its Sun compass. Since they knew each bird was headed exactly to its burrow, they could compare those predictions with how the birds actually deviated from their course. The time shifts were tiny. “About an hour is pretty much the maximum that we observed. Most of these are probably 15-minute, 10-minute time differences,” Lewin says. “For us, that would be pretty imperceptible, but it does still have an effect on their compass orientation that we can detect.” Across 335 homing flights made out of sight of land, the birds veered just as the Sun compass predictions said they would. Because the study was not a controlled experiment, though, Lewin argues we need to be careful about causality. Still, the team calculated the biases that crosswinds, the Coriolis effect, or a magnetic compass might introduce, and none of those fit the data well. “Our observed biases match by far the best to the Sun compass biases that we would expect,” Lewin said. The navigational errors apparently caused by jet lag reached up to around 15 degrees, which for birds was way less costly than one might expect. A bird with a map sense can check its position, notice it’s drifting, and correct. The team calculated that for a shearwater flying 1,000 kilometers east in a day, about the maximum the scientists observed, the average error would be roughly seven degrees. If the bird keeps checking where it is, that adds only five kilometers to the trip. The researchers also found that when shearwaters followed coastlines, they still drifted slightly closer to or farther from the shore depending on their expected compass error. Work done in Oxford two decades ago showed that homing pigeons with shifted clocks do the same, even while following a familiar road. This led researchers to hypothesize that birds may run two parallel navigation systems, one based on the compass and one on familiar landmarks, with the balance between them never tipping entirely to one side. An alternative explanation, Lewin suggests, might be that they don’t know exactly where on the coast they are and use the Sun to pick which way to follow it. “We don’t quite know. And I think it’s an interesting question, especially as people try and design bio-inspired autonomous navigation systems,” Lewin says. Lewin’s study could not answer whether Manx shearwaters suffer from other jet lag symptoms like poor sleep or hunger at odd hours. “We’ve not been able to investigate that yet,” Lewin said. The next step for his team would be finding out whether the time shifts affect when birds forage, how much food they bring home, and their breeding success. Another question is whether more animals routinely experience jet lag. Lewin thinks it’s quite likely. The same jet lag effect, Lewin said, could hit any organism with an internal clock that moves far enough east or west in a single day. That mostly means fliers, or animals living near the poles, where lines of longitude are packed closer together. The long-term goal is to find out whether animals that constantly suffer from jet lag evolved some ways to cope with it. “In humans, if you do it a lot, in the long term it has really negative health impacts,” Lewin said. “So maybe we could take some inspiration from these animals that are constantly experiencing jet lag and see if there are ways that we can mitigate jet lag affecting humans.” Science, 2026.  DOI: 10.1126/science.adz2542

The FDA wants to pull its approval of a drug. It’s not so easy.

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-31

Visit Original Website
In May 2021, the US Food and Drug Administration released a lengthy briefing document on an experimental medication for a rare autoimmune form of vasculitis that often damages the kidneys and lungs. The agency was on the fence about whether to approve the treatment, and it wanted an independent advisory committee to weigh in. Among the questions at hand: Was the drug meaningfully better than existing therapies? Participants in the study, which had been conducted by the California biotechnology company ChemoCentryx, did appear to have slightly higher rates of symptom remission after one year. But according to the FDA, it was hard to interpret the results because the study’s design had been less than optimal. Some advisory committee members expressed a similar view. The effect of the drug, said one, “is just not well verified by this study.” At the same time, many patients, and some physicians, saw in the data a potentially helpful medication, one that might lessen patients’ reliance on steroids, which are highly effective but often cause severe side effects. The advisory committee ultimately voted narrowly to recommend approval, and the FDA followed that advice, greenlighting the drug on October 7, 2021. The drug, marketed as Tavneos, started making its way into clinics. A year after approval, ChemoCentryx was acquired by Amgen, one of the world’s largest pharmaceutical companies. The cost, even today, isn’t minor: $220,000 per year, according to Drugs.com. In fiscal year 2025, sales of Tavneos reached nearly half a billion dollars. This financial windfall has unfolded despite a long-roiling investor lawsuit—one that incidentally uncovered what the FDA today believes is evidence that ChemoCentryx employees manipulated the data in the pivotal trial submitted to the agency. In April of this year, the FDA issued a letter proposing that Tavneos be withdrawn from the US market, citing reports of serious, sometimes fatal, liver injury, and arguing that the drug now lacks “substantial evidence” that it works. These revelations have triggered a dramatic cascade of events. This summer, the New England Journal of Medicine retracted a paper describing the clinical trial and Tavneos was taken off the market in the European Union. The United Kingdom has announced that it, too, will remove the product from the market. And on October 29, a court hearing is expected to proceed over Zoom to consider a proposed $69 million settlement for the lawsuit. But the fate of Tavneos, at least in the United States, is far from sealed. Amgen has pushed back against its critics, insisting that—whatever the details of ChemoCentryx’s data handling—its drug works as advertised and should stay on the market. In July, the company submitted a 104-page document to the FDA stating that “the totality of the evidence supports the continued availability of TAVNEOS.” Some patients and academic experts also believe the drug is an important treatment option. “This appears to be an efficacious medication,” said Eric Roberts, an epidemiologist at the University of California, San Francisco, who co-authored an independent registry study of Tavneos. The dispute is emblematic of the FDA’s longstanding struggle to regulate drugs of uncertain efficacy, said Reshma Ramachandran, a physician and co-director of the Yale Collaboration for Regulatory Rigor, Integrity, and Transparency. Absent more rigorous studies, either before or after a drug goes on the market, Ramachandran worries, “we’re just going to be living in uncertainty in terms of whether or not these drugs actually work.” For American consumers, the saga of Tavneos also raises potent questions around the safeguards in place to ensure the integrity of drug approvals, particularly when a relatively small company conducts the pivotal trial and has so much riding on the success of a single drug. Chloe Cusano was a 20-year-old college student when she was struck, this past December, with unbearable pain in her legs, as well as bouts of breathlessness from routine tasks like walking upstairs or bending down to retrieve her dog’s water dish. After going to the emergency room, she was hospitalized for more than two weeks, during which she was diagnosed with ANCA-associated vasculitis, or AAV, an umbrella term for a group of rare diseases in which the immune system mistakenly attacks its own blood vessels. When it was first identified, the condition was usually fatal. Over the past four decades, scientists have developed drugs that allow many AAV patients to live a relatively normal lifespan. Because the disease can rapidly lead to organ failure, particularly of the kidneys and lungs, patients are immediately started on a high dose of steroids to rapidly tamp down inflammation. They also typically receive slower-acting medications that target the underlying disease process. Steroids, however, have severe side effects. Within a month and a half of going on the steroids, Cusano recalled, she gained 40 pounds. Her emotions were “just like crazy,” swinging from intense sadness to anger. She had trouble falling asleep, and when she did fall asleep, she was haunted by another side effect: nightmares. “I did not like it at all,” she recalls. AAV patients may need to take steroids for months to years, and Tavneos was supposed to replace or reduce their use. David Pisetsky, a rheumatologist and researcher at the Duke University School of Medicine, was on the FDA advisory committee that considered the ChemoCentryx trial data. He voted against recommending approval, in part because many patients in the control group received just one round of a slower-acting medication — rituximab — in addition to steroids when, in the real world, they would have received two, according to Pisetsky. The trial’s design, he said, made the results difficult to interpret. In an editorial in the journal Rheumatology, Mike Putman, medical director of the Vasculitis Program at the Medical College of Wisconsin, and co-authors would later make a similar case: Patients in the comparison group did not receive treatment consistent with the current standard of care. “It is somewhat debate-able about what the ‘standard of care’ would have been” when the study was done, Putman wrote in an email to Undark. “We had not yet established rituximab as maintenance therapy at that point, though most people were already doing that.” Other members of the advisory committee were persuaded, including Walter Kraft, a clinical pharmacologist and researcher at Thomas Jefferson University. He voted yes to approval. “I felt the evidence, though limited, met the societal and therapeutic goals,” he said during the meeting. Requiring another trial, he added, could delay the drug’s approval by several years. But that vote, he told Undark in a recent interview, was “based upon an assumption that the sponsor was following the rules of the road.” These days, a significant number of drugs approved by the FDA start out at a small company with few or no medications on the market, said Sean Nicholson, an economist at Cornell University. Large companies increasingly recognize that their comparative advantage is not in developing breakthrough science, which is more likely to come from “nimble, small startup biotechs,” so rather than trying to compete, Nicholson said, a big company will look to buy or partner with the smaller company. That’s what happened with ChemoCentryx, which was bought by Amgen for nearly $4 billion one year after the FDA issued its approval of Tavneos. When bringing a drug to market, small companies face a slightly different set of incentives from large companies, said David Ridley, a health economist at the Duke University Fuqua School of Business. A large company with many products in its pipeline may more readily abandon an experimental drug rather than invest in costly late-stage testing. “Small companies have more to lose,” he wrote in an email to Undark. “The entire company can be at stake. That urgency can drive them to work harder and move faster.” “In rare cases,” he added, “they might cut corners.” Back in May 2021, Tavneos’ approval appeared far from certain. Almost immediately after the FDA released its briefing document for the advisory committee meeting, ChemoCentryx’s stock value plummeted, and its shareholders sued for securities fraud. Four years later, as part of that lawsuit, a report was filed, alleging that ChemoCentryx had not, in fact, followed the rules of the road. The report indicated that the company’s own trial analysis originally found that the patients receiving Tavneos had not achieved better one-year remission rates than the patients who received the usual treatment. After company employees unblinded the trial data—meaning they looked to see which study participants had actually received the drug and which had received a placebo—they selected a handful of patients whose remission status would be reassessed. This readjudication, as it is called by the FDA, violated the study’s protocol. What’s more, the agency alleges, the patients were cherry-picked to get the results the company needed for approval. ChemoCentryx did not disclose the original results, nor the fact that it had reassessed the original data, to the FDA. In December 2025, Tracy Beth Høeg became acting director of the FDA’s Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, or CDER. In an email to Undark, Høeg noted that legally, she can’t speak to the specifics of how this issue came to her attention. But, she noted, “One of my key roles as CDER acting director was letting everyone know my door was open, and I encouraged CDER employees to speak openly with me, especially about matters potentially affecting public safety.” “When I learned of this case,” she continued, “I distinctly remember thinking this story had so much intrigue that it could easily be made into a movie.” But the repercussions, Høeg argued with unusual candor, are very real. “Essentially ChemoCentryx swindled billions of dollars by developing a drug that gained FDA approval only because they manipulated the data,” she wrote. Since the drug went onto the market, it has been linked to eight deaths and dozens of hospitalizations. “This can be a deadly drug,” wrote Høeg, “and we don’t know if it works.” Such was the essence of the FDA’s letter sent in April to Amgen, in which the agency proposed to withdraw approval of Tavneos. The retraction of the Tavneos trial data by the New England Journal of Medicine followed two months later, at the request of the study’s two academic authors. “This will be a pivotal moment for rheumatology,” wrote Putman, in a lengthy essay on Substack. It’s a story not just about Tavneos, he argued, but evidence that the normal systems of scientific oversight — including peer review and regulatory approval — “failed to detect a shocking case” of alleged data manipulation. According to Amgen, ChemoCentryx’s actions were less than ideal, but they don’t amount to data manipulation—and they certainly don’t warrant withdrawal of Tavneos. In the 104-page document submitted to the FDA this summer, Amgen said that ChemoCentryx’s re-adjudication doesn’t “align with the approach Amgen would have taken.” Amgen called it “unfortunate” that ChemoCentryx didn’t disclose its actions to the FDA, noting that it, too, had been in the dark about this. Still, Amgen pointed out, none of the underlying data were actually changed, and the reassessment of the data, Amgen maintains, was “conducted to correct a small number of errors.” ChemoCentryx was not legally required to disclose the fact that it revisited the data to the FDA, Amgen maintains. In an emailed response to questions from Undark that Alison Chartan, director of Amgen’s global media relations team, requested be attributed to a company spokesperson, Amgen reiterated many of the points made in the document submitted to the FDA. “As stated in our response to CDER,” the email noted, “the 2019 data readjudication conducted by ChemoCentryx followed a prespecified procedure to correctly and consistently apply prespecified trial rules to adjudicated outcomes. No clinical data were altered.” The email also emphasized the real-world impact a withdrawal would have on those currently taking Tavneos. “Preserving treatment options for these patients,” Amgen wrote, “is central to our mission.” (The company plans to present new data on Tavneos at a conference in November.) The FDA does have the authority to withdraw its approval of a drug, said Patricia Zettler, a law professor at The Ohio State University who formerly served as an attorney for the Department of Health and Human Services. The law requires a stepwise approach. As an opening gambit, the agency must give notice and offer the drug’s manufacturer an opportunity for a public hearing. If the manufacturer can demonstrate a genuine disagreement about the facts, then the hearing may be held. After that, the FDA can withdraw approval of the drug. If Amgen disagrees with the decision, it could sue the agency. All of this leaves patients and physicians in a potentially protracted state of uncertainty about whether Tavneos actually works. Amgen says it does. To make its case, the company has pointed to an independent re-adjudication of its pivotal trial, conducted by researchers at the Duke Clinical Research Institute, part of the Duke University School of Medicine. Although the drug still didn’t meet the FDA’s bar of higher one-year remission rates, the study participants who received the drug fared no worse on this measure. The company also pointed to real-world data, meaning data collected via routine medical care, suggesting that Tavneos can indeed reduce steroid use. Chloe Cusano is among those who would like to see Tavneos retain its approval. She received it while in the hospital and credits it with helping her taper off steroids more rapidly. Roberts, the UCSF epidemiologist, said his team’s registry study found low rates of drug-induced liver injuries among patients in the U.S., a finding he characterized as “incredibly reassuring,” particularly when care is coupled with careful monitoring. It’s important to keep in mind, he said, that the current alternative to Tavneos is “much higher levels” of steroids. Under the law, Tavneos “really shouldn’t remain on the market,” said Joshua Skydel, a clinical fellow in rheumatology at Yale New Haven Hospital and a researcher at the Yale Collaboration for Regulatory Rigor, Integrity, and Transparency. The FDA has standards for drug approval, including substantial evidence of safety and effectiveness. In this case, the pivotal trial has been called into question, and several of the studies that Amgen has pointed to have yet to be published: “I’d at least like to see them go through peer review,” Skydel said, “before hanging my hat on them.” Høeg, meanwhile, is skeptical that clinical evidence gathered since Tavneos was approved will do much to support its case. “I suspect real world data, much of which were already available when we requested withdrawal in April, will not meet the FDA’s evidentiary bar for approval,” she wrote. “We do know the data were insufficient for ongoing marketing authorization [in] the EU and UK.” Skydel additionally noted that at the time of Tavneos’ approval, the FDA requested that additional post-marketing studies be conducted. But Amgen has fallen behind on deadlines for those studies. “This episode really, for me, highlighted the way that drugs are allowed to reach the market with open questions,” Skydel said. “Our capacity to answer those questions is really only as strong as FDA and industry’s collaboration,” he continued, as well as FDA’s willingness to use its authority after a drug has gone on the market. In its April letter, the FDA argued that the stakes of this debate go beyond patients with one rare disease, stating that withdrawing the drug “is critical to protect the public health and the integrity of the drug approval process.” Absent such a consequence, the agency stated, other companies will have an incentive to act deceptively. Walter Kraft, who voted to recommend approving the drug based on what he knew at the time, also worries about the potential for knock-on effects. The clinical trial system “is built on a level of trust,” he said. “And when that trust is put into question for a narrow parochial gain, it puts the whole system at risk for other subsequent actors in the space.” This article was originally published on Undark. Once again, NASA is asking for ideas to rescue Hubble from certain death

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-31

Visit Original Website
The Hubble Space Telescope still shines in the eyes of astronomers after 36 years in orbit, but it is not as nimble as it once was. Left alone, the observatory will plunge back into the atmosphere in the early-to-mid-2030s, closing the book on one of the most famous scientific instruments of the century. NASA last raised the orbit of Hubble in 2002, when the Space Shuttle Columbia visited the observatory for a servicing mission. The final shuttle servicing flight in 2009 did not reboost the telescope’s orbit, and with the retirement of the shuttle fleet two years later, Hubble has steadily lost altitude with no way to stave off eventual destruction. The space agency released a Request For Information (RFI) on Thursday asking commercial companies for ideas on how to reboost, service, or a controlled disposal of Hubble. Responses are due November 30. Specifically, NASA is looking for information about companies’ current and near-term capabilities for capturing Hubble and boosting it to an altitude of at least 373 miles (600 km), enhancing the telescope’s performance, or guiding Hubble to a controlled reentry. “The requirement includes that the boost and/or servicing mission must not harm the HST and that this mission is expected to occur by 2032,” NASA wrote in the RFI. The contractor would be responsible for providing the launch vehicle, servicing spacecraft, mission operations, and “crew if applicable.” NASA is not committing to buying a Hubble rescue mission, but officials want to know who might be interested in mounting such a mission, how much it would cost, and which companies would have a realistic shot at reaching Hubble before it falls out of orbit and burns up on reentry. Orbital dynamicists currently predict that it will likely happen in 2033 or 2034 without intervention, but reentry forecasts can change based on solar activity. It took 24 years for Hubble, which lacks onboard propulsion, to glide from its 2002 altitude of 362 miles (583 km) to its current orbit some 292 miles (470 km) above Earth. A reboost to NASA’s requirement of 373 miles (600 km) would likely keep Hubble in orbit for at least 25 more years. You’ll be forgiven if you’ve heard this before. Jared Isaacman, a billionaire commercial astronaut, approached NASA in 2022 about a servicing mission to Hubble with a team of astronauts on SpaceX’s Dragon spacecraft. He proposed funding most of the mission, which would have reboosted Hubble by at least 30 miles (50 km). Engineers also studied using Dragon to attach a rescue pod onto Hubble with new gyroscopes to overcome problems with the telescope’s debilitated pointing system. NASA also released an RFI in 2022 for other teams to share how they might boost Hubble’s orbit. The agency received eight responses, including a joint robotic reboost proposal from Astroscale and Momentus to pair their satellite servicing technology and in-space transport capabilities. Agency officials announced in 2024 they would not pursue a reboost mission after reviewing the responses. Mark Clampin, the head of NASA’s astrophysics division at the time, said the servicing plans from Isaacman and SpaceX “were really just notional concepts.” Fast forward to 2026. Isaacman is now NASA administrator, and the satellite servicing industry is maturing. Northrop Grumman launched the world’s most sophisticated robotic servicing satellite earlier this year in a partnership with DARPA, the Pentagon’s research and development agency. Smaller startups like Astroscale and Starfish Space have contracts for servicing and refueling demos with the US Space Force and NASA. So it’s not unrealistic to think the commercial industry could reboost and service Hubble by 2032, given enough time and resources. That was one of the lessons learned from NASA’s last-minute bid to rescue one of its smaller space telescopes, named Swift, from an impending reentry. NASA awarded a $30 million contract to a startup named Katalyst Space Technologies last year and gave the company just nine months to develop a mission to fly up to Swift, grab onto it, and propel it to a higher orbit, extending its useful lifetime. Remarkably, Katalyst built a rescue satellite and launched it by the deadline, and got within about 6 miles (10 km) of Swift. Ground teams decided not to move closer after problems with Katalyst’s power and reaction control systems. The Swift rescue attempt showed a servicing mission doesn’t have to cost billions of dollars, as one of NASA’s now-canceled servicing demo projects was predicted to cost. But Hubble is a significantly more expensive asset than Swift, so if NASA goes forward with a rescue mission, the agency would like to give a contractor more than $30 million and nine months. NASA does currently not have any funding allocated to procuring a Hubble reboost or servicing mission. “Hubble still has important science ahead of it, and we should be looking at every reasonable opportunity to keep it delivering for as long as possible,” Isaacman said in a NASA statement. “If there are innovative and cost-effective ways to extend the life of an asset already on orbit, we owe it to the American taxpayer and the scientific community to explore them. Extending Hubble’s life would mean more discoveries, more science, and more opportunities to expand our understanding of the Universe.” A crewed servicing mission like the one Isaacman envisioned in 2022 is unlikely to happen now. SpaceX aims to retire its Crew Dragon fleet by 2030. In a post on X, Isaacman wrote that he expects the new RFI will “draw interest from robotics companies.” NASA’s newer flagship observatories, sometimes described as successors to Hubble, are not actually one-for-one replacements. Hubble is tuned to observe the Universe in visible and ultraviolet light, while the Webb and Roman telescopes peer deeper into the infrared Universe. Astronomers will likely have to wait for the launch of NASA’s Habitable Worlds Observatory in the 2040s for an improvement on Hubble’s visible and ultraviolet vision. “Commercial industry has rapidly evolved in recent years to offer new capabilities for in-space satellite capture and servicing,” said Nicky Fox, NASA’s associate administrator for science. “Given the time we have until Hubble’s anticipated reentry, we are eager to explore the breadth of possibilities to boost Hubble to a higher orbit while we develop the flagship mission to follow.”

Flesh-eating screwworms spread to third state and are attacking pets

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-30

For a genuine fright this Halloween, you can turn your gaze to the southern border, where ravenous, flesh-eating maggots are festering and have taken to eating people’s pets. This week, the country’s New World Screwworm (NWS) infestation spread to a third state, Arizona, which joins New Mexico and Texas in being plagued by the horrifying parasites. To date, 74 animal cases have been identified in the US since the flies re-invaded the country in June. The flies lay hundreds of eggs in wounds and moist orifices of animals, and the resulting voracious maggots feast on their victims. The maggots resemble screws and tortuously bore and twist into living flesh, causing severe, sometimes fatal wounds, before dropping to the ground to develop into adults. Because of their ability to ravage livestock, the flies were eliminated from the country in the 1960s through aerial releases of sterile male flies, which thwart successful breeding. For years, they were held at bay using a biological barrier along the Darién Gap at the border of Panama and Colombia. But the barrier was breached in 2022, and the flies have made their way back to the US through Central America. While they are largely seen as a threat to the livestock industry, they can also devastate wildlife, domestic pets, and in rarer cases, humans. According to the latest data from Mexico’s Ministry of Health, the country has confirmed 806 cases of human screwworm infestations since the flies reemerged. Nine people died from the savage maggots. In the US, most of the 74 confirmed cases have been in livestock. But some of the cases have been in domestic pets, mainly dogs. Just this week, the US Department of Agriculture recorded four new cases in domestic dogs—which includes the new detection in Arizona—as well as a domestic cat in New Mexico. That brings the pet total to 10 cases since the flies reemerged. The US has also detected one case in a wild elk, which was reported last week. Arizona’s agriculture department said this week that the state’s index case was identified in a dog in Cochise County, which borders both New Mexico and Mexico. A local veterinarian examined the poor pup on October 26 and found a wound on the canine’s back that was “heavily infested with maggots consistent with NWS characteristics.” “We remain prepared and vigilant, and we urge all livestock owners, pet owners, and other animal industry partners to continue to monitor animals closely and report any unusual wounds consistent with NWS immediately,” the state said in an announcement. The USDA confirmed the infection and said it was working closely with Arizona on a joint response to the expanded infestation.

Oh captain, our captain: Ars staff remembers Patrick Stewart

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-30

Visit Original Website
Along with the rest of the world, we at Ars are mourning Sir Patrick Stewart—the beloved award-winning actor best known as Captain Jean-Luc Picard in Star Trek: The Next Generation and Professor Charles Xavier in the X-Men franchise—who died Wednesday at the age of 86. A statement released by Stewart’s representatives said: We announce with great sorrow the passing of Sir Patrick Stewart yesterday at his home in Los Angeles, in the loving care of his beloved wife Sunny, following a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer’s Disease and Lewy Body Dementia. He has been held in the warm embrace of family and friends, surrounded by live music, prayer, tears, laughter, and the kind of profound emotion that only he could inspire after a lifetime of devotion to us all. We will forever cherish the peaceful ease and reflection on his face, an enduring reminder of his invincible grace. Through the actor’s X account, the family has requested that, in lieu of flowers, memorial contributions be made to the Johns Hopkins Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center Fund and the Alzheimer’s Foundation of America Caregiver Resources. Born in Yorkshire, England, in 1940, the acting bug bit Stewart at the age of 12, when his English teacher handed out scripts for Shakespeare’s The Merchant of Venice and asked young Patrick to read the part of Shylock—a role the adult Stewart would later play for the Royal Shakespeare Theater (RSC), among many other parts. His signature bald head emerged early, around 19, thanks to alopecia. He briefly worked as a cub reporter and a furniture salesman before winning a scholarship to the Bristol Old Vic Theater School, and he landed his first professional role in 1959, playing a marooned pirate in a production of Treasure Island for the Lincoln Repertory Company. Stewart worked regularly in theater and on British television, and his big break came in his late 40s. If you’re a Boglehead like myself, you can talk endlessly about the compounding powers of the three-fund “lazy portfolio”: some bonds, some domestic stocks, and some international ones. Set it and forget it; never look at it again until you need to. If you don’t feel comfortable with the three-fund portfolio, you might want to get a financial advisor who can pick specific stocks for you for an AUM. The opportunity cost is in your head: For a low-expense ratio, you can have some ETFs that fit the three-fund portfolio, or you can shove all decision-making onto an advisor who will charge more to manage your portfolio. All it means is someone or something will be managing your money, and depending on the amount of effort and work you want to put into it, you can pay a higher fee and get it done for you for potentially higher rewards (although FAs rarely beat the market returns of ETFs) or grab a low-expense ratio fund and call it a day. If you’re a Warren Buffett aficionado, you’ll recognize this as his ever-touted “circle of competence,” in which you stick to what you know and leave what you don’t know to those, well, who know. Invest in what you understand and on things that have long-term value, and leave the frequent trading and market volatility to those who know what they’re doing. It seems as if the king of compounding’s philosophy might not just work for investing but also for picking credit cards—and the perks you get with them. And so it makes the same sense with credit cards too: you can grab a high-annual fee card that comes with all these perks and so-called “freebies,” or if you don’t want to shuffle through a rolodex that explains credit card maxxing, you might just settle on a simple no-fee card with cash back bonuses. At least that’s according to Chris Fred, TD Bank’s head of credit cards and unsecured lending, who said sometimes, points chasing (or “churning,” as those in the know call it) might prove too difficult for the average person. “Just like Warren Buffett says to buy the index fund, a good flat‑rate card often wins out over all the fancy bonus categories,” Fred told Fortune. The concept is simple, à la Buffett: If you know what you’re doing, you are fully encouraged to open several cards, each with various amounts of points or cash back per category. If you don’t, you should stick to the “circle of competence” and opt for a blanket cash-back card so you’re not trying to day trade at the checkout counter. Churning, although a fairly new (within the last three decades) concept with regard to credit cards, might be as old as personal finance itself. You might remember the App-O-Rama days of the aughts, in which people tried to fool financial institutions by opening multiple credit cards at once so as not to tank one’s credit score with each pull. In 1999, David Phillips brought churning to the mainstream by taking advantage of a pudding promotion to earn over 1.25 million frequent-flier miles. (For what it’s worth, credit card perks had just started—take a blast from the past and go though the 2003 web page of Amex’s offerings to see the beginnings of credit card perks). In the 1900s, banks would encourage folks to open savings accounts with a free $100 or so deposit. And even in the 700s and onwards, people were purchasing silver coins at face value and turning them into the mint in England for new coins, worth more than their initial purchasing price. Long story short, churning, in various forms, has been around for a while. In the credit card world, the r/churning subreddit boasts nearly 30,000 weekly visitors, and even has a whole FAQ section about dissuading the average person from engaging in churning, offering several reasons that Fred agrees with. “People think, ‘I can always beat that 2%.’ On average, they don’t,” Fred said. Fred referenced TD Bank’s three credit card offerings, which include 2% cash back on everything, and another base 1% cash back on everything in addition to 2% to 3% cash back on select categories. When compared with other card issuers that have cardholders deliberating over which card to use at the pump versus the restaurant table, Fred said, the mental math just isn’t worth it for the average consumer, especially when they never end up beating the blanket 2% cash back they are guaranteed to get with other cards. Take, for example, a premium card that offers 4x on dining but only 1x on pharmacies and basic goods. It offers 3x on groceries, 1.5x on travel, but not transportation. The points on dining and groceries may very well exceed the 2% cash back from other cards, but it would be offset by the 1x and 1.5x elsewhere. Then add in those with multiple cards, and you have cardholders who, Fred joked, would need to constantly refer to a spreadsheet to ensure they are getting the most bang for buck—when a blanket 2% cash back would leave the cardholder without a care in the world knowing they’re getting the most they’ll get. That’s just the points/cash-back debate. Add in the exorbitant annual fees, and it really becomes a race to use all of your cards’ perks. “The higher the fee, the more benefits you tend to have,” Fred said. “It’s a dangerous proposition: You’d better start using those benefits, or it’s going to be really hard to justify the fee.” Some of these cards can cost nearly $1,000, but are marketed as being worth thousands more in perks, only if the cardholder remembers to use it accordingly. Use one card and get a monthly takeout or rideshare credit; use another and get a semiannual hotel bonus or early access to restaurant reservations or exclusive sporting events. These are designed in a way to discourage using them, Fred said. There’s a reason you have to opt into an offer on your credit card’s portal instead of it being automatically applied as a bill credit. And it’s also the reason that keeps customers coming back. “Those customers are sticky. They know they’re spending a certain amount each year in annual fees, so they’re vested,” Fred said. That’s not to mention how some card fees keep going up, and annual fees are climbing on entry-level cards, too. American Airlines announced that it will raise the fee on its Citi AAdvantage Platinum Select World Elite Mastercard to $150 from $99, its first increase since 2018, and the business version of the card will cost $150 as well. But in exchange, cardholders get three times the miles on hotels booked through American’s portal, a credit of up to $120 every four years toward Global Entry or TSA PreCheck, and a $100 annual statement credit for in-flight purchases that replaces the old 25% discount on food and drinks. United and other rivals have raised fees on similar cards, and entry-level airline cards that once cost about $100 are moving closer to $150. A recent Merry Money Survey by TD Bank found 79% of consumers are actively seeking coupons, sales, and deals, while 72% of credit card users planning to use a card for holiday spending expect to apply rewards toward those purchases. Those offers, Fred said, might be how some even budget their credit card spend. It gets complicated by the third-party partners who also offer perks to cardholders, which then is how card issuers and cardholders justify the high annual fee. “They believe they’re going to get a good deal if they keep that card and use it—and that’s what makes these ecosystems so powerful,” Fred said. Generation X spent three decades being defined by its own indifference—the “slacker generation,” too disengaged to bother with the culture wars being fought around it. Baby boomers, meanwhile, are the generation that actually got the deal the American Dream promised: cheap houses, pensions, a stock market that mostly went up. Neither group was supposed to be the one lying awake over money. And yet, Gen X continues to lag in consumer confident compared to younger generations, notching a six-month average of about 60 points on the Conference Board’s Consumer Confidence Index—flatlining around its worst reading in at five years. Gen X’s anxiety isn’t a mood; it’s structural, and it’s better documented than the Conference Board number alone suggests. This is the first generation of Americans hitting their 50s while simultaneously managing aging parents’ care and dependent kids’ expenses, at the exact life stage psychologists call the bottom of the U-shaped happiness curve. Baby boomers make up 20% of the U.S. population and hold 31% of the country’s wealth, or about $85 trillion in assets, but that massive sum isn’t equally distributed among the generation. Pew Research Center found that in 2022, the top 10% of boomer households held 71% of the generation’s wealth, creating a lopsided effect where a handful could comfortably retire, while most have few financial resources for the next chapter of their lives. A version of this article was originally published by The Conversation (CC BY-ND 4.0) It’s three in the morning. You’ve woken from a dead sleep because you’ve heard a thud in the darkness or a floorboard creaking downstairs. Or perhaps you’ve woken because you have the unmistakable sensation you’re not alone. During daylight, these phenomena might be dismissed as the heating, your neighbours or the sounds of an old house settling. But in the middle of the night, they can feel a tad more sinister. Your mind might immediately start reeling, jumping to the worst-case scenario – wondering if you’ve been broken into, or if you’re being haunted by some supernatural force. In reality, the explanation is far more simple. Things really do go bump in the night. But occasionally, the culprit is closer to home than we might imagine. Falling asleep is not the neurological equivalent of switching off a light. The brain moves gradually from wakefulness into sleep, accompanied by changes in sensory processing, muscle activity and awareness of the world around us. But these changes don’t always occur in perfect synchrony. So for brief periods, characteristics of sleep and wakefulness can overlap. The results can be surprisingly spooky. If you’ve ever been on the verge of sleep only to feel that you’ve fallen off a kerb, missed a step or dropped through the bed, you’ve probably experienced a hypnic jerk. Also known as a sleep start, this is a sudden involuntary contraction of one or more muscles as we move into the earliest stages of sleep. It’s an example of how the transition between wakefulness and sleep isn’t always a smooth one. Hypnagogic experiences can also involve vivid sounds, images or sensations. So as you’re on the edge of sleep, you may think you hear footsteps or whispers – or you may even think you see a figure in the room. Exploding head syndrome – perhaps the most alarming name for a harmless sleep condition – is another example of how the boundaries between sleep and wakefulness can become blurred. As someone is falling asleep, or occasionally waking, they suddenly perceive an enormous noise: an explosion, gunshot or slamming door – yet nothing in the outside world has made a sound. Its precise cause remains uncertain. But the phenomenon demonstrates something important: hearing a sound and a sound actually occurring are not quite the same thing. The conscious experience of hearing is constructed by the brain. So neural activity generated without an external sound can still produce an auditory experience that seems entirely real. The brain doesn’t even need to be asleep for this to happen. If you’ve ever thought you’ve heard someone call your name – only to turn around and find no one there – you’ve been tricked by your brain. Another possible explanation for the sounds we think we hear at night involves source monitoring. This is the brain’s ability to distinguish events generated internally from those coming from the outside world. Thoughts, memories and imagined speech are internally generated, yet normally retain the quality of being ours. But occasionally, that attribution goes awry – allowing something generated within the brain to feel as though it came from somewhere else. Around sleep, that boundary becomes particularly interesting. Another common – but frightening – experience that can happen at night is waking up with the sudden feeling that someone else is in the room. This experience is usually the result of sleep paralysis. During rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, when most of our vivid dreaming occurs, the brain temporarily suppresses activity in most muscles. This protective mechanism, known as REM atonia, prevents us from physically acting out our dreams. But during sleep paralysis, our waking awareness returns while this physical paralysis persists. You know where you are, but cannot move. Yet elements of dream perception can remain active. This is why you may have experienced the sensation that someone – or something – is in the room with you, despite clear sensory evidence of the contrary. One clue as to why this happens comes from experiments examining how the brain distinguishes itself from others. In one study, participants controlled a robotic device positioned in front of them, while a second robot, which was placed behind them, reproduced these movements by touching the participants’ backs. But when researchers introduced a slight delay between the movement and the touch, some participants began to feel as though somebody else was touching them. Some even sensed another person standing behind them. While this experiment doesn’t explain every felt presence that can happen during sleep paralysis, it does reveal something remarkable: the distinction between ourselves and others is something the brain has to construct. Usually, movement, touch and our sense of body position agree about what belongs to us. But if that relationship is disrupted, an internally generated sensation can begin to feel like it originated elsewhere. Now picture this happening in a dark bedroom, when you’re partly awake yet still on the edge of dreaming and unable to move. The sensation that somebody else is there becomes rather less mysterious. Perception is not a passive recording of the world. It combines incoming sensory information with expectation and previous experience to construct the most likely explanation. But at the boundary between sleep and waking, this process can become less reliable. We may hear sounds that were never made, experience fragments of dreams intruding our bedroom or interpret sensations arising from our own bodies as the presence of somebody else. The illicit drug supply has changed rapidly over the last few months, with nitazenes, medetomidine, cychlorphine, and other novel synthetic substances appearing in rapid succession. Rapid tests for emerging substances are being developed and marketed with impressive speed, and these can return results within minutes. Law enforcement, medical examiners, employers, community organizations and even the general public can use them, if their states have decriminalized them. Physicians treating overdoses cannot. The contrast is hard to ignore: A test can be used to fire someone or support a criminal case, yet it  remains unavailable to the physician in an emergency room trying to save that person’s life, when minutes are essential. The divide is largely regulatory. Many tests for emerging drugs are labeled for forensic use only, and clinical tests for living patients require approval by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as a diagnostic tool. As such, they cannot legally be used by medical clinics that adhere to federal law and funding policies. Tests used in patient care must meet FDA requirements for clinical diagnostics. Point-of-care testing—testing performed at or near the bedside—also requires an additional determination that the test is simple and low-risk enough to qualify for waived status under the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA). There is a legitimate reason for that regulatory distinction. Tests used to make medical decisions should be accurate, reliable, and interpreted appropriately. A rapid screen is not definitive toxicology, and it should not be treated as such. Fentanyl shows why speed matters. In early 2023, we pointed out that rapid fentanyl tests already existed outside clinical medicine, yet hospitals relied on expensive spectrometry testing that could take an hour or longer, if the equipment was available onsite at all. By the time FDA-cleared and CLIA-waived rapid fentanyl tests arrived, the illicit market had already changed again. Starting in 2020, xylazine spread through the fentanyl supply, introducing a new clinical hurdle: naloxone reverses opioids, but not xylazine. Patients can remain dangerously sedated or unstable even after naloxone is given. Further, xylazine is associated with necrotic skin wounds that increase risk of sepsis, and withdrawal syndromes that are more complex to treat than opioids alone. Not long after, medetomidine overtook xylazine supplies. It, too, does not react to naloxone, causes profound sedation and cardiovascular instability, and its withdrawal syndrome—which can come on just hours after someone stops using it—can trigger extreme hypertension and tachycardia that has been connected to heart attacks in younger people. Knowing these facts could significantly alter a patient’s treatment or discharge plan. There are no point-of-care tests for either of these drugs or other alarming and novel chemicals like cychlorphine, an opioid that does not show up on fentanyl tests. Because of that, clinicians have to follow information from law-enforcement seizures, public-health alerts, and months-old fatality data rather than from tests at the bedside. These data are in siloes and not easy to access or combine. The result is a system that can identify emerging drugs in seized samples and autopsies before clinicians can identify them in living patients. There is a better approach. The FDA should establish an expedited pathway for clinical tests targeting emerging drug threats, triggered when agencies such as the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) or the Office of National Drug Control Policy identify a substance of significant and growing public-health concern. The agency already has a dual pathway through which manufacturers can seek a special clearance and a CLIA waiver together. That framework could be prioritized and adapted for emerging drugs rather than forcing each new threat through the ordinary timeline. Federal agencies could also help fund independent validation. The economics of emerging drugs are unusual: a substance can appear quickly, spread unevenly, and be replaced by something else before a manufacturer can justify the cost of extensive clinical studies. Federal and state agencies could make up this gap if the threat of the new drug is significant. A small market does not mean a small public health or public safety need. None of this requires pretending a test strip is mass spectrometry. Point-of-care testing should be explicitly treated as presumptive. Positive results can be confirmed when necessary. Clinical care rarely rests on a single perfect source of information, anyway. Physicians already make decisions by combining an imperfect history, physical examination, vital signs, laboratory results, and response to treatment. A rapid drug screen would simply be another tool in that toolbox that helps dispel the fog of drug supply changes. Further, the value extends beyond the individual patient. Better hospital data could become an early-warning system rather than a retrospective record of a crisis already underway. Emergency departments are on the front lines of the overdose crisis; they should have access to the most innovative testing tools at the bedside, when minutes can make a difference. The lack of oxygen in landfills causes pumpkins to rot “anaerobically,” which releases methane. Though a single pumpkin might not do much damage, if you consider how many pumpkins are trashed each autumn, it adds up. “Maybe if we had a handful of pumpkins going into that waste stream, that might be one thing,” says Chris Enroth, horticulture educator with the University of of Illinois Extension, which organizes annual “Pumpkin Smash” events to help people recycle and compost old jack-o'-lanterns and pumpkins. “But we have millions of people who buy multiple pumpkins. Earlier this week, the New York Times published a splashy feature detailing how allies of Mary Peltola, the Democratic nominee in this year’s Senate race in Alaska, worked to “stack” the ballot with candidates who could help her defeat the Republican incumbent, Dan Sullivan. As a result of these efforts, when Alaskans go to the polls next week, appearing alongside Peltola and Sullivan on the ballot will be one Daniel J. Sullivan, a retired teacher who voted for Peltola in previous elections—and who, according to the Times, was recruited by Democratic-operative types to play the spoiler role. First and most important, Alaska uses ranked choice voting, which means that Sen. Sullivan supporters confused by their two options can simply vote for both. Second, Peltola beat Sen. Sullivan by 8 points in the August primary, and Daniel J. Sullivan picked up 2.5 percent of the vote—significant, sure, but nowhere close to Peltola’s margin of victory. Third, Alaska officials have taken great pains to distinguish the two Sullivans on the ballot, displaying their names differently and designating Dan Sullivan as the incumbent. Sullivan appealed, and in June, an Alaska trial court judge reversed the Division of Elections’ decision. The gist of Judge Thomas A. Matthews’ order, which the Alaska Supreme Court later affirmed, is that under state law, the qualifications to run for Senate straightforwardly track the language of the U.S. Constitution: Candidates must be at least 30 years old, have been citizens for at least nine years, and live in the state they seek to represent. Given that Daniel J. Sullivan checks all three boxes, Matthews found that officials had no authority to invent an additional “good faith” requirement and use it to “exclude an otherwise qualified candidate.” Matthews’ opinion does not suggest that he is naive about what’s going on here. Legacy public switched telephone network service is to be retired on January 31, 2027 Openreach has issued a stark reminder to customers that the legacy phone network is being officially switched off on January 31, and says the deadline will not be postponed. The infrastructure arm of BT Group, Britain’s biggest telecoms biz, circulated the warning this week in a letter to comms providers that use its network to deliver their services. It informs them that any customers still on legacy public switched telephone network (PSTN) connections are at risk of disruption when these are retired at the end of January, 2027. The switch-off could affect businesses and consumers alike. Beyond the plain old telephone, systems that rely on PSTN connectivity include point-of-sale terminals, fire and security alarms, and personal care alarms for elderly and vulnerable people. The move is part of a wider government scheme to get the UK onto all-digital high-speed networks running on fiber-optic infrastructure, and the legacy phone service – with its dependency on copper cabling – was standing in the way. Many households have now ditched landline handsets and instead use only mobile phones, yet for those who still want a landline, the changeover means plugging the phone into the back of a broadband router to access a Digital Voice service. Openreach has been planning this move for the best part of a decade. It has already pushed back the deadline at least once, after the government asked telecoms providers not to force vulnerable customers who use phone-network systems such as TeleCare to switch until fixes are in place. Nevertheless, public awareness seems low that analog phone services will soon be disconnected. "Openreach and all providers need to coordinate a much more cohesive campaign that reaches customers directly," said telecoms analyst Paolo Pescatore at PP Foresight. "TV, billboards and newspaper advertising should reinforce a consistent message, supported by targeted letters, calls and door-to-door support for older and vulnerable people who need extra help," he told The Register. "Customers need to understand what is changing, what action to take, and who will help them. Providers must take responsibility for ensuring people remain safely connected, especially those who rely on their landline or a care alarm." Caroline Abrahams, charity director at Age UK, agreed. Eating disorders are on the rise. GLP-1s could make things worse.

MIT Technology Review | 2026-10-30

Visit Original Website
GLP-1 weight-loss drugs are one of the biggest biotech breakthroughs of the last decade. But to some clinical psychologists, they can represent something more sinister—a potential trigger for eating disorders. A new report suggests that the global incidence of anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa shot up between 1990 and 2023—by a whopping 71%. By 2023, there were an estimated 18.1 million people living with one of these two disorders. What’s worse is that the number is likely to be much higher than that once we factor in other eating disorders. And, disturbingly, those in the field are already starting to see GLP-1 weight-loss drugs exacerbating the issue. Cheri Levinson is a clinical psychologist at the University of Louisville in Kentucky who has specialized in eating disorders for almost 20 years. She says she’s now seeing more patients with more severe symptoms than at any other time in her career, and that many of them are misusing GLP-1s. “This is the worst I’ve seen things,” she says. Eating disorders are mental health conditions in which people’s eating behaviors impact their health. They include anorexia nervosa (defined by a fear of weight gain), bulimia nervosa (in which people binge and purge), binge eating disorder, and others. It’s thought that some people have a genetic predisposition to certain eating disorders, but that some external factor is what triggers their onset. That might be stress, or social pressure to be skinny. Often, simply restricting food intake can trigger an eating disorder, says Levinson. Eating disorders—along with the pressure to look thin—were already prevalent in the 1990s (the heroin chic era springs to mind). To find out how things might have changed since then, Ruhai Bai at the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University in China and his colleagues pulled together data from studies and surveys conducted on populations around the world. The team used statistical models to fill in any data gaps. That research allowed the researchers to estimate the annual incidence of two eating disorders—anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa—for each of 204 countries and territories between 1990 and 2023. They also estimated the impact those disorders had on global health by calculating their disability-adjusted life years (one DALY represents a year of healthy life lost). The findings, published in JAMA Psychiatry on Wednesday, are unsettling. Between 1990 and 2023, there was a 71% increase in the incidence of those two eating disorders, the team found. Those conditions had a huge impact on global health, too. In 2023, they “cost the world the equivalent of about 3.83 million years of healthy life,” says Bai. Bai and his colleagues expect these figures to continue to rise. In 2023, an estimated 18.1 million people had either anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa; by 2050, the team predict, the two conditions could affect 21.8 million people. It gets worse. Those two conditions represent less than half of all eating disorders, says Levinson, who was not involved in Bai’s study. The true impact of these disorders is “probably three times the rate that they’re reporting,” she says. Bai and his colleagues didn’t explore the reasons for this rise (although Bai points out that it can’t be entirely explained by population growth). So how did we get here? Social media may have played a role. “Social media comparisons, influencers, challenges, diet trends can all be precipitating … events for an eating disorder,” says Cynthia Bulik, a clinical psychologist at the University of North Carolina, who was not involved in the new study. Bulik has been studying and treating eating disorders since the early 1980s. “Between 1990 and 2023 there has clearly been an upsurge of those types of environmental exposures,” she says. Many social media users are exposed to filtered and modified images of skinny influencers who may have undergone cosmetic surgery or extreme dieting and who are promoting “thinspiration” or “fitspiration” content. But users with eating disorders appear to be more likely to engage with this content—and to be fed more of it as a result. When researchers at the University of Melbourne investigated this phenomenon a couple of years ago, they found that the algorithms of TikTok users with eating disorders delivered 4343% more “toxic eating disorder videos” than those of users who didn’t have the disorders. They also got more content focused on dieting or exercising. Many were fed over 1000 problematic videos per month. Bulik describes social media as “quicksand” for people with, or at risk of, eating disorders. The covid-19 pandemic—and the social isolation that accompanied lockdowns—also appears to have contributed to the rise in eating disorders. Research conducted in 2021 found that the number of people hospitalized with eating disorders in the US doubled between 2018 and 2020. Researchers don’t yet have good data on how trends in eating disorders might have progressed since 2023. But anecdotally, things seem to be getting even worse. “There are more eating disorders, more severe eating disorders… and we’re seeing earlier ages of onset,” says Levinson. “We’re seeing eight- and nine-year-olds with eating disorders.” “From what I’m hearing and seeing, I am not going to be surprised if that number isn’t at least doubling again,” she adds. Levinson particularly worries about the impact of GLP-1 weight-loss drugs, which have exploded in popularity in recent years. A Gallup survey conducted earlier this year suggested that 15% of Americans had used one of these drugs for weight loss. And they’re becoming more accessible—drug companies are working on new formulations that are both easier to take and more effective, and many people can buy versions of these drugs from compound pharmacies or dubious online sources. In a small study published earlier this year, Levinson and her colleagues found that 30% of people with eating disorders had used GLP-1 drugs. The drugs might benefit some of those people. But 10% appeared to have misused the drugs. And Levinson thinks that figure is also an underestimate. Levinson says she and her colleagues have seen children develop eating disorders after being prescribed GLP-1s for medical reasons. They’ve seen others—even children as young as 11—buy versions of these drugs over TikTok. “We’re very concerned that GLPs will lead to even faster and more extreme weight loss in people with anorexia nervosa, and that patients will come to us in even more advanced stages of illness that become difficult to reverse,” says Bulik. Both Bulik and Levinson are continuing to study the impacts of these drugs in eating disorders. In the meantime, both urge medical professionals to consider the risks GLP-1s might pose to people with those disorders. Bulik contributed to a set of research and clinical guidelines, published earlier this month, that recommends doctors screen for eating disorders before prescribing the drugs, for example. The guidelines also recommend more patient education and more robust monitoring of people who take GLP-1s. If you think you or someone you know might have an eating disorder, you can find information and support at the National Eating Disorders Association. This summer, hundreds of OpenAI agents tried to cheat on a cybersecurity test. They broke out of their test environment and successfully hacked Hugging Face, a site that they believed held clues about how they’d be graded. Around the same time, another OpenAI agent accessed private files from an Australian government website. It’s a problem that’s by no means confined to OpenAI. In the months since, Anthropic, Google, and Meta have also disclosed incidents in which their agents have gone off track. And worryingly, in many cases the agents eluded the very researchers and safety teams tasked with monitoring them. AI agents are taking unauthorized actions, and the companies building and running them are mostly relying on luck and hindsight to mitigate any harmful impacts. They must do more to ensure safety. That’s why EU regulators and US agencies are rightfully calling for agent developers and deployers to monitor agents in real time. But these rules assume that companies can see what their agents are doing and why—when in reality, they lack sufficient visibility. As agents roll out across critical sectors, from finance to government to medicine, we must be able to monitor them in real time to prevent wide-ranging and potentially devastating consequences.  And while safety depends on monitoring, monitoring requires observability: the capacity to capture what the system did, process it into signals, and present those to humans or automated oversight systems. In other words, we need to know what the agent did and why it did it. And as our recent research shows, current agent frameworks—the software that runs agents— do not reliably generate the crucial observability records we need to ensure that agents are deployed safely. Agents present new kinds of problems that generative AI alone does not. They inherit the unpredictability of the LLMs they are built on, but where a generative AI system produces a single output for a human to act on, an agent performs longer tasks—it plans, picks tools, spawns sub-agents, and takes actions. So an agent’s course can take shape in a multitude of different ways, and no tests before launch can fully anticipate the risks. This makes observing the agents while they work even more challenging, and even more important. With colleagues from academia, civil society, and industry, we studied the four most widely used agent frameworks to see what signals they actually emit. We found major gaps that made it hard to achieve key safety goals, like noticing policy violations, unauthorized actions, and even human oversight failures. Key observability requirements were missing—like Agent IDs that persist over time, and records about which humans approved agent tasks. This made it hard to tell which agents did what or assess whether they were taking unauthorized actions. The good news is that these gaps, once identified, can be closed. Doing so starts with capturing two types of records: those that reveal what happened in an agent’s run and how the agent made its decisions. The “what happened” records identify the agent (while preserving privacy) and log how humans intervened and whether agent permissions or “memory” shifted, while the “decision” records show an agent’s reasoning and levels of confidence in the actions it’s taken. Take, for example, agents working to process a bank’s mortgage loans. The system examines an application and recommends denying it, and then a human loan officer signs off on the decision. In a later audit, or perhaps even in real time, a bank or regulator needs to be able to reconstruct which agent made the call, which officer approved it, and how long they took. And the bank must also analyze records of how the agent arrived at its refusal decision in order to ensure it did not use off-limits information, like details about the neighborhood the applicant is from. Recording “what happened” and “how the agent decided” require different approaches, but both are feasible. The current lack of “what happened” records is mostly a software plumbing problem that can be addressed through OpenTelemetry, an open observability standard maintained by engineers across the industry and by the agent frameworks adopting it. OpenTelemetry has launched a working group drafting shared formats for tracing agents, but they have yet to be finalized. The group should urgently incorporate standards for maintaining persistent agent identity and for recording human interventions and changes to agents’ permission or memory. And it should be done before these systems can scale, all the while attending to the privacy implications of agent ID that links to real people. Preserving “how agents decided” records is more challenging. The issue raises open research questions for model providers and researchers: Does the step-by-step reasoning a model displays, known as chain of thought, reflect how the model actually made a decision? And do we have reliable ways to measure how confident the agent is in that decision? These questions need answers before anyone sets standards for how such observability records are used. No single organization or sector can close the observability gaps on their own, but together, they can support the infrastructure required to prevent dangerous incidents and support safety. AI developers building agent frameworks, like OpenAI, Anthropic, CrewAI, and LangChain, must integrate telemetry signals recording an agent’s persistent identity, any human interventions, and any changes to an agent’s permissions and memory. Meanwhile, enterprises adopting these agent frameworks have an influential role to play; enterprises in regulated industries, like banks and hospitals, can put pressure on the agent developers to supply these missing observability records where standards do not yet exist. Enterprises must also define policies, permissions, and roles that set out what agents can safely do, since telemetry alone cannot assess whether agents broke specific rules. Finally, regulators and AI standards institutes must specify the observability records required for high-stakes AI deployments while leaving the technical details to industry. We have already seen initial momentum here: NIST’s agent standards initiative in the US is working on agent identity, one of the most salient gaps we found in our research. No bank would let a thousand unbadged employees loose on customer accounts and hope to piece together what happened when something goes wrong. Yet that’s how we’re deploying AI agents. Making their behavior observable is the first step toward fixing this. And by building the regulatory and policy ecosystem that makes it possible to act on what we see, we can hold organizations accountable. Until then, every task we hand to an agent hands over our agency, too. Our climate models have a big, dangerous blind spot

MIT Technology Review | 2026-10-29

Visit Original Website
Climate change itself is increasingly fueling climate change. Rising concentrations of greenhouse gases are driving up global temperatures, setting off dangerous feedback loops that we have not fully accounted for in our climate models—or our emissions-reduction goals. Scientists have long warned that warming would propel further warming in various ways, but a growing number of observations and studies are at last providing a sharper sense of just how powerful these cascading effects could be. And, by extension, they’re showing how much faster the planet may warm in this century. A study in Environmental Research Letters in September, synthesizing the results of previous papers, found that the additional carbon dioxide and methane released from thawing permafrost, intensifying wildfires, hotter lakes and reservoirs, and wetter, warmer wetlands alone could drive global average temperatures up 0.2 °C to 0.4 °C this century. That would come on top of the 2.6°C of warming above preindustrial levels that modelers are already expecting through 2100. The additional heat could subject many of the world’s cities to monthslong heat waves and expose hundreds of millions of people to flooding. None of the models that inform the major UN climate reports, which assess how much the planet will likely warm under various emissions scenarios, account for all these feedback effects. Many of them don’t incorporate any. That leaves a huge blind spot in our understanding of how much the world could actually warm in the coming decades—and how rapidly nations will need to slash emissions to stay within critical boundaries. A separate analysis published in One Earth in March, which examined what the feedback problem means for our international climate goals, concluded that the world may be much further off track than is widely believed. It found that just three regional sources of warming-driven emissions—thawing permafrost, increasing fires in boreal forests, and warming tropical wetlands—may shorten by as much as 25% the time before the planet consistently breaches the 1.5 °C and 2 °C warming thresholds. Our climate models and the UN’s reports clearly need to be updated to better represent these effects. Nations must also step up their efforts to reduce emissions to reflect this improving understanding of the dangers we face, says Sam Abernethy, the lead author of the paper and a climate scientist at Spark Climate Solutions, a San Francisco–based nonprofit research group. At least some of this is starting to happen. Spark kick-started an initiative, called the Warming-Induced Greenhouse Gas Emissions Model Intercomparison Project, through which more than 20 international teams are running the same set of analyses on different models to develop greater understanding of feedback effects’ potential power. Researchers involved hope that the UN will incorporate those results into its next major climate assessment, due out in 2029. That report will update estimates of the remaining emissions that can be released before the world crosses key temperature thresholds. But what is already clear is that the planet is rapidly warming and that nations haven’t done nearly enough to cut fossil-fuel pollution. And even if we did manage to halt it, warming-driven emissions could continue. So a number of the research groups involved in the recent studies have started to explore what nations could do to get feedback emissions under control. Scientists at the Woodwell Climate Research Center in Massachusetts are, for example, investigating the possibility of preventing fires or rapidly detecting them and putting them out when they threaten to burn the vegetation and soil that help insulate particularly carbon-rich wedges of permafrost (a subject I explored at length in a recent feature story). In addition, the Environmental Defense Fund’s Climate Innovation Initiative has backed lab research on the potential of adding sulfate, iron, or other compounds to wetlands to counteract fast-rising methane emissions. Several startups are also exploring possibilities. They’re looking into suppressing the lightning strikes that are igniting more and more fires in high latitudes, placing synthetic mats above permafrost to prevent it from melting, and deploying autonomous drones to quickly suppress potentially high-emissions fires before they burn out of control. Investigating all these ideas is worthwhile. But researchers are only in the earliest stages of figuring out how big a dent in emissions any such efforts could achieve across sprawling global ecosystems undergoing massive transformations. The surest path to reducing the effect of warming-driven warming remains the one we’ve known all along: breaking society’s addiction to fossil fuels.
