Nintendo's biggest ROM chip supplier is bowing out of the business

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-04

Visit Original Website
For years, the AI industry’s insatiable demand for memory and storage has had plenty of knock-on effects on the price and availability of PC components like RAM, SSDs, and GPUs. Now those shortages are also squeezing the supply of ROM chips that power physical Nintendo Switch and Switch 2 game cartridges. Chip manufacturer Macronix announced in its third-quarter earnings report that the company’s production of ROM chips “has reached its end” and that it will begin “gradually decoupling from the game industry,” as reported by Taiwanese news site BigGo Finance. That could be an issue for Nintendo, which analyst David Gibson notes uses Macronix as its “main memory chip maker” for physical game cartridges. That ROM production—which has shrunk 39 percent in the last year and now represents just 5 percent of Macronix’s business—is being crowded out by production of more lucrative, in-demand chips like NOR- and NAND-based memory and eMMCs, the company said. With Macronix’s production capacity stuck at 25,000 12-inch wafers a month—and $1.2 billion in planned new capacity taking a year or more to spin up—less profitable ROM chips are taking up wafer space that can earn more through other types of chips. This doesn’t mean Nintendo will follow Sony into a world without physical game production. On the contrary, the Switch maker said last month that it had no plans to give up on the game cartridges in the near future. But those cartridges could get more expensive to produce as Nintendo is forced to rely on suppliers like Rohm and Lapis Semiconductor for its remaining ROM chips. That could have a knock-on effect on the prices publishers charge for physical Switch cartridges, too. Earlier this year, Nintendo started charging more for some physical games than for the equivalent digital download. And last year, Japanese publisher Inin only committed to a full Switch cartridge release for R-Type Dimensions III after it said “two new smaller cartridge [storage capacity] sizes for Nintendo Switch 2” lowered the cost of producing those carts. On the other hand, Gibson notes that digital software sales represented 61.5 percent of Nintendo’s business in the second calendar quarter of 2026, up slightly from the year before. If that number keeps increasing, lower demand for ROM-based Nintendo cartridges could end up balancing out the costs involved in due time.

Hackers possibly linked to the Kremlin exploited previously patched iOS and Android flaws in an attempt to hack visitors of multiple websites, including one celebrating the 250th anniversary of the signing of the US Constitution, researchers said Wednesday. The attackers, which the researchers assessed with “moderate to high confidence” were part of Russia’s Foreign Intelligence Service, targeted other sites, many with links to that country’s ongoing war against Ukraine. The attacks began when iPhone or Android users visited one of potentially hundreds of different sites. JavaScript injected into the traffic then profiled the device. The final payloads, tailored to the type of platform visiting, attempted to steal browser cookies and, in some cases, Wi-Fi passwords and full mobile device data. One of the impersonated sites was america250.org, belonging to a non-partisan group of lawmakers, business executives, and at least one former member of the Trump administration. The attackers registered america250[.]holiday and used it as a reverse proxy to america250.org. The imposter website delivered content from the real one that added malicious JavaScript into it. “We assess that the intrusion set may have deliberately selected this website due to its association with a high-profile national celebration, potentially leveraging the event as a pretext for invitation-themed spear-phishing campaigns,” researchers from security firm Sekoia said. A second website, theowo[.]club, proxied the website Raffles of London OWO, a luxury hotel physically close to some of the UK’s most sensitive government institutions, including the Ministry of Defence and 10 Downing Street, the official residence and office of the British Prime Minister. Open source reporting indicates that Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy stayed there during a visit on October 14. “By doing this watering hole [impersonating] the Raffles London at The OWO, the intrusion set may have sought to target diplomatic delegations and high-profile guests through targeted spear-phishing campaigns,” the researchers said. The attackers also delivered their attacks through compromised websites—including a top Ukrainian gas production company—and a Ukrainian web analytics service that serves thousands of websites. Sekoia said its attribution to the Kremlin is based on the targets’ profiles and similarities to a previous attack attributed to APT29, one of the names the suspected Russian group is tracked under. The hackers relied on three infection vectors: direct JavaScript injection into traffic from legitimate websites, proxy domains such as america250[.]holiday, and the supply-chain compromise of the Ukrainian web analytics service. In each case, the injected JavaScript was the same. During the first stage, the JavaScript profiled the visitor’s browser and device. The attackers then used that information to deliver one of three zero-click exploit chains, which work without requiring any interaction from the target. One of the chains was DarkSword, an iOS exploit kit that Google researchers said customers of PARS Defense, a commercial surveillance vendor in Turkey, and at least two other threat actors have used. Sekoia said the more recent campaign used two other exploit chains that weren’t named. All of the vulnerabilities exploited in the campaign were “n-days,” meaning they had already been patched and attacks worked only on outdated versions of iOS or Android.

Uranium cubes reveal why the German nuclear project failed

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-04

Visit Original Website
During World War II, physicists on the Manhattan Project worried that the Nazi German nuclear program might achieve the first nuclear bomb before the US and its allies. That’s not what transpired, but historians have debated just how close the Germans came to achieving criticality in their last attempt at building a reactor: the B8 pile. Werner Heisenberg, who led that effort, claimed that they were close and had only needed a little more uranium. But according to a paper published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences Nexus, that was not the case: the project simply didn’t have sufficient uranium and heavy water to build a working bomb, given the design they were using. This particular research started in 2013, when University of Maryland physicist Timothy Koeth—a co-author on the new paper—received the gift of a mysterious heavy metal cube from a friend. He instantly recognized it as one of the uranium cubes used by German scientists in their unsuccessful attempt to build a working nuclear reactor. Only 14 such cubes are known to exist today. In 2019, we reported on Koeth’s quest to track down the cube’s origins, as well as several other similar cubes that had somehow found their way across the Atlantic. At the time, Koeth and his partner in the quest, then-graduate student Miriam “Mimi” Hiebert, had tracked down ten such cubes around the US. For instance, the Smithsonian Institute had a German uranium cube in storage. They tracked a third cube to Harvard University, where it was regularly passed around to students in introductory physics classes as a curiosity. Underpinning the Manhattan Project in the US was the fear that German scientists under Adolf Hitler’s Nazi regime would beat the Allies to a nuclear bomb. The Germans had a two-year head start, but according to Koeth, “fierce competition over finite resources, bitter interpersonal rivalries, and ineffectual scientific management” resulted in significant delays in their progress toward achieving a sustained nuclear reaction. German nuclear scientists were separated into three isolated groups based in Berlin (B), Gottow (G), and Leipzig (L). Heisenberg headed the Berlin group, and as the Allied forces advanced in the winter of 1944, Heisenberg moved his team to a cave under a castle in a small town called Haigerloch—now the site of the Atomkeller Museum. That’s where the group built the B8 reactor. It resembled an “ominous chandelier,” per Koeth, because it was composed of 664 uranium cubes strung together with aircraft cable and then submerged in a tank of heavy water shielded by graphite to prevent radiation exposure. As the German scientists were racing against time, Manhattan Project lead Lieutenant General Leslie Groves kicked off a covert mission dubbed “Alsos,” with the express purpose of gathering information and materials related to Germany’s scientific research. When Allied forces closed in, Heisenberg took apart the B8 experiment and buried the uranium cubes in a field, ferreting away key documentation in a latrine. (Pity Samuel Goudsmit, the poor physicist who had to dig those out.) Heisenberg escaped by bicycle, carrying a few cubes in a backpack. As Heisenberg reported, the German scientists’ final experiment failed because the amount of uranium in the cubes was insufficient to trigger a sustained nuclear reaction. But Heisenberg was confident that “a slight increase in its size would have been sufficient to start off the process of energy production.” A model described in a 2009 paper supported that, showing the group would have needed only 50 percent more uranium cubes to make the design work. Koeth and Hiebert uncovered a box of declassified documents about German uranium in the National Archives and found about 400 other uranium cubes from a separate reactor experiment by the Gottow group. They concluded that the combined inventory would have been more than enough to achieve criticality in the B8 reactor, suggesting that Germany’s secretive, isolationist approach hampered their nuclear program because the two groups weren’t sharing information or resources. This latest paper builds on that earlier work but reaches a different conclusion. Koeth, Hiebert, and their co-authors analyzed two of the surviving uranium cubes, along with combing through historical records, technical reports, correspondence, and similar archival sources to learn more about the uranium, heavy water, and graphite blocks used for the B8 reactor experiment. Among their findings: The B8 reactor would have needed between 1,292 and 1,775 uranium cubes to achieve criticality, and twice the heavy water produced by the Norsk Hydro plant in Vemork, Norway—the only source at the time—over the course of the entire war. In short, “Our calculations suggest Heisenberg significantly underestimated the materials required,” the authors wrote. The authors also cited prior work noting key differences in American and German graphite production. The former used petroleum coke. While some of this supply reached Germany, the British naval blockade in January 1940 deprived the Germans of that vital resource. The German nuclear physicists had to use metallurgical coke from the Ruhr, which had twice as many impurities as American coke. (The US scientists had similar issues until they located an unusually low-boron coke in Pennsylvania.) Thus, “no natural uranium, graphite-moderated pile could have gone critical” with the materials available to the German scientists at the time. PNAS Nexus, 2026. After alienating many long-time small- to medium-sized business (SMB) customers, Broadcom’s VMware is trying to appeal to its smaller customers. Some SMB users have reported that VMware sales representatives previously told them that vSphere Standard, VMware’s prior entry-level virtualization offering, wasn’t available anymore. A VMware spokesperson told The Register that VMware will sell a per-core subscription license at a price that will be “significantly lower” than the VMware vSphere Foundation (VVF) bundle and VCF but wouldn’t provide a price quote. IT distributor TD Synnex lists vSphere Express as costing $9,600 a year for running on up to 128 cores. The price doesn’t get cheaper with fewer cores, which still presents a challenge to the smallest use cases. vSphere Express includes basic capabilities, like vMotion, for “live migration of running virtual machines with improved migration speed and efficiency,” and high availability for the “automated restart of failed virtual machines,” VMware’s announcement said. The product only supports discrete instances, and organizations won’t be able to manage them centrally, Prashanth Shenoy, VP of product marketing for VCF, said, according to The Register. “We started listening to a lot of our lower-end customers who didn’t really want the VVF but wanted the core virtualization capability, and [vSphere Express is] really meant for that market,” Shenoy said, per SDxCentral. Whereas vSphere Standard hasn’t seen a meaningful update in years, vSphere Express will be updated as frequently as VCF and VVF are, The Register reported. Returning focus to a streamlined low-end virtualization product is an about-face for VMware, which has largely focused on enterprise-size customers since Broadcom’s takeover. This focus shift has been evident as the tech giant has reduced the number of channel partners that it works with, bundled its product lineup into a small number of expensive SKUs, and canceled plans to take its biggest clients direct. While VMware has been financially successful under Broadcom, vSphere Express suggests Broadcom may worry about losing business from smaller organizations and, more broadly, that its changes are pushing customers of various sizes to explore their options. In September, while teasing what would become vSphere Express, Paul Turner, VCF’s chief product officer, told The Register that Broadcom issued sales incentives that pushed its sales team to sell VCF. He said that VMware had “too big a focus on VCF.” Nick Watson, chief technologist at UK-based tech advisory firm C4C Group, told TechTarget today: “My reading is that Express exists because customers looked at VCF, did the sums, and started packing. This is Broadcom putting a door back on the building.” Migrating off VMware can be very costly and time-consuming, making matters particularly complicated for SMBs. For organizations that can’t currently juggle a migration alongside other projects and/or have limited resources, vSphere Express can be a much simpler and more easily attainable solution than changing virtualization platforms. Some SMBs, though, including the thousands of firms that have already migrated to VMware rivals and firms whose preferred resellers no longer work with VMware, will be much harder to sway, though. “Broadcom has lost all credibility. No thanks. We have two more locations to migrate off VMware and we’re done with this nightmare. Not playing these games,” a Reddit user said last week in response to details shared about vSphere Express. Speaking with Ars Technica in September, Dean Colpitts, CTO of Canadian managed services provider and former VMware partner Members IT Group, noted that even though migrating to the new low-end virtualization offering may be easy, Broadcom has “already shown both customers and partners they can’t be trusted.”

A New York appeals court on Wednesday threw out a lawsuit over the rollout of Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s pied-à-terre tax, ruling that the homeowners who sued had not adequately shown they were harmed. The Appellate Division, Second Department, reversed a Sept. 29 judgment by Staten Island Supreme Court Justice Wayne Ozzi that had granted the homeowners injunctive and declaratory relief. The four-judge panel was unanimous. It denied the petition and dismissed the case a week after hearing arguments. Seven homeowners, represented by former First Deputy Mayor Randy Mastro, sued the city in August. They challenged the Department of Finance’s publication of a supplemental roll of about 900,000 properties and the roughly 17,000 notices it mailed to owners who might owe the surcharge, arguing the city made owners prove they live in their homes instead of checking its own records first. The court did not rule on that argument. It found the homeowners lacked standing, or the legal right to sue, because they showed no concrete injury. Appearing on the roll or receiving a notice wasn’t enough, the judges wrote. The notice, court documents read, “serves an informational purpose,” like a debt collection letter, and the state legislature “must have anticipated that the list of notice recipients would not be exact or flawless,” or it would not have created an exemption process. The court also said the rollout confusion was too “generalized, tenuous, and ephemeral” to count as an injury. It said the homeowners offered no evidence that anyone had misused the information or that they faced stalking or fraud, and it noted that the city’s annual assessment rolls already list each owner’s name, assessed value and market value. The court also said that the cost of applying for an exemption was not enough cause for injury, as all taxpayers spend time and money complying with tax rules, and the homeowners had not documented or quantified their costs. Two of the seven petitioners, Rachel O’Brien and Carmine Morano, never alleged they received a notice. The other five were exempted after the suit was filed. The panel sided with the homeowners on two points: It rejected the city’s argument that exempting the petitioners made the case moot, and its argument that the tax law limits challenges to appeals before the city’s Tax Commission. It also declined to decide whether DOF broke its own rule by not relying on 2024 income-tax data when it chose who got notices. The ruling does not settle the tax’s other legal fights. Gothamist reported the surcharge faces three separate challenges. The city has also changed course since the notices went out. After the state sent early 2025 income-tax data on Aug. 12, DOF cleared 1,210 owners and said it would send new letters to about 10,800 others. Gothamist reported that DOF has now granted more than 8,800 exemption requests. Comptroller Mark Levine estimated earlier this year the tax could raise up to $380 million from about 11,200 properties. Mamdani has said it will raise $500 million a year. After ChatGPT launched in late 2022, tech companies raced to open data centers at unprecedented rates, pouring billions of dollars into AI hubs in states like Texas and Ohio. Amazon, Microsoft, Oracle, and others did so even as polling showed the centers were highly unpopular among a large swath of the U.S. population. On Tuesday, the issue came to a decisive tipping point. U.S. voters delivered an overwhelming victory for Democratic candidates throughout the country that will upend the halls of power in Washington and data center hotbeds like Texas. While tech companies have established years-long plans to keep opening the centers, and are regularly plugging more online, they will be met with new state and national political leaders whose mandate is to slow down the unpopular facilities. They also now face a wave of voter rejection on numerous projects across the U.S. Voters sided against data center development in all but one of 38 local ballot contests with complete counts tracked by Redbud Consulting, a Washington, D.C., polling and data science firm, according to preliminary results. That includes Piqua, a 20,000-person city in west central Ohio, where Meta is planning to spend $1 billion to construct a data center with a capacity of up to 1.4 gigawatts. Residents voted to recall the mayor and two city commissioners who supported the project. In Ohio, voters in at least 14 municipalities approved data center bans or other restrictions, showing that the opposition extended well beyond a handful of high-profile projects. And in Texas, Democrat Gina Hinojosa defeated longtime Republican Gov. Greg Abbott after campaigning on a moratorium on data center development until lawmakers establish additional protections for residents. Texas has more than 400 data center facilities and many more under construction, according to the American Edge Project and Technology Councils of North America. “Most definitely, there will be challenges — two or three steps forward, one step back,” Baron Fung, senior research director at market research firm Dell’Oro Group, told Fortune, speaking about what Big Tech companies now face. “There could be delays … trying to address any kind of guidelines that weren’t really reinforced before regarding data centers.” Fung said overall progress will continue, but companies may shift projects as they deal with pushback, and they will be asked to assume more of the financial and energy burden going forward rather than relying as much on municipalities. Elon Musk, whose SpaceX expects $35 billion to $40 billion in capital expenditures in the quarter ending in December, wrote on X Tuesday night that “Data centers in space looking pretty appealing right now.” Musk spent more than $200 million supporting Republican Senate candidate Ken Paxton, who lost. The unpopularity of data centers in the U.S. has been a central theme of the AI revolution. Most Americans oppose data centers and are more fearful about AI than hopeful, according to recent Pew Research Center polls. During the second quarter of this year, at least 45 data center projects worth roughly $68 billion were disrupted by local opposition, according to Data Center Watch, a research project run by the company 10a Labs. The hyperscalers opening the centers have themselves warned about disruptions to their plans. In September, Oracle executives noted during an earnings call that the company doesn’t assume every project will arrive on time, that it has backup options, and that it brings large campuses online in phases. The company has developments across the U.S. and internationally, so “all of our eggs are not in a single basket,” Co-CEO Clay Magouyrk said. Oracle, in particular, has stood out for its risk-taking during the AI buildout, having more than $125 billion in debt and other notes payable and fundraising aggressively. Earlier this year, the company said it would need to raise between $45 billion and $50 billion in 2026 alone to fund its infrastructure expansion. Before Tuesday’s election results, Microsoft, Amazon, and other major companies had mounted charm offensives in communities throughout the country in the hopes of winning over weary residents. The strategy has centered on money, utility-bill protections, local jobs, and transparency. In October, Amazon announced “Built Together,” pledging more than $1 billion over five years to U.S. communities hosting its data centers. Amazon also pledged to publish its annual energy and water use, install lower-emission backup generators at new sites, and pay enough for power to avoid increasing other customers’ electricity bills. In January, Microsoft promised to cover the power infrastructure costs associated with its facilities and stop seeking local property-tax breaks. Amazon, Microsoft, and Google also dropped requirements that local officials sign nondisclosure agreements regarding data center projects. That may not be enough in states like Texas. Incoming Gov. Hinojosa has called for repealing data center tax breaks and requiring developers to protect household utility bills and local water supplies, while giving residents more say over new projects. “The impact may ultimately be less about restrictions than extracting more from data center operators for the social license to operate through taxes, power rates, environmental considerations, and community support,” analysts at investment banking firm Macquarie Group Limited wrote in a note Wednesday. AI cloud providers could face higher rents for power-equipped data center buildings needed to fulfill customer contracts, potentially passing those costs on to customers, the analysts wrote. Demand from large cloud companies and AI labs could further tighten the supply of available sites. Big Tech companies may also fall back on upgrading or retrofitting existing data centers, Dell’Oro’s Fung said. Amazon, Microsoft, Google parent Alphabet, and Meta are projected to spend between $720 billion and $740 billion in combined capital expenditures for calendar 2026. The Grand Cherokee is lined with wood paneling, its windshield is cracked, and chunks are cut out of its side-view mirrors like pierced ears. That’s where the cameras go, a grinning Joydip Saha tells me from behind the wheel. The manager of Stellantis’ hands-free driving project lovingly taps what he calls “the most broken-up car,” jerry-rigged with a yellow box sitting on the dashboard that lets a human grab control. It’s an industry-standard “kill switch” used in many prototype vehicles. In technical terms, the safety driver can hit the kill switch to disconnect power to the advanced driver assist system module, stopping the ADAS system from controlling the vehicle. Then, the driver has full control over the vehicle. Another piece of equipment connected to the kill switch — the STLA AutoDrive L2++ system embedded in the vehicle — beeps when it needs him to take control, a bit like a dog wearing a smart collar. And it’s confused. We are in the bowels of the Chrysler Technology Center in suburban Auburn Hills, Mich., navigating through the rabbit warren of a parking structure on the campus built by Lee Iacocca, the former Fortune posterboy who broke ground on the 500-acre site in 1986 and dedicated it in 1991, at a cost the company puts at about $1 billion. With the headquarters tower added in 1996, it spans 5.4 million square feet, the second-largest building in the U.S. by floor space, behind only the Pentagon. Stellantis still runs its North American engineering out of Iacocca’s building, and the people who work there still say “all under one roof.” Inside, it’s a pure shot of early ‘90s design. Skinny escalators climb skyward around an octagonal skylight, like a Clinton-era MC Escher drawing. The diamond-shaped parking garage attached to it is unique and confounding. Saha’s development mule heads toward the wrong exit and Saha has to put his hands on the wheel Even he finds the garage complicated. Auburn Hills’ suburban roads are much better for a test drive and I watch Saha’s mule smoothly navigate an intersection where two Michiganders decide to both make illegal multi-lane changes within seconds of each other. Saha, the global engineering manager who serves as the owner of Stellantis’ L2++/hands-free door-to-door supervised automated driving system, has been at this longer than most. He worked on the Chrysler Pacifica that Stellantis supplied for Waymo’s first robotaxi in Arizona, then managed a group that integrated Level 4 autonomy with partners including Aurora and Lyft. He still test-drives every new software build himself, as often as twice a week, because he considers any problem a customer faces problems  to be his responsibility. He said his team built the Cherokee’s hands-free system in six weeks with the help of AI tools, when a similar project used to take a year or even 18 months. He has a rule for AI, though: “I will not let people use AI to architect stuff because they will forget how to debug stuff,” he said. In a safety-critical business, he adds, engineers have to be able to answer every question. Across two days of interviews at the technology center, engineers told Fortune that AI has turned yearlong projects into weeks and overnight simulations into minutes. Stellantis wants that speed to cut development time on its new computing and cockpit software, STLA Brain and STLA SmartCockpit as part of a general push to speed vehicle development from four years to 24 months, starting with two models in 2027. But nearly everyone who described a speed-up also described checking the machine’s work. Stellantis also has problems bigger than time savings. The company has 14 brands, a European business that is losing money, and a second-quarter adjusted operating margin of 1.8%, while CEO Antonio Filosa has set a margin target of 7% for 2030. Wall Street isn’t waiting. Stellantis is the member of Detroit’s Big Three most exposed to Europe, which accounted for about 38% of its 2025 revenue. (GM sold its European brands, Opel and Vauxhall, in 2017 to PSA, one of the companies that later merged to form Stellantis.) Europe also leans much harder toward electric vehicles than truck-happy America: : battery-electric cars made up 17.4% of new EU registrations last year, according to ACEA, compared with 7.8% of U.S. sales, according to Cox Automotive. Stellantis had also bet heavily on EVs in North America, and it took the biggest hit in Detroit after the $7,500 federal EV tax credit ended on Sept. 30, 2025. In February, Stellantis wrote down roughly €22.2 billion (about $26.5 billion), more than Ford’s $19.5 billion charge in December or the $7.6 billion GM took across two charges. Stellantis shares fell 25% that day, and the reverberations are still being felt, with the stock down nearly 60% year-to-date. Mel Roberts, the director of North American infotainment and features planning, who plans the displays inside Stellantis vehicles five to eight years out, said AI was not her biggest surprise of the last several years. “What I couldn’t anticipate is just the complete collapse of the electrification ambitions and how that really did affect all of our future plans,” she said. “It trickled into everything.” Stellantis is the only one of Detroit’s Big Three to be legally headquartered abroad, in the Netherlands. It’s the result of a pandemic-era merger in 2021 between Fiat Chrysler and the PSA Group that brought iconic European brands such as Peugeot and Citroën together with Fiat, Jeep and Ram. That gives it a distinctly international air, as Fortune met executives born in France, Mexico and Bangladesh within minutes of each other, along with a Michigander back from a few years stationed in Paris. The mergers also left behind a machine built by committee. Ben Zawodni, manager of software engineering, is working to fold the hundreds of electronic control units, each a small computer with its own job, into a central “brain” called, naturally, STLA Brain. Over the decades, he says, the car’s architecture had evolved over time to really mirror the supply chain. “We were bolting on features.” Amanda French, who leads the company’s platform teams, is tackling the same problem one layer down. Stellantis historically built separate platforms, each to a fixed size. Its new platform, STLA One, is built to stretch from small B-segment cars to midsize D-segment vehicles. The company says it is designed to grow into a mega platform supporting 30+ models and targeting more than 2 million units by 2035. Asked whether building it felt like playing with Legos, French responded, “Yeah, with a lot more people and emotions involved.” Upstairs at the technical center, Chief Engineering & Technology Officer Ned Curic is a self-described “intense” but talkative and warm presence. He came to Stellantis by way of Amazon, with previous stints at Microsoft and Toyota. He explained that Stellantis is not where it needs to be. Some teams show “phenomenal successes,” he said. In building that culture, he said, “as a whole organization, we are not there.” Curic sounds exasperated with an industry that has piled on complexity. Cars now come with “five screens, like digital noise, like 350 features,” he said, and automakers have historically tended to “checklist the heck out of the features” without measuring how people actually use them. He said he learned from Toyota that “the highest quality part that you have in a car is the part that you don’t have in a car.” In Stellantis’ research on drivers, for instance, he said most people preferred a mechanical stick to a button on a screen. At Amazon, Curic received Jeff Bezos’ notorious emails forwarding on customer complaints. The body of each email was a single question mark. “I never found it stressful,” Curic said. “Never.” One executive would handle the problem, he explained, and the others would each write a couple of paragraphs to help. From Amazon and Toyota, Curic also brought small teams from the “two-pizza rule” and single owners for each system, so engineers who once sat in separate groups can’t blame each other when something breaks. “If you see something that’s wrong, you just pick it up,” he said. “I own it.” The hardest idea he’s selling is that “the best thing that you can do for your career is to make yourself redundant.” He has done it himself several times by getting a team in good shape, leaving, and starting something new. For that to work, Curic said, the company has to make sure there is always a new opportunity for those people, and that leaders need maturity and confidence. Asked whether Detroit’s long run of hard times makes people defensive about change, he replied that while there is always a bit of a fear, change is a lot more accepted, ”and actually sought after.” The man telling engineers to make themselves redundant is also hiring them. At the Paris auto show in October, Curic told Bloomberg that Stellantis would add “multiple hundreds” of engineers in Europe and the U.S. this year. Both of them are tackling Stellantis’ tricky margins issue by simplifying the wiring for each vehicle to bring the manufacturing into alignment, cutting costs and boosting Stellantis’ profits back to where they need to be. “The AIs aren’t going to tell us what the new platforms are,” Zawodni said. Zawodni said he started working on consolidating the cars’ central computing during COVID, when engineers went fully remote and Stellantis set up labs in India, Poland and North America that could be logged into from anywhere. A proof of concept for a global observability platform, which monitors software deep inside the car’s computers, took a week. Before agentic coding tools, he remembered something like that would take six months. AI could become an increasingly large part of that conversation as voters stress about data centers hiking up electricity bills, AI replacing their jobs, and technology’s economic gains flowing only to a handful of wealthy companies. Lehane acknowledges many of these concerns, but he offers few concrete proposals to prevent AI’s gains from becoming concentrated. Instead, he largely pitches AI as a path to greater prosperity for Americans willing to embrace it. Take his proposals for the tax code. He suggests incentives for workers to learn AI skills, easier capital access for entrepreneurs, and new approaches to employee ownership and profit-sharing. Those are not the same as binding requirements that would ensure workers share in AI-generated wealth or that companies pay the costs of displacing workers. “I was struck by how much of this policy is for a future…that is already predicated on mass AI adoption and AI benefiting society, which we have yet to see,” Brennan said. “All of it is predicated on benefiting from AI’s upside.” Brennan said it would make more sense to see things like a revised corporate tax rate or tax incentives for hiring human beings rather than automating jobs, as well as ways to keep companies from amassing outsize market power. Lehane’s emphasis on expanding AI’s economic opportunities, rather than limiting potential harms, extends to data centers. He notes concerns about data centers while emphasizing the construction jobs and local tax revenue they could generate. He then warns that politicians who support a data center moratorium could jeopardize America’s lead over China, going so far as to compare that stance to the 2002 vote to authorize the Iraq war. Lehane also makes child safety and education central to his proposed agenda, stressing the importance of age-appropriate protections and safeguards around AI companions for minors. He also warns against keeping AI out of classrooms, arguing that doing so could lead to inequality between wealthy students and “children in rural communities.” But the argument takes for granted that AI improves learning, a claim that remains unproven and one critics say deserves more examination than more funding or individualized attention from human teachers. Lehane also calls for enforceable AI safety standards, independent evaluations, public oversight, and the ability to restrict AI systems or use a kill switch to shut them down — though recent studies show that OpenAI and other AI labs haven’t published comprehensive plans for what they would do if a model escaped human control. Brennan called these stances “disingenuous,” pointing to OpenAI’s history of opposing legislation designed to impose such measures. In 2024, for example, OpenAI opposed California’s AI safety bill, SB 1047, which would have mandated catastrophic risk evaluations and shutdown capabilities. Last year, OpenAI also sought to limit state regulation, arguing that states could undermine American innovation. The company has more recently shifted some of those positions, backing several state safety laws and saying it would be open to independent evaluators embedded in the company. Lehane also argues that people should maintain control over consequential decisions made using AI, rather than handing off judgment entirely to machines. But that raises the question of what human control really looks like in a society where humans are increasingly being required to use AI in education, work, healthcare, and government. Throughout his essay, Lehane frames AI as a technology that could give Americans back their sense of agency and economic opportunity. But he ties average Americans’ “middle class” success to participation in an economy increasingly shaped by companies like OpenAI. Whether his policies will meaningfully redistribute power or subtly entrench it is less clear. During national elections, we often hear a lot about the rare candidates who had working-class jobs. If you follow politics, you’re likely to know that Maine’s newly elected Senator Troy Jackson was a logger, or that New York Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez was a bartender. But these candidates are the exceptions to an often-unspoken rule of U.S. politics: the working class doesn’t govern. Our research shows that people in working-class positions—manual labor jobs like construction worker, service industry jobs like restaurant server, and clerical jobs like receptionist—almost never go on to hold elected office. Working-class jobs make up over half of our labor force, but historically, the share of members of Congress who last had a working-class job has never cracked 3%. The same pattern holds in state and local politics: as of 2024, in the average state legislature, less than 2% of members have, or last had, a working-class job. This kind of polarized inequality has real consequences. Research from across multiple countries shows that when politicians from working-class jobs are scarce, people tend to trust their governments less. Studies from the U.S. and elsewhere show clearly that a shortage of politicians from the working class tilts economic policy against the concerns and priorities of working people—and in favor of the wishes of the well-off. As the old saying goes, in politics, if you’re not at the table, you’re on the menu. What keeps working-class Americans out of office then? It isn’t that they run and lose, or that voters dislike them—it’s that they so rarely run in the first place. Yesterday’s midterms were a case in point. According to our tallies, there were 933 Republican and Democratic candidates for Congress, but the two major parties ran just seven candidates in the general election whose campaign websites listed a working-class job as their most recent non-elected occupations: Tim Rogers, a delivery driver (Wisconsin-4), Brian Poindexter, an iron worker (Ohio-7), Sam Forstag, a smoke jumper (Montana-1), Ray Pooley, a machinist (Michigan-9), Christopher Reichard, an electrician (Missouri-8), Rebecca Cooke, a waitress (Wisconsin-3), and Troy Jackson, a logger (Maine Senate). These seven candidates—so few you could easily write all of their names on a Post-It note—have recent experience in the kinds of jobs that most working Americans go to every day. Studies generally find that when working-class candidates run, they tend to do about as well as other candidates—and the 2026 midterms were no exception. The one major-party Senate candidate from the working class, Troy Jackson, unseated incumbent Republican Susan Collins; she trounced her Democratic opponent 51-42 when she last ran in 2020. In the House, the six major-party candidates from the working class all ran for seats their party had lost in 2024; so far, two have been declared winners (Poindexter and Cooke) and a third is leading as the final votes are counted (Forstag). As the vote totals stand now, we estimate that the Democratic House candidates from the working class performed about 9.3 percentage points better than the Democrats who ran in their districts in 2024; nationally, the average Democratic House candidate is doing about 8.8 percentage points better than in 2024. The one Republican House candidate, Tim Rogers, performed 5 percentage points worse than in 2024; nationally, Republicans are down about 7 percentage points. In short, where workers ran, they won votes and flipped seats at reasonable rates. Why, then, didn’t more workers run? We’ve spent the last 15 years trying to understand this thorny problem in the U.S. and in democracies around the world. We’ve chased down explanations that turned out to be wrong, like the idea that working-class people are less qualified or less interested in running (there’s no real evidence for either claim). We’ve considered explanations that don’t take us very far, like the idea that things were better back when labor unions were stronger, and elections were less expensive (they weren’t). What we’ve learned suggests that workers rarely hold office because, like this year, they rarely run. And they rarely run because of what it means to run for office in our current political system. Running for office means taking on significant personal burdens, like taking time off work, giving up control over your day-to-day life, and taking massive risks with your reputation and future. Working-class people are less likely to be able to shoulder those burdens. In our research, we consistently find that qualified people in working-class jobs are more likely to express basic concerns about running. As Representative-elect Brian Poindexter said in the opening words of his acceptance speech on Tuesday night, “Who would have thought we’d put a worker in Congress?” Political powerbrokers know exactly how hard it is to put a worker in office. Party leaders, interest groups, and sitting politicians are constantly recruiting the next generation of political candidates, sometimes with splashy candidate schools and recruitment programs, and sometimes with routine encouragement and quiet offers of support and resources. None of the leaders who do this work have infinite resources; most pass over qualified workers in favor of qualified professionals who they think will have an easier time running. Workers almost never hold office because they face more obstacles, and the people who could help them will usually prioritize candidates who have an easier time running. It doesn’t have to be this way, of course. There are successful models for recruiting working-class candidates, like the New Jersey AFL-CIO Labor Candidates School, which identifies, recruits, trains, and supports candidates every election cycle and boasts a 75% win rate. There are also models of how political parties can create deep institutional partnerships with unions and other pro-worker organizations to create a pipeline of working-class candidates, like the British Labour Party did at the start of the 20th century. And there are ways to modify elections, like reserving seats for underrepresented groups or making campaigns shorter. There are also ways to sidestep elections entirely, like the randomly selected citizen assemblies that are used all over the world to advise elected leaders about the viewpoints of ordinary citizens. Working-class people rarely hold office in the U.S., but the results of yesterday’s midterms remind us that it doesn’t have to be this way. The obstacles keeping working-class people out of office are built into our elections. But every year, a handful of working-class people manage to beat the odds, overcome the barriers, and get into office.
