Now JustWatch has its own streaming service and wants people to subscribe. Launched today, JustWatch TV offers some movies available for free with ads that aren’t free on any other streaming service, including Green Room (2015), Locke (2013), and Sing Sing (2023). Other free movies include Fiddlin’ (2018), Friday the 13th (1980), and Hotel Mumbai (2018). I could play movies available for free on JustWatch TV without creating an account. JustWatch TV also has movies you can rent. The streaming service also sells a subscription for people who don’t want to watch JustWatch’s library with ads. Without getting more specific, JustWatch SVP Quentin Carbonell told StreamTV Insider that the service has “a few hundred titles across” its different tiers. JustWatch TV has 30 content partners, including Paramount and New Regency, globally and plans to add more. The JustWatch Group aims for the new streaming service to fuel its advertising business and called it a “logical addition” to the streaming guide. “[JustWatchTV] will provide even deeper audience data and insights for JustWatch Media, the group’s campaign planning and buying service for film marketing campaigns across digital platforms like YouTube, Meta, and TikTok for studios, distributors, streamers, and media agencies,” the announcement said. JustWatch Group’s advertising business already leverages first-party audience data from the JustWatch streaming guide for ads for studios, distributors, streaming services, and media. JustWatch Group says the streaming guide has 50 million customers. “From the beginning, our vision was to create a single platform that directs fans to all of their entertainment options in one place,” David Croyé, founder and CEO of JustWatch Group, said in a statement. “JustWatch TV will help fill in the gaps and will make it easier for the massive JustWatch audience to discover and watch great movies and series, including those hard-to-find titles, right from the app itself.” JustWatch TV aims to have 100 million monthly users “in the years to come,” Carbonell told StreamTV Insider.

Meta drops the word "train" from its commercial TOS after AI backlash

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-20

Visit Original Website
Hoping to quell criticism, Meta has dropped the word “train” from its “streamlined” commercial terms of service that kick in on October 30. Last week, Meta faced backlash after legal experts warned that the tech giant’s sweeping new commercial terms of service seemed to give Meta permission to endlessly scrape websites linked on business pages. Talking to Nieman Lab, experts suggested that Meta could possibly use the policy to avoid millions in licensing fees from news organizations by requiring all business users to share a wealth of AI training data simply for running a business page. A Meta spokesperson confirmed that Meta did not alert users to the changed language following backlash. This potentially only adds to confusion ahead of the policy rollout. In response to backlash, Meta’s spokesperson provided a statement to Ars, acknowledging Nieman Lab’s initial report but not its follow-up report reiterating experts’ concerns: “We’ve heard you and want to clarify: the crawling terms we consolidated only applied to our ads and business products, and we’ve clarified that in the terms themselves. It does not change how Meta uses web crawlers. As always, content owners can use robots.txt to signal their preferences. This is not about scraping news sites, and these updates don’t give us new rights that supersede those preferences.” The same experts who sounded the initial alarms were not impressed by Meta’s updates, which they told Nieman Lab do not go far enough to protect business page users from invasive scraping. Matt Topic, a media and intellectual property attorney at Loevy & Loevy, told Nieman Lab that “publishers should remain concerned.” “The revised version of the new terms still allows Meta to use that scraped content to develop any ‘business product,’” Topic said. “That doesn’t seem to be a defined term, and presumably Meta is developing AI business products. I think ‘develop’ is broad enough to include ‘train.’” Initially, Meta’s terms authorized Meta to use “any content” from websites posted on business pages “to provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain the integrity” of any Meta products, services, or features. The updated terms delete the word “train” and no longer refer to “any content.” Instead, they allow Meta to “periodically pull updates” from business users’ websites in order to “provide, develop, improve, research, and maintain advertising and business products,” as well as to help Meta “maintain the integrity and security” of its products. This scraping, Meta says, is for users’ benefit, feeding into Meta research into products to “help you create and deliver ads and other commercial content, provide users information about your products and services, and connect you with users who may be interested in your products and services,” the policy says. Following tweaks, it’s good that Meta added “more language about the permitted uses,” James Grimmelmann, a professor of digital and information law at Cornell Law School, told Nieman Lab. But “[the] ‘develop…advertising and business products’ [language] is still broad enough to drive a truck through,” he said. Ultimately, Grimmelmann agreed with Topic that “this does nothing to restrict the ‘associated with your products or services’ [language] that would let Meta scrape entire sites.” What would help—as University of Wisconsin law professor Benjamin Sobel told Nieman Lab—would be if Meta added specific language to the policy where the company agrees to respect scraping instructions in robots.txt files. Since complying with robots.txt is voluntary, Meta could possibly rely on the legally enforceable TOS omitting mentions of robots.txt to scrape sites that attempt to block scraping via robots.txt, experts suggested. When it comes to robots.txt, “you can still express the preference, and unless it’s a legally enforceable preference, it can still be ignored all the same,” Sobel said. Criticism goes beyond Nieman Lab’s reporting, as news groups globally urge regulators to intervene and block Meta’s controversial terms from kicking in. A chief executive for the United Kingdom News Media Association, Theo Bamber, accused Meta of a “shameless land grab” relying on the “worst kind of arrogant tech platform bullying.” Bamber urged UK regulators to “stand up for British creators and the business community” by “preventing Meta from setting this dreadful precedent.” Meanwhile, the European Publishers Council has already asked the European Commission to intervene. “Publishers must be able to decide whether and how their journalism is used, and negotiate fair payment,” the group’s executive director, Angela Mills Wad, said. “Burying such far-reaching claims in platform terms is no substitute for consent.” Similarly, Danielle Coffey, the president and CEO of the News/Media Alliance—which Nieman Lab noted is the largest news publishing trade association in the US—protested the terms as “outrageous and probably illegal.” In the US, Coffey urged regulators to recognize that Meta’s “decision to withhold access to its audience unless businesses allow Meta to scrape content from their websites is an appalling, bad-faith policy, and a clear abuse of the company’s market power.” “It cannot be allowed to be implemented on October 30,” Coffey said.

Google kicked off 2026 with the addition of Personal Intelligence to Gemini, allowing its AI to pull in more of your data to formulate answers. It started as a paid feature but eventually came to all Gemini users. However, this feature wasn’t available in the car until now. Google is rolling out Personal Intelligence to Android Auto and Google Built-in, and it is still, thankfully, an optional feature. As Google points out, a generative AI’s outputs are better “when it knows the world and knows you,” which is correct in an uncomfortable sort of way. The more context you can provide to a model, the better the output will be at the end of the token shuffle. Since Google has so much of your data already, it sees Personal Intelligence as a differentiator versus other AI systems. You don’t have to provide the AI context because it already has it from apps like Gmail and Calendar. Personal Intelligence is beginning its rollout in Android Auto today, and it will come to vehicles with Google built-in in the next few months. As an aside, “Google built-in” is questionable branding, but it refers to cars that run Google software in the infotainment system rather than projecting an interface from your phone. Very few vehicles have Google built-in, but Google often releases new car features on them first. This time, it’s the other way around. Google says Personal Intelligence is a natural fit for the car, allowing you to focus on the road instead of providing detailed instructions to your digital assistant. For example, you can say “take me to my doctor’s appointment,” and Gemini can get that data from your connected calendar. It can also leverage visual analysis to find locations in your photos to begin navigation. Personal Intelligence is off by default, but it’s possible you’ve turned it on at some point while interacting with Gemini, which has now replaced Assistant on all devices. Google has a habit of nudging users to opt in to data sharing in Gemini, so you may not even realize you’re sharing account data with Gemini, and those settings will transfer over to Android Auto. For Google built-in vehicles, you only get personal results if you’ve connected a device over Bluetooth. You will be able to turn this off via in-car settings even if your phone has Personal Intelligence enabled. There may be other people interacting with the screen in your car, so perhaps you don’t want more of your account data informing replies. The Personal Intelligence options are just one level deep in the Gemini app’s settings. You’re looking for the “Connected Apps” section, where you can toggle Workspace (Gmail, Calendar, etc.), Search, Photos, and more. While Google says it doesn’t use your personal data to train Gemini, it does use your inputs and outputs while using the AI. Some of those outputs can have personal information when you have these services connected, and while the company does attempt to filter that out, it won’t make any promises. The rollout of Personal Intelligence in your car is probably a good opportunity to check your data-sharing settings and make sure you’re comfortable with them. The Internet goes on high alert after discovery of 8 compromised TLS certs

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-20

Visit Original Website
Security personnel around the world are on high alert Tuesday following the discovery of eight TLS certificates with private keys so weak they could allow anyone to mint trusted browser and app credentials for any site on the Internet. As of late morning Tuesday, browser makers and some major apps and programming languages had blocked all eight of the intermediate certificates. There are no reports of certificate transparency logs, which track the issuance of all browser-trusted certificates, showing the compromised keys being used maliciously. The threat remains far from over, however, because it will be hard and time-consuming to update other key parts of the Internet, such as Linux distributions and embedded and Internet of Things devices. The eight intermediate certificates belong to India-based certificate authority eMudhra Limited. An informal advisory published to emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com over on Tuesday posted the message “We have a problem here – Wayne.” The words were digitally signed using the private key of one of the eMudhra certificates. With proof that the reporting party had control of the key, defenders scrambled because the same key could be used to sign unauthorized certificates that would be trusted by millions of servers worldwide. “This will be a massive cleanup job, bigger than Heartbleed,” Filippo Valsorda, a cryptography engineer and a cryptography maintainer for the open source Go programming language, told Ars. “There will be a very, very long tail, IoT devices, Docker containers, forgotten Linux machines, unmaintained CLI tools that shipped their own copy of the roots (which is common in some ecosystems, like Python). Also, Linux distributions are still rolling out updates, some of them are not ready yet, and then system administrators will need to apply them to a lot of machines.” Heartbleed is the name of a critical vulnerability in the OpenSSL cryptography library. In 2014, it came under mass exploitation. Exploits let many unaffiliated hackers decrypt TLS-protected exchanges between parties. They also recovered private TLS certificate keys, session cookies, and passwords. The attacks compromised thousands of servers, some belonging to large companies and government agencies around the world. Recovery required a long series of steps that took years. Steps included patching OpenSSL on all affected devices, reissuing millions of TLS keys, revoking old certificates, rotating passwords and session tokens and updating embedded devices and other forgotten systems—something that was extremely time- and resource-intensive, especially at Internet scale. “Every time you need a lot of different people in different contexts to do something, it will take a long time,” Valsorda explained. “This time it’s worse because there’s more contexts, more people, and more heterogeneity.” The compromised eMudhra keys were defective at a level that’s challenging the imagination of many of the engineers studying the incident. A normal size for a NIST P-384 elliptic curve cryptography private key contains roughly 384 bits of entropy, a size allowing almost 2384, or a number with 116 digits, different possibilities. The private key sizes of the compromised keys ranged from 3 to 8 bits. Astonishingly, the private keys were 4, 56, 66, 115, 155, 156, 230, 237. “That is extremely in the ‘this should not be possible’ category,” Valdorda said. “Maybe some horribly dangerous key generation interface has a ‘size’ parameter and they set it to 1 by mistake???” Ben Cartwright-Cox, director of BGP.tools, said: “My guess is that it’s example code that accidentally leaked to production, but hard to know. But it’s bonkers that no one noticed.” At this early stage, the most immediate threat appears to be for those using VPNs, especially in countries with governments likely to maliciously intercept traffic. There are likely many other cases for concern. End users largely have no recourse other than to hold off using many non-browser forms of TLS communication until they receive assurances updates are in place. As noted, most browsers have already blocked the intermediate certificates. The exception appears to be Edge running on Windows 10, which at the time this article went live was still connecting to 12 vulnerable hosts. In response to questions asking why and if the company has plans to do so, a Microsoft representative the company will be in touch if it has anything to “share.” Meanwhile, Signal, WhatsApp, Wireguard, and other apps that don’t rely on TLS for confidentiality aren’t affected. This page and the emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com site show apps known to be patched so far. The incident is the latest to expose a fatal flaw in the TLS regimen. It’s only as strong as its weakest link, which in this case was eMudhra. Fortunately, transparency logs account for much of that failure, but this measure requires parties to actively monitor massive feeds and works only after unauthorized certificates have been issued. eMudhra representatives weren’t immediately available to answer questions. One caveat: exploiting compromised certificates requires an active adversary-in-the-middle attack, meaning the attacker must act as a proxy between two TLS parties and receive encrypted traffic, decrypt it, reencrypt it, and send it to the other party. Such capabilities, in some cases, are usually limited to only the most well-resourced attackers. People with control of a local network—at a coffee shop, university, or other organization—face much less of a challenge.

TP-Link tries to appease Trump admin with pledge to assemble routers in US

TP-Link yesterday announced plans to assemble Wi-Fi routers in the US, as the company attempts to get the Trump administration’s approval to sell its newest routers to American consumers. The timing is uncertain, as TP-Link’s press release said it expects US-based assembly to begin “as early as” 2027. TP-Link’s application to sell new routers in the US could be rejected by the Trump administration or remain pending long enough to push the US-based assembly to 2028. “Pending US regulatory approval, TP-Link expects domestic assembly of its groundbreaking Wi-Fi 8 home networking product line to begin as early as next year,” the company said. TP-Link said it signed a Letter of Intent with US company Keytronic, which agreed “to become a primary domestic manufacturing partner for TP-Link, supporting assembly of the company’s advanced networking portfolio in Corinth, Mississippi.” TP-Link can’t sell its Wi-Fi 8 routers in the US because of the Federal Communications Commission ban on consumer-grade routers produced wholly or partly in other countries. Previous TP-Link routers that were already approved for import and sale are still available. The FCC has given exemptions to a number of prominent router makers, but TP-Link’s history as a Chinese company is making it difficult to obtain FCC clearance. Founded in China in 1996, TP-Link moved its headquarters to California in October 2024. It says that “TP-Link Systems devices sold in the US are manufactured in Vietnam and TP-Link Systems is an independent, US company that is not owned or controlled by any foreign government.” While TP-Link assembles routers in Vietnam, most of the components are reportedly imported from China. TP-Link’s plan to assemble routers in the US will still require it to import components, but the company hopes investment in the US will help it gain the Trump administration’s favor. While the FCC is urging router makers to move more of their operations to the US, a TP-Link official said the government has provided no clear guidance on how much US manufacturing is enough to obtain an exemption from the foreign router ban. “There are no published guidelines that say, ‘Well, it could be 25 percent US, or it could be 15 percent US,'” TP-Link corporate affairs officer Steve Kovsky told PCMag. Kovsky said that many components “are just not available from a domestic source, and this is not a TP-Link issue. This is an issue for all manufacturers that are trying to onshore manufacturing.” In a letter to FCC Chairman Brendan Carr and the agency’s commissioners last week, Kovsky said TP-Link is proposing “a nearly $1 billion investment in America that would result in equipping existing US factories, and ultimately building new, highly automated manufacturing facilities, to start assembling next-generation Wi-Fi 8 routers in the United States as soon as next year, creating hundreds of new jobs.” TP-Link’s letter said it will make the proposed investment if it receives approval to sell Wi-Fi 8 routers in the US. “This investment would build upon TP-Link’s existing US footprint, which includes a global headquarters in Irvine, Calif., and more than 700 US employees,” TP-Link told the FCC. “As part of this investment, TP-Link would begin assembling these Wi-Fi 8 devices in the United States by next year, and the devices will incorporate software and firmware developed, written, and stored in the United States by TP-Link’s US engineering organization.” The FCC isn’t the only agency that TP-Link has to convince. The FCC process for issuing exemptions from the ban requires each router maker to first obtain a determination from the Department of Defense or Department of Homeland Security that its devices do not pose national security risks. Router makers that received exemptions so far include Netgear, Asus, Adtran, Amazon and its Eero subsidiary, Calix, Nokia, SpaceX’s Starlink division, and others. TP-Link urged the Trump administration to ensure that its “reviewers fairly apply the same consistent review standards to TP-Link as have been applied to all other applicants.” TP-Link’s 2024 move to the US came amid investigations into high-profile attacks linked to the Chinese government, including a botnet composed mostly of TP-Link routers. The Russian GRU also compromised TP-Link routers to steal information, US intelligence agencies said in April 2026. TP-Link is far from the only router maker with security problems. For example, so-called AirSnitch attacks described in research this year were reported to work on routers made by Netgear, D-Link, Ubiquiti, Cisco, and those running DD-WRT and OpenWrt. TP-Link’s letter to the FCC last week said its application for an exemption provides “complete responses” to questions about its corporate structure and manufacturing and supply chain. “TP-Link prioritizes the security of US consumer networking data; user data processed by TP-Link’s US products is encrypted and housed on Amazon Web Services infrastructure in Virginia,” the company said. “And of course, TP-Link does not, and will not, share customer network data with foreign governments or unauthorized third parties.” TP-Link also faces opposition from Republican state attorneys general. The AGs from Florida, Montana, Iowa, and Nebraska sued TP-Link this month, alleging that it lied to consumers about its connections to China and the risks of its routers being exploited by Chinese state hackers. A group of 21 Republican state AGs then sent a letter to the FCC questioning TP-Link’s claims regarding router security and the company’s independence from China. The letter urged the FCC to require remedies for “past and ongoing state-law violations” in its review of TP-Link’s application and to protect router users “from exploitation by the Chinese Communist Party.”

Before there were guests, it was just Bartlett, reading from his diary at 2 a.m. and trying to make sense of what he had written that week. He eventually began interviewing other people about theirs. Now, The Diary of a CEO is one of the world’s biggest podcasts and is on track to overtake Joe Rogan as the most-subscribed podcast on YouTube. The show has grown into a business Bartlett forecasts will generate about $60 million in revenue this year. But growing it has also meant turning down money—including a deal that would have required more advertising and taking the show off YouTube. He described the proposed arrangement as a trade: Accept a large payment, put 12 advertisements in the podcast, and have little say over those ads. “One of the central reasons why I turned down such an offer is because that is four times more ads than I have to put in now,” Bartlett told Fortune’s Ellie Austin on Fortune Daily. Working with what he calls “fragmentation vultures”—companies seeking exclusive podcast distribution deals—can mean sacrificing revenue or control over the relationship with an audience, Bartlett argued. Instead, he sees the relationship with his audience as worth protecting, even when a lucrative offer like that comes along. The business he aims to preserve already makes money beyond advertising through products, subscriptions, and events. Bartlett, who dropped out of college to pursue entrepreneurship, launched The Diary of a CEO in 2017 by turning his own diary entries into podcast episodes. He later began interviewing other people about their own diaries, finding it more intellectually stimulating to learn about their own lives than to read about his own. Those conversations explored his guests’ motivations, insecurities, and ambitions, eventually broadening the show’s scope to subjects ranging from neuroscience and psychology to performance and sports. And with it, the business has expanded too. When asked where the show’s projected $60 million comes from, Bartlett pointed to brand partnerships, products his business owns and manufactures, subscriptions, memberships, and events. On top of that, he runs an investment fund. “It’s a wide range of things and again, this goes to the point of diversification,” he said. Steven.com, Bartlett’s holding company, houses the podcast and his other creator businesses. It also includes other creators, helping them build media brands and develop products and ventures. Bartlett forecasts about $90 million in revenue for his broader media company this year, including the projected $60 million from The Diary of a CEO. He also put next year’s holding-company forecast at about $160 million. But he’s not the only person to see nine-figure valuations as a result of the podcasting business: Call Her Daddy podcast host Alex Cooper’s holding company Unwell raised investment at a $500 million valuation in August. Bartlett sees that evolution as part of a wider shift of how creators work with brands. Early in his career, he said companies treated influencers like billboards: Pay them, and they would post something. Then came the creative-director titles, equity arrangements, and opportunities to cofound businesses. That is most evident with Sydney Sweeney, who became an equity partner and creative director at sports prediction market Novig, shaping its advertising rather than just simply appearing in it. He believes the next stage involves creator businesses making acquisitions and operating more like large holding companies. “You’re no longer an influencer, you’re no longer an equity partner, a creative director,” he said. “You are an owner or cofounder.” Distribution has become a point of negotiation across podcasting, perhaps most famously in 2024, when Spotify ended Rogan’s exclusivity and allowed his show to stream on rival platforms. Bartlett prefers fewer partnerships, and only with businesses that matter to him personally. About 70% of his podcast partners, he said, are companies whose founders he approached himself. Asha Sharma returns from one of her walking meetings around Microsoft’s sprawling campus in Redmond, Wash., to her relatively new office in the Xbox building, reached via a 1,100-foot-long bridge from the main offices. It’s a brisk early September afternoon, and Sharma is wading through the next series of changes she’ll make at the legacy gaming business. There are more layoffs to complete and more studios to consolidate. Much is still new for Sharma, the 38-year-old executive whom Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in February put in charge of turning around one of entertainment’s biggest brands. Her office, adorned with Xbox controllers, framed Halo game discs, and a Tony Hawk’s Pro Skater skateboard, at first seems like an odd fit for an executive who famously had zero past video game experience. Yet the Xbox CEO who’s replaced gaming legend Phil Spencer is moving fast. Xbox’s finances have been in shambles while its market share has tanked from its peak years ago. Sharma must fix its economics while improving its brand. She has been blunt about Xbox’s poor performance; has cut thousands of staff members; is reimagining how Xbox markets and sells its products; is upending its tech stack; has divested or reorganized smaller studios; and is focusing Xbox around its biggest franchises and publishing groups, including Blizzard Entertainment. If Spencer was a gamer’s gamer, Sharma is the decisive boardroom boss. Only Xbox’s is a very big, and opinionated, board. Every gaming conference Sharma attends, every decision she makes, and every post she publishes on social media is being closely watched by rabid gaming fans and Microsoft’s top brass. “It’s hard to be a first-time CEO … where everybody sees everything you do,” Sharma told Fortune, sitting in one of her office’s blue velvet chairs, with several packs of NeeDoh stress balls by her side and wedding invitations from fans on her desk. “We are trying to get done an inordinate amount of things in an inordinate amount of time.” The attention, Sharma said, is a reflection of how much Xbox matters to people. “The benefit of everybody caring is that you’re going to be able to pick out things that people really care about to actually make our product better,” she said. Unafraid to create tensions, the jacket- and sneaker-loving executive has come in clear-eyed and with swagger, indirectly questioning her predecessor’s strategy, and has shown a remarkable knack for marketing that plan with deftly written employee memos, new slogans, and strategic posts on social media, where meme after meme of Sharma also appear. (One deepfake video shows her yelling, “You’re all fired!” to a group of studio employees.) Flashy moves aside, Sharma has a challenging road ahead. Turning around a sprawling $21 billion business that includes hardware, creative studios, and thousands of video game titles is a task that would test even the most experienced of CEOs. And at a time when Microsoft is intensely focused on competing in the AI race, many industry observers wonder how committed the company is to the gaming business, and if Nadella has put Sharma in charge to prepare for an eventual sale or spinoff. For an enterprise software company like Microsoft, a consumer gaming business stands out as a bit of an “odd condiment,” said Joost van Dreunen, a gaming expert and professor at NYU’s Stern School of Business. “How you value, operate, manage, and finance a creative division is very different than most of the other parts of the Microsoft empire.” As Sharma works to revitalize the storied gaming unit, Microsoft employees, investors, analysts, and gamers are all focused on a key question: Can a gaming industry outsider bring Xbox back to its glory days—and if she can, under what structure will that happen? For all its current challenges, Xbox is a Microsoft success story. In the quarter-century since the software giant launched its first console, Xbox has grown into one of the company’s most visible and influential global consumer brands. Even if you’ve never picked up a game controller, there’s a good chance you recognize some of its hits. There are cultural touchstones like Halo, a first-person shooter game that became a global multimedia franchise spanning a television series, comic books, and toys. There’s Xbox Live, which helped make multiplayer online gaming a mainstream form of entertainment. And there’s the beloved Xbox 360 console of the mid-2000s, which at its peak rivaled Sony’s PlayStation in sales, and on which a generation of young gamers—including this writer—cut their teeth. With Microsoft’s 2023 acquisition of game publisher Activision Blizzard for $69 billion, Xbox gained custody of blockbuster titles like Call of Duty, World of Warcraft, and Overwatch, making it one of the most significant players in a video game market that has ballooned to several times the size of the global theatrical box office. The fact that Xbox now finds itself in fix-it mode instead of on a victory tour for its 25-year milestone is the result of what current and former employees say has been a series of strategic miscalculations, conflicting internal priorities, and a loss of focus on the basics. Game Pass, a Netflix-style monthly gaming service, helped Xbox build a subscription business. But it also led to a cannibalization of sales for the company’s higher-priced first-party game titles, especially during the critical launch period. And competing internal metrics within different teams—such as subscription revenue versus store revenue—exacerbated the problem, said former employees. As Microsoft acquired more game publishers—including Minecraft-maker Mojang Studios—it didn’t pay enough attention to its core console, which still represents 80% of its business, former Xbox employees said. PlayStation and Nintendo have offered more exclusive titles that lock in gamers. The Activision Blizzard acquisition, in particular, made the overall business more complex and intensified pressure to show returns. The purchase vaulted Xbox to become Microsoft’s third-largest business by revenue. “If there’s any place that we got out of sync maybe in the last five years, it’s not that any one of those [individual bets] wasn’t working. It’s just that we hadn’t connected the dots maybe as well as we should have,” Matt Booty, Xbox’s chief content officer, told Fortune. While Xbox remains one of the three major console platforms, it’s seen a big drop-off. Current-generation Xbox consoles have sold just a little over a third as many units as the PlayStation 5, according to third-party estimates. Higher hardware costs, fueled by memory chip shortages, are adding to the woes. A Microsoft measure of profit margin has been around 3% for the Xbox unit, a third to a tenth of competitors’ levels and a far cry from the typical expectations Microsoft Chief Financial Officer Amy Hood has for its major businesses. Sharma’s turnaround strategy is about focusing on hardware, player experience, high-value franchises, updating Xbox’s tech, and meeting gamers where they’re at. “We’re not here to do the minimum viable thing to just have a good business,” she said. “We’re here to be the No. 1 entertainment company.” Some of her vision is becoming clearer. Xbox is planning a major overhaul of Game Pass that would have players choose features à la carte, including add-ons for console-only games, cloud gaming, and multiplayer, with the system operating on a tiered structure, according to people familiar with the strategy. Sharma also plans to release a next-generation handheld device by the 2027 holiday season and the next console by the holidays in 2028. Inside the company’s secretive lab, known as Building 87 on campus, she has been plotting with lead designers such as Carl Ledbetter about the next-gen Project Helix hardware that could get gamers to stick with Xbox for mobile play, tinkering in regular stand-up meetings over prototypes and which strategic partnerships might help Xbox evolve. While she has a reputation for being blunt, she is also introverted. In meetings, she often sits back and listens, sometimes while on her phone, yet tracking every detail, stopping discussions to ask sharp, probing questions or offer direct feedback, staff members said. She’s often chewing gum, and she is known to call colleagues from her car to check in while on her morning commute. “As a leader, you’re taught to be confident, and I think being curious is way more important,” Sharma said. “If you’re confident, you might be right, and if you’re curious, you will find a better way. If people understand what’s happening, they will come up with better ideas to fix it than anyone else will.” Soon after her appointment as head of Xbox, Sharma traveled to Blizzard’s campus in Irvine, Calif., to get a sense of gaming culture and meet with Blizzard President Johanna Faries, who told Fortune that Sharma has led primarily through inquiry, asking a myriad of questions to understand how things are working. Ever the marketing whiz, Sharma had Xbox’s new motto plastered on office walls after she took over: “Great games; return of Xbox; future of play,” it reads. On her X account, which she runs herself, she celebrates major announcements and posts photos of her work travels. One recent photo captures her wearing aviator sunglasses and an Xbox zip-up, standing next to Japanese video game designer Hideo Kojima, who will develop his much-anticipated stealth game Physint with Xbox after a deal with Sony fell through. Sharma recently created a new arm within Xbox meant to centralize and double down on commercial opportunities for top franchises such as Minecraft, similar to how Disney spreads its Marvel brand into movies, shows, theme park attractions, and more. A new team will also explore fresh ways to create and distribute its games across its devices using technologies such as AI. Xbox is also planning and rolling out more games exclusive to its platform, including Gears of War: E-Day, which launched Oct. 6. Another noticeable shift has been to steer financial resources away from smaller studios toward those that make Xbox’s most iconic franchises, with the goal of more frequent major releases. (With Minecraft, specifically, the goal is to make it more competitive with Roblox, which is popular with younger generations). Brian Fargo, a game designer who is developing the upcoming first-person role-playing game Clockwork Revolution, told Fortune that under Sharma, studios must present a clear, commercial “investable business case” rather than expecting blank-check funding. The hard-nosed approach to costs is also evident inside Xbox. So far, Sharma has cut 1,868 people as part of plans to reduce headcount by 3,200 during the next year, and she’s slashed several management layers. She’s planning the next rounds of layoffs for this month, November, and January, people familiar with the plans said, although layoff plans can change. Among the laid-off have been devoted staff members who had worked at Xbox for decades, which could mean a loss of valuable institutional knowledge. Some employees have appreciated Sharma’s transparency, but the decision to structure the layoffs in several waves has left some staff demoralized, feeling as if they are sitting under a yearlong cloud, waiting to see if their roles will be eliminated next. There have also been some hiccups: Fortune identified at least one Xbox employee who was laid off but then given a chance to stay at the gaming unit. Sharma acknowledged the high-stakes environment she’s navigating and tough decisions she’s had to make, but said she values people and doesn’t intend to be transactional. At an organization as big as Microsoft, there’s also been inevitable drama, including a recent rekindling of an old turf battle between the Xbox and Windows teams. The two sides have clashed over what software should run Project Helix, the next-generation hardware. Windows leaders wanted an upcoming handheld device to use a trimmed-down version of Windows Home. Sharma’s team, meanwhile, has sought to build and control its own software to get the best gaming performance. Tensions ran high enough that a senior Xbox executive threatened to scrap the handheld project rather than adopt the Windows build, according to people briefed on the clash. Xbox is now largely controlling the direction of the operating system for the project, though Sharma emphasized the two still collaborate closely, saying, “We can’t build Helix on our own.” Some people familiar with the matter also said engineering deadlines for Project Helix have been moved up by at least several months, putting pressure on development teams to get the work done faster, including by using AI. Responding to how AI affects Xbox’s goals or place within Microsoft, Sharma said she won’t use “AI for AI’s sake,” viewing it as a tool to solve specific engineering and performance problems such as developer workflows. Successfully navigating those kinds of conflicts will be key if Sharma is to build up the authority and credibility necessary to get Xbox back on track. Before Nadella chose her to run Xbox, Sharma led the product side of the group that built Microsoft’s AI Foundry platform and was also involved in products for the coding AI tool GitHub Copilot. She’s one of several newer or younger executives whom Nadella is entrusting as longtime company leaders continue to exit during the fast-moving AI boom. A mother of three who is of Indian descent, Sharma grew up in Racine, Wis., along Lake Michigan. Her parents divorced when she was young, and her mother worked in retail. As a teenager, Sharma worked at a golf course and sold coupon books to earn money. She’s discussed her habit of morning runs and pour-over coffee, her packed work and travel schedule, and her second-degree black belt in the Korean martial art tae kwon do. “The number one thing that I’ve worked on through [my life] is just how to learn,” she said. “How to learn the space; how to learn to solve problems; how to learn from people that are just like you and nothing like you.” After earning a business degree from the University of Minnesota in 2011, she began her career in Microsoft’s marketing department before eventually landing at Meta Platforms, where she was vice president of product leading teams behind the messaging tools on Facebook and Instagram. In 2021, she became Instacart’s chief operating officer, helping guide the grocery-delivery company through its initial public offering and the effort to scale the business toward profitability. Sharma’s specialty is jumping into industries where she has no background and quickly identifying problem areas to fix, said Eric Boyd, a former Microsoft executive who brought Sharma back to Microsoft in 2024. “If someone’s not performing, she will tell them that day that this is not working,” Boyd said. “She will change direction on the course that we need to go.” When it comes to Xbox’s trajectory, many wonder if the turnaround effort will ultimately point to a new home base. After Sharma was announced as Xbox CEO in February, staff members’ emails changed from ending in microsoft.com to xbox.com. In April, Microsoft dropped its Microsoft Gaming title and changed the division’s name to Xbox. Several current and former staff members believe Xbox is being prepped for an eventual sale, spinoff, outside investment, or conversion into a subsidiary. Some said they were aware of a high-level presentation, made after Sharma took over, that explored such scenarios. Tech news website The Information earlier reported on discussion of a potential spinoff. Judson Althoff, who is in charge of Microsoft’s commercial side and is one of the company’s highest-ranking executives, told Fortune that Xbox is valuable to Microsoft’s broader enterprise business because its low-latency demands stress-test Microsoft’s cloud, and it helps the company maintain retail relationships with giants like Walmart while connecting the company to creator demographics through Minecraft. “Asha is one of the best leaders we have at the whole company. We would not have put her on a business we intend to sell,” Althoff said. “There’s going to be a ton of speculation about all this. Time will tell.” It’s also clear that few things matter at Microsoft right now as much as AI. Most employees work on the technology in one form or another as Nadella pivots the software giant to win the enterprise and cloud computing parts of the AI revolution. Former employees and analysts have wondered whether Xbox has a place in that push. Because Microsoft is spending heavily to win the AI race, it may over time simply have to offload non-AI parts of its business, said van Dreunen, the NYU professor. “Before they drive high-margin revenue from their AI investment, they’re going to start having to—to stay warm—they’re going to have to burn the furniture,” he said. “If that’s the future that comes around, then Xbox would be increasingly attractive for selloff to fund their AI future.” On a sunny Saturday in September, thousands flocked to the Anaheim Convention Center in southern California for Blizzard’s BlizzCon. When organizers opened the gates, a flood of people ran inside. Fans passed under life-size World of Warcraft portals and a massive replica of Diablo’s demonic Lilith character on their way to lines of computers loaded with the game’s latest updates. Nearby, Sharma walked the floor with members of her executive team, taking in the fandom and people’s excitement over an announcement earlier in the day that Blizzard would release World of Warcraft: Forever, a reimagining of the original WoW. It’s too early to know whether all of Sharma’s moves are working; the next test is Microsoft’s quarterly earnings report this month. Sharma has set a long-term goal for Xbox to become one of the few entertainment platforms to reach more than 1 billion people a day. Right now, it has around 100 million daily active users. Active console usage has rebounded year over year, compared with the previous year’s decline, and Sharma said she expects financial improvements to start showing in Microsoft’s fiscal 2027. As it stands, the price of the Helix console has internally been projected to cost consumers between $1,200 and $1,800, unless Microsoft decides to subsidize the cost. That’s a hefty price tag, especially at a time when consumers have so many other gadgets, from smartphones to wearable AI devices competing for their dollars. Piers Harding-Rolls, a gaming analyst at Ampere Analysis, said the biggest challenge companies face is the rising cost of hardware. Other analysts estimate that the global console market remains capped at roughly 200 million users per generation, with Gen Z and Gen Alpha largely skipping dedicated home consoles for mobile, PC, and platforms like Roblox. The badge on his chest reads, “Innovation Fitness.” “These words are everywhere now,” he says with his ever-present smile and laugh. “It’s total saturation.” Frank has spent three decades arguing that Americans misread and misunderstand their own economy, perhaps most famously in his 2004 modern classic, What’s the Matter with Kansas?, which followed his evolution from college Republican to something else and probed why so many people from his native Kansas City area voted against what he understood as their class interests. His latest, The Creativity Con: How Innovation Became America’s Biggest Racket, takes aim at a staple of business journalism: the idea of “innovation” as something fundamental to American business. Frank is gracious toward Fortune, which publishes America’s Most Innovative Companies, runs the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum and Workplace Innovation Summit, and hosts a “Leaders in Innovation” series. “You guys have been very critical of all the stuff you ordinarily cover,” he says. He recalls that The Baffler, the journal he cofounded in 1988, kept a stack of old Fortune issues in its office. “We had this huge box of back issues,” he says, recalling the great painted Art Deco artwork that Fortune was known for in the 1930s and ’40s. “We used to just sit there in the office and pore over it.” Much of Frank’s book is about the cult of innovation and creativity that dates back to the postwar years. Much of the modern concept traces back to, of all things, the mind of an adman straight out of Madison Avenue: Alex Osborn, the “O” in BBDO. Among other things, Osborn popularized the term “brainstorm,” which once meant a mental seizure of sorts but which Osborn turned into a militaristic business term, as in storming an idea with your brains. Frank spent a week in Buffalo, looking through an archive of Osborn’s papers. He says Osborn and his colleagues collected everything that used the word “creativity” in the 1950s and ’60s to document the progress of their pet idea. “The word I use in the book is ‘ideology,’” Frank says, adding that he hopes that doesn’t sound too “political and really heavy-handed.” He describes the ideology: “This is how history works; this is how a country prospers … We’ve been saying that since the 1950s.” The problem is that it just “hasn’t really worked out,” Frank says in chipper but mournful fashion, pointing out that Osborn, “no fan of the New Deal,” pushed the ideology toward considering blue-collar workers as uncreative. “It has hurt us. It’s hurt us really bad.” Now Frank says AI has made his argument harder for white-collar workers to ignore. This is the point where the creativity gospel turns on the people who preach it the most. It’s “sort of an unspoken strain in all of the talk about AI,” he says. Just as “innovation” came for the working class and the Rust Belt as automation and outsourcing devastated communities like his hometown, Kansas City, AI now threatens the same displacement for the supposed creative class. “I have a lot of fears about it, too,” Frank says. “I’m right in the crosshairs … Nobody gives a damn about the creative class and their innovation districts anymore.” Frank’s own use of AI is mixed. He says it has “revolutionized” archival work, recalling that when he wrote The Conquest of Cool in the 1990s, he sat in a library paging through old issues of Life, Fortune’s former Time Inc. stablemate. Now the whole run is searchable; he can even count how often a word appears. When he pushed back, it said, “You are absolutely right,” before offering another wrong answer. Frank’s deeper complaint is that AI won’t be revolutionary enough: It will replicate the fatal flaw of journalism in treating authority’s misleading answers as truth. He calls the way LLMs work as dangerous: “Do the experts say it? An early journalistic coup was when Frank exposed the New York Times’ Style section had printed slang from Seattle’s grunge scene that Sub Pop employee Megan Jasper had made up. Punk, he argues, is a perfect example of true innovation, unlike the supposed innovation of the Osbornian fashion. “People talk about Paris in the ’20s or these moments of medieval Florence. The only one that’s happened in my lifetime was the punk rock explosion.” Describing it as “totally unauthorized,” he says it’s “not the sort of creative thing that anybody would have celebrated at the time.” A lot of punk rockers were “unemployed people, working-class people; they did something really amazing. We haven’t seen an explosion of creativity like that in a long time.” This is a very Gen X kind of attitude, I had to point out, which Frank mostly dismisses. “In America, we like to talk about generations, but we hate to talk about social class,” which is something he considers “really obviously the motor behind everything.” At the same time, he says younger people struggle to picture the economy he grew up in. His father was an engineer for General Motors, and all of their neighbors also worked on the GM assembly line. “We all lived in the same kind of houses,” he says. “The idea that blue-collar and white-collar would basically have the same standard of living … at the time, that was a cliché. When Ted Lasso took over AFC Richmond, the fictional England soccer team at the center of Apple TV’s hit series, his relentlessly positive attitude and folksy charm made him an unlikely leader. But beneath his optimism was a coach who understood how to bring out the best in people—even when that meant confronting their insecurities, calling out bad behavior, and demanding better. It’s a leadership style that even JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon admitted he’s learned from. “I love Ted Lasso,” Dimon recently told the Wall Street Journal. “He comes in and he doesn’t know anything about football, and he’s just gracious. He’s working with people—their insecurities, their bad behavior, their work ethic—and he’s getting them to function as a team.” The 70-year-old added that Lasso gets at something many corporate leaders struggle with: balancing humility and empathy with an unwavering drive to win. “Most of these companies have huge capabilities, IP, and great people. However, it would suit many to be more like Lasso to begin with—and speak up when there are problems, even if it may not be easy to hear. “A good leader over time figures it out, removes some of the bad ones, and the place starts to shine,” Dimon added. “It can take some time because these bullshitters have been doing it for 25 years, and they’re usually pretty good at it. It’s hard to notice in real time.” Dimon isn’t the only business leader who saw leadership lessons in the show, now in its fourth season and starring Jason Sudeikis and Hannah Waddingham. Chevron CEO Mike Wirth has also praised Lasso’s approach, particularly his willingness to listen before jumping to conclusions. “In our work, it’s easy to assume we already know the answer,” Wirth told the WSJ. “Easy to think we know what caused a problem, what a customer wants, or what the best option is. But the best leaders I’ve worked with do something different. They ask questions. They listen. They seek to understand before they decide.” That philosophy is central to Lasso’s unlikely coaching career. Russian President Vladimir Putin has thrown Donald Trump a bone that’s so big and juicy, Trump must feel sorely tempted to chase it—but it’s laced with so much poison that even Trump might calm himself and resist. The bone, according to the Wall Street Journal, is the proposal of a tripartite summit—just Putin, Trump, and Chinese President Xi Jinping—to plot an ending to the Russia–Ukraine war. Clearly the idea appeals to Trump’s idealized view of the world: great powers carving their spheres, calling the shots, collecting the spoils, and Donald J. Trump taking his seat—recognized by all—as one of those great powers. No small or medium-size democracies need apply—thus no other pesky Europeans, and certainly no Ukrainians, need take part in determining their fates. The view is of a piece with Trump’s adoration and envy of men holding absolute power. He basks in their reciprocated respect (whether feigned or genuine), feels his own power elevated, and yearns for more. During the first year of his first term as president, Trump spoke in awe of North Korean dictator Kim Jong-un: “He speaks and people sit up at attention. I want my people to do the same.” He was similarly enamored by the red carpet and sword dance at his first meeting with the Saudi royal family. These were people who ruled a country, the heirs to generations of rulers. By comparison, meeting with elected elites, whose fleeting power depends on the whims of the populace, reminds him all too much of his own limitations. But as anyone who has worked on a large scale compute cluster understands, whether for LLM training or traditional HPC workloads, more chips inevitably means more problems. In an eight-GPU node, the chance of having a GPU fail is relatively low. In a 100,000-GPU cluster, failures are unavoidable. Meta is building something that's 10x larger and requires distributed training runs across multiple datacenters. Keeping the entire thing humming long enough to reach the next checkpoint becomes a major headache. In a blog post published this week, Meta offered a unique look at the work it's done to keep failures from derailing the development of models like Muse Spark or its most recent release Muse Wonder. When Meta began the first phase of its Prometheus deployment in New Albany, the company says, it was experiencing roughly 1,000 interruptions per day for every 8,000 GPUs. Interruptions don't mean GPU failure. They simply mean something crashed, failed, or timed out, forcing the training run to stop and roll back to the last known good checkpoint. Meta claims in the year since it's been able to cut down the number of interruptions by 99 percent, to about 11 per day per 8,000 GPUs. One of the ways it's done this is by keeping some of Prometheus' GPUs in reserve. The cluster is currently powered by a combination of GB200 and GB300 systems, each with 72 GPUs per NVLink domain. However, only 64 of them are ever in use at any given moment. The other eight are essentially hot spares. Meta did this because a single failed GPU would take out an entire rack and interrupt the training run. Now, when a GPU fails, one of the spares can slot in and take its place. While this saves time — Meta doesn't have to drop the entire rack from the cluster or wait for someone to swap the faulty component to start training again — a failed GPU would still result in an interruption, albeit a shorter one. To mitigate this, Meta took advantage of the fact that large scale training jobs usually use data parallelism, where multiple copies of the model are resident within the cluster. "When a fault can be isolated to a data-parallel model replica, we reduce the impact of a hardware failure by allowing a single faulty replica to be taken offline, while the remaining cluster repeats that step and continues productive training," Meta explained. "The compromised slice simply restarts on available spare hosts and resynchronizes, converting a potential total system halt into a minor, temporary reduction in aggregate throughput." In other words, Meta's training runs only get interrupted if the fault exceeds the data parallel domain. When this happens, the company has implemented a comprehensive checkpointing scheme, which you can think of a bit like auto-save or a revision history. That way if something does fail, Meta doesn't have to start over from scratch. "Our distributed checkpointing architecture asynchronously saves the model state — including gradients and optimizer data — to multi-tier storage," the company wrote. Most IT estates have embraced technological diversity, some by design, some through standardization, some by inertia. Modern enterprise environments have become innately heterogeneous – and innately complex. They run a diverse combo of system software, hardware devices, cloud services, legacy applications, et al. But that doesn’t mean that companies’ existing applications and data simply disappear. One trying but inevitable outcome of rapid AI deployment is the so-called “VM-container divide”. Two years ago, the site was a conventional data centre running web traffic. “At full scale, Prometheus will comprise roughly 1 million distributed GPUs, all functioning in lockstep,” Meta wrote. Prometheus runs two Nvidia platforms today, both built around racks of 72 GPUs that act as one accelerator. Catalina uses the GB200 NVL72, and Clemente uses the newer GB300 NVL72. A third platform, San Miguel, will add Nvidia’s Vera Rubin NVL72 racks. The GPUs sit in traditional data centres, AI-optimised buildings and rapid-deployment structures, linked by miles of fibre. Each building’s network connects up to 20,736 GPUs. A separate aggregation network joins the buildings over distances from 2km to more than 100km. Meta also uses capacity leased from partners. At this scale, a fault on a single machine can stop a whole training run. In mid-2025, during the first phase, interruptions peaked at nearly 1,000 events per 8,000 GPUs each day, Meta said. A year of engineering work has cut that by 99%. The biggest early problem was signal quality on more than 600,000 NVLink connections between GPUs.
