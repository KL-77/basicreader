Under Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the Food and Drug Administration is moving forward with plans to ease access to unproven peptide drugs that are popular among members of Kennedy’s Make America Healthy Again movement—despite FDA staff scientists finding that the drugs have not been sufficiently studied to establish safety and efficacy and may cause harms. The FDA submitted an “Interim Access Policy” on peptides for review by the White House, according to a filing notice posted on an Office of Management and Budget website. The posting does not provide information on the proposed policy. But people familiar with the proposal told The Associated Press that it would give the FDA “enforcement discretion” on certain peptide drugs. This would allow the agency to choose not to enforce current restrictions on peptides and provide an assurance to specialty pharmacies eager to start making them that they will not be targeted by the agency. The Department of Health and Human Services, which oversees the FDA, did not respond to a request for comment from Ars Technica. The proposal comes as the FDA is currently re-reviewing a dozen popular, but unproven, injectable peptide drugs. In 2023, FDA scientists determined that the peptides lacked data on safety and efficacy and posed risks. As such, they were stricken from a list of drugs that can be made by specialty pharmacies, called compounding pharmacies, which make customized formulas for drugs. Still, the dodgy drugs have grown more popular, with sellers claiming, without evidence, that the drugs can improve appearance, boost energy, and help heal injuries, among other wellness claims. They’re especially popular among the MAHA crowd; many prominent MAHA figures sell peptide products, and Kennedy, a self-proclaimed “big fan” of peptides, vowed to end “FDA’s war” on peptides upon taking office. In April, the FDA announced that it would reconsider 12 unproven peptides. In June, Kennedy appointed nine new members to a 13-member FDA advisory panel that would review the peptides. Of the new members, at least six have ties to peptide-related businesses and clinics, raising significant conflict-of-interest concerns. In a July meeting, the panel voted to recommend adding six of the first seven peptides considered back to the list of drugs compounding pharmacies can make. The recommendations overruled FDA staff scientists who maintained that the drugs still lack data on safety and efficacy and posed risks. It’s unclear if the FDA will follow through with the recommendation. The AP notes that formally adding the peptides back to the compounding list involves a federal rulemaking process that could take years and require multiple rounds of public comment. The new proposed enforcement discretion could ease access in lieu of that rulemaking or provide access during the process. Science-based advocacy group the Center for Science in the Public Interest (CSPI) is among those strongly pushing back on Kennedy’s efforts to support unproven peptides. The group sent a letter to Acting Food and Drug Administration Commissioner Kyle Diamantas this week, urging him to disregard the panel’s recommendations. “If this conflicted committee’s recommendations stand, charlatans will continue to put unsuspecting consumers at risk of serious injury by making unsupported claims about their [peptides’] purported benefits,” Pete Lurie, CSPI president and a former associate commissioner at the FDA, said in a statement.

Legal experts are sounding alarms that Meta will soon update its commercial terms to require business accounts to allow Meta to train AI on their linked website content, not just their social media posts. Kicking in on October 30, the TOS update won’t allow businesses to opt out, instead suggesting that businesses stop using Meta services if they won’t agree to let Meta bots freely crawl their linked content. As Nieman Lab reported Thursday, this will affect all business accounts but is particularly shocking for news publishers who could perhaps charge Meta millions in licensing fees to continually access their articles for AI training. Currently news organizations are fighting a battle with OpenAI over whether such training without licensing is fair use, and Meta’s terms seemingly risk granting it sweeping permissions, no matter which way the court leans. “While journalism isn’t the only industry that will be affected by this update, virtually every news organization has at least one Meta business account, including Nieman Lab,” the lab said. You authorize us (or any entity acting on our behalf) to use automated software (such as web scrapers, bots, robots, spiders, crawlers, and user agents) and create test credentials to access, index, cache, analyze, collect, retrieve, or crawl the domains, web pages, or URLs (and any content available through them) that are associated with your products or services, or that are made available through your or users’ use of Meta Company Products and services, and to periodically pull updates from such domains, web pages, or URLs. Meta may use any content from such domains, web pages, or URLs to provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain the integrity of, the Meta Company Products, services, and features provided under these Commercial Terms and any other applicable supplemental terms. Notably, there is no mention of AI, but it’s inferred from a single use of the word “train” and references to automated scraping and crawling. Meta’s spokesperson told Nieman Lab that Meta has no plans to use the terms as a legal basis to ignore robots.txt instructions that block scraping on news sites. The company still allows news organizations to opt out of scraping and provides instructions on how to block crawling. A spokesperson told Ars that “there is no change to how Meta uses web crawlers; any suggestion to the contrary is false.” However, should courts affirm that AI training on non-news articles is fair use, news organizations question if Meta’s stance might change, and the terms could be used to freely grab access to content by legally overriding robots.txt instructions. Three legal experts told Nieman Lab that it’s not clear if a court would enforce the terms, but they could be “used to argue that Meta can scrape content from news sites and use that content to train the company’s AI products.” Particularly concerning, James Grimmelmann, a professor of digital and information law at Cornell Law School, told Nieman Lab that Meta could “train AI on any content from their sites” and possibly “deploy some of that content, even in competition with the publishers themselves” on Meta platforms. Additionally, Meta just began charging users to post links on its platforms, Nieman Lab noted. So, the terms seem to give Meta a path to first charge publishers for sharing links, then use that cash-for-links program to seize permission to scrape those links to train AI. Matt Topic, a media and intellectual property attorney at Loevy & Loevy, told Nieman Lab that “this is a remarkable provision to bury in [the] terms of service, given that this would give Meta access to material that would likely cost many millions of dollars in a licensing market.” Meta announced the update in a blog, where the company claimed it was simply “streamlining” its commercial terms. In a statement to Nieman Lab, a spokesperson said that “language around crawling was previously scattered across different terms of use, including terms about using generative AI in ads, and this update consolidates them in one place.” “Content owners can still manage how their content is accessed by using robots.txt,” the spokesperson said. However, Ars compared the current terms to updates, and what Meta calls “streamlining” seems to ignore that the new terms use fresh language and grant broader provisions. In Meta’s current commercial terms, there are no mentions of “test credentials” that, experts warned Nieman Lab, could allow Meta to bypass paywalls and automate scraping. “Test credentials” also are not mentioned in Meta’s Product Catalog Terms or Ad Creative Generative AI Terms, which are the other policies that are being combined with the commercial terms. For news groups parsing policies Meta discussed in its blog, this seemed like fresh language that Meta only introduced this month in Self-Service Ads Terms. Asked to clarify if “test credentials” were referred to in any prior policies, a Meta spokesperson pointed Ars to this Seller Agreement, which is not mentioned in Meta’s blog announcing the commercial terms update. That policy requires sellers who use Meta’s e-commerce products to allow Meta to “use automated software and create test credentials to retrieve information from domains, webpages or other URLs you provide,” so that Meta can optimize their listings and content. Just as the seller agreement limits permissions to a specific purpose, the self-service ad terms are limited to users who want to grant Meta permission to scrape their content in order to generate ads. In both cases, a policy of requiring “test credentials” to bypass paywalls perhaps seems less egregious when it’s tailored to a niche business purpose. Legal experts told Nieman Lab that Meta’s purported “streamlining” seems more like the antitrust concept of “tying,” where businesses force a buyer to purchase a second product as a condition of buying another. They likened it to Google’s prior practice of requiring websites to opt in to scraping for AI Overviews if they wanted their websites to be indexed for general search. (Google has since started allowing opt-outs.) If you provide URLs in connection with your Ad Content, you grant us and any entity acting on our behalf the right to access, index, cache, analyze, or crawl the domains, webpages, URLs and any content available through such URLs, and to periodically pull updates from such URLs, for your use in connection with your ads or for the purpose set forth in these Terms. In Meta’s blog, the company insisted that “these changes reduce overlap between our agreements and improve the clarity of the terms that apply to your use of our products.” Nieman Lab is now looking to talk to aggrieved news publishers about the terms to find out if the update might trigger a widespread scale-back of news organizations’ participation in Meta platforms. At least one Meta user—Stephen Beale, the founder and editor of The Steampunk Explorer—who depends on Facebook for traffic, is already considering quitting the platform over the update. He told Nieman Lab that he knows that his website has already been “scraped to death,” but the TOS update seemed like “such an arrogant, in-your-face move on” Meta’s part. “It just makes me angry, and I don’t want them to get away with it,” Beale said. Polymarket saw $600 million in election trades after the elections were over

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-16

Visit Original Website
Prediction market boosters often tout the platforms as barometers of public opinion, or even “truth machines” offering a clearer picture of where things are headed than polling or other data. In 2024, for example, Kalshi beat many pollsters in forecasting the outcome of the US presidential race. But their trading activity can also tell more perplexing stories. Two global election markets on Polymarket’s offshore platform, for instance, generated over $600 million in trading volume, far more than most political markets typically attract. Most of the activity took place buying and selling contracts tied to long-shot candidates who ultimately did not win. This summer, Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed’s Prosperity Party cruised to victory in federal elections, winning 438 parliamentary seats and paving the way for him to serve another five-year term. The closest competitor won 13 seats. On prediction markets, trading volume—the total value of contracts bought and sold—typically tracks roughly with the odds of a given outcome; frontrunners attract far more volume than long shots. But in this case, the trading patterns deviated sharply from the norm. Just $173,000 was wagered on Ahmed winning, compared to more than $41 million traded on his chief of staff, Shimelis Abdisa, becoming the country’s next leader. Altogether, the volume traded added up to over $285 million, nearly all on losing candidates. Even odder? “Nearly all of it—about $284 million—came from pairs of accounts repeatedly buying and selling with each other,” says former Polymarket trader Chris Park, who now runs the prediction-market forensic analysis firm MSR Decode. Such a high volume of seemingly pointless back-and-forth transactions has raised eyebrows among experts. “It’s obviously some form of volume manipulation,” says Rajiv Sethi, a Barnard College economics professor who researches prediction markets. When asked about these markets, Shana Bautista, Polymarket’s head of global intelligence and investigations, told WIRED that the team “immediately flagged this activity, investigated, and blocked the accounts from trading.” Polymarket did not respond to follow-up questions about why the accounts were able to trade such a high amount of volume before it took action. The timing of these trades made things even weirder. Polymarket’s rules stipulated that the Ethiopia market would be resolved in October after the prime minister was formally appointed and sworn in, which meant that it stayed open for several months after Ahmed’s victory was all but a foregone conclusion. For 123 days after the election, bursts of seemingly irrational trading continued, prompting Polymarket users to leave comments like “THIS NOT RESOLVED?” and “fuckkkk.” Sethi calls the phenomenon a “zombie market,” where people can buy and trade on events that have already taken place or are no longer happening. Another market about a 2024 election in Romania facilitated what Sethi described as “even more dramatic” trading anomalies. The initial election was called off due to fears about foreign interference. A new race was held several months later, but instead of closing the original market in the meantime, Polymarket kept it running alongside the new option. It was during this period that Sethi first noticed something weird appeared to be happening with the older market. An algorithm Sethi and his colleagues created to flag potential wash trading on Polymarket identified nearly all of the volume as suspicious. “99.6 percent of volume in this market for certain weeks has been wash trading,” Sethi says. Wash trading is a type of market manipulation in which accounts repeatedly buy and sell assets to create the illusion of heavy trading activity, when in reality, money is simply moving back and forth. It’s banned on regulated financial exchanges in the United States and is specifically prohibited in Polymarket’s terms of service. An earlier study conducted by Sethi and colleagues found evidence of widespread wash trading on Polymarket’s offshore platform. Wash trading could explain what happened with the Ethiopian and Romanian political markets, but because the identities of the traders is unknown, uncertainty remains. “What appears to be wash trading isn’t always wash trading,” says Sethi. MSR Decode ran an analysis of the Ethiopian market for WIRED, which found that the most likely explanation for the anomalous trading is “activity farming,” which is when people try to artificially inflate their trading volumes. The firm suspects that traders may have been interested in boosting their volume because they believed that they could benefit if Polymarket launched a cryptocurrency token. In 2025, Polymarket chief marketing officer Matthew Modabber told the hosts of a crypto-themed podcast that there “would be a token” but did not provide specifics. Sethi also finds it plausible that traders could be motivated by potential “token allocation” to higher-volume accounts. This type of behavior has been observed before in the cryptocurrency industry. “These are textbook examples on how market participants farm a potential airdrop,” says Andre Guettler, the director of Ulm University’s Institute of Strategic Management and Finance, who researches the structure of prediction markets. When MSR Decode analyzed the Ethiopian election market, it found that it was highly unlikely that the trading volume could be attributed to sophisticated traders using algorithms to eke out profits. It analyzed over 38,000 “trade loops” that took place after the election (instances where individual wallets bought and sold to each other in quick succession) and found that over 98 percent resulted in neither side losing or gaining money. The median time that the accounts held onto the contracts they purchased before selling them again was nine seconds. These types of unusual trading patterns are not a new phenomenon on Polymarket. Earlier this year, Barron’s reported that much of the activity on markets predicting the 2028 US presidential election centered on unlikely candidates such as Elon Musk and New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani, neither of whom is eligible to run because they were born outside of the country. Both Polymarket and its chief rival, Kalshi, have been publicly scrutinized for unusual market activity in recent months. Furor over suspicious activity on its recently launched perpetual futures market for cryptocurrency grew so intense on X that Kalshi published a blog post denying allegations of wash trading on it. (Sethi agrees with Kalshi that the allegations were unfounded. He argues that the unusual volume could instead be explained by Kalshi’s incentives for market makers, which create opportunities for other traders to profit from small price discrepancies.) Polymarket recently raised an over $1 billion round of funding led by 1789 Capital, the venture capital firm where Donald Trump Jr. is a partner. The company is now valued at an estimated $21 billion, and earlier this summer, it announced that its annualized revenue was now over $1 billion. Polymarket’s explosive growth is often measured in volume traded, since it’s a metric that can point to how active users are. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission, which regulates prediction markets in the United States, declined to comment on whether it is looking into these election trades. The CFTC previously investigated a number of suspected instances of market manipulation on Polymarket, including several exclusively reported by WIRED, but they usually involved potential insider trading rather than wash trading or other suspicious activity. In the past, the agency has issued hefty fines for wash trading in other sectors. “It seems to me that the CFTC would want to know about weird activity in a very large market,” a former CFTC employee tells WIRED. Microsoft isn’t the only one debuting an RTX-chipped laptop. It’s still early days, but it’s the kind of software/hardware incompatibility that you hate to run into when just trying to check out what your new computer can do. The Surface Laptop Ultra delivers on the gaming portion of its promises, too: AAA titles running on an ARM machine, complete with the latest bells and whistles, and displayed on one of the nicest laptop displays I’ve ever seen. From the screen to the keyboard to the trackpad, everything feels premium, because it is. The problem isn’t the laptop’s hardware, but everything currently sitting on top of it. Microsoft’s own AI Dev Gallery still feels half-baked, with a limited selection of small models mostly tied to the CPU. Phi Silica, the flagship LLM, is still gated behind developer registration. If you’re a CUDA developer who favors portability and needs to test local AI models on the fly, and you happen to have an extra $2,599–$5,899 in your back pocket, this is genuinely exciting hardware. For everyone else? I’d advise patience. Take a pause to see how the software matures before jumping ship from your MacBook Pro. There’s also an element of PC philosophy to consider. I’m the type of person who enjoys purpose-built systems for specific tasks. A desktop rig for gaming, a mini-PC for office work, a laptop or Chromebook for cloud applications on the road. It’s just how I’ve become wired. “This is my PC for that,” as it were. If you’re the type of person who really does want everything in one package, then the Surface Laptop Ultra’s multiple personalities may actually suit you. But the math is still somewhat complicated. You’re paying a premium for bleeding-edge tech where not all features are available yet, and having to do so under the umbrella of Microsoft services, with all the live logins and subscriptions that come with it. Has Microsoft and Nvidia’s partnership delivered the ultimate all-in-one PC? Not yet, but they have delivered the first ARM laptop I’ve used that makes the question worth asking—now the horse just needs to show up.

What makes a clot superficial—and when does it become dangerous? We asked doctors to explain. To understand blood clots, picture a road map. Your deep veins run within the muscles of your arms and legs, carrying most of the blood returning from your limbs to your heart. Superficial veins sit closer to the surface, just underneath the skin and in the tissue beneath it. Connecting veins bridge the two systems. “You have a really well interconnected network of veins,” says Dr. William Shutze, a vascular surgeon at Texas Vascular Associates in Dallas and vice president of the Society for Vascular Surgery (who was not involved in Hurts’ care). Think of it as “a very well developed road and highway system,” with multiple routes to get where you need to go. When a clot blocks one route, blood can take a detour through other veins. A superficial clot forms in one of the veins near the skin. The area can become red, warm, and tender. “It’s usually very uncomfortable,” Shutze says. In a major suit filed this week, a 26-year-old Texas woman using the pseudonym Maria Puerto is suing under H.B. 7, a law passed in Texas last year. H.B. 7 allows anyone (literally) to sue those who violate or intend to violate the statute’s prohibition on manufacturing, mailing, transporting, distributing, or providing abortion pills. Puerto argues that pro-life activists from Students for Life of America, one of the organizations that championed H.B. 7, are actually guilty of violating it. The lawsuit comes in the run-up to the 2026 midterm, where abortion has not played a particularly important role. Republicans continue to downplay abortion—with reason, given what most polls say about Americans’ attitudes—but Democrats are largely focusing on other issues too. That abortion doesn’t appear to be a central political issue is likely temporary. A number of major federal cases are looming in 2027, any of which could impose limits on abortion that Americans have never experienced since the reversal of Roe v. Wade. The most likely target is telehealth access to abortion. With lawsuits, Environmental Protection Agency complaints, and extradition requests, abortion opponents have taken a no-holds-barred approach to shutting down telehealth access. H.B. 7 is a central piece of that strategy. The law is a bounty-hunting bill on steroids: It allows anyone to sue those who manufacture, distribute, mail, or provide abortion pills in Texas, even if the defendant is based in a state where abortion is constitutionally protected. And it seems to allow for a lawsuit against anyone who even intended to violate the law, regardless of whether an abortion took place. The breadth of the law is key to its design: It is meant to incentivize a large group of potential plaintiffs to file suit,and maximize the chances that a plaintiff will come forward with the kind of story that conservatives want to tell about abortion in America. The post may also have been designed to gather evidence about the ease with which anyone—including abusers—can purchase abortion pills. It’s also possible that the post was just a post, and that no pills were ever ordered. Following up on an experiment launched last November to implement a confidential channel for LLM conversations with the help of NEAR AI's trusted execution environment (TEE), the browser maker has connected its Leo AI assistant to Z.ai's GLM 5.3 Flash under the protection of end-to-end encryption. More than 5 million people around the world with age-related macular degeneration (AMD) experience atrophy in their retinas. And until now, no treatment could restore their vision. The PRIMA system, which was approved in Europe in July and is awaiting FDA clearance in the US, changes that. It combines a retinal implant with camera-­equipped eyeglasses to improve sight in patients who develop a blind spot in the middle of their vision due to advanced AMD. After training sessions, more than 80% of recipients were able to read, do crosswords, and use their phones. Developed by the California-based Science Corporation, the system takes advantage of the fact that AMD doesn’t damage all retinal circuitry; only photoreceptors—the cells that convert light into an electrical signal—die off. PRIMA’s implant mimics those cells and transmits visual information directly to healthy eye circuitry and on to the brain. “We’re leveraging all of the body’s own natural functions to process and transmit the information,” says Frank Brodie, an ophthalmologist at the University of California, San Francisco, and Science Corp.'s chief medical officer for ophthalmology. Glasses outfitted with a miniature front-facing camera capture what someone is seeing in real time. The video feed is roughly five megapixels running at 30 frames per second, which is similar to TV. The raw video is transmitted to a connected processor via a fiber-optic cable. A small, handheld processor, which can be set on a table or tucked in a pocket, converts the video feed into infrared light signals that route back to the glasses. The device also includes physical buttons that let the wearer adjust contrast, brightness, and zoom. A laser projector on the glasses faces the wearer and shines pulses of near-infrared light directly at the eye at 880 nanometers—an invisible wavelength. This signal passes through the pupil and on to the retinal implant in the back of the eye. The implant is made up of 378 pixels, each with an electrode and two photovoltaic cells, which do double duty. They convert the light into a current that healthy bipolar and ganglion cells pass on to the brain. They also act as solar cells to power the chip, eliminating the need for a battery. Social media bans are stacking up. In July, France joined Australia in restricting minors from accessing social media—barring children under 15 from major platforms, before the ban was blocked by the country’s top court on human rights grounds. The UK aims to forbid kids under 16 from accessing Facebook, TikTok, and other platforms in early 2027, and the EU has announced its own plan to prohibit social media for kids under 15. But early lessons from Australia, which enacted a ban in 2024, show that these sorts of age restrictions are easily circumvented. A study published earlier this year found that four in five teens under 16 made their way onto barred platforms. Teens have successfully explored many tactics for bypassing bans, from using a sibling’s face for ID to drawing fake facial hair. Some proponents of bans have called for an increased focus on enforcement that blocks these workarounds. But circumvention is a signal, not a nuisance or something to be simply deterred. The fact that kids are actively looking for ways to outwit the bans highlights their limitations. Social media bans suppress kids’ access to information, including information that’s essential to their development, identity formation, and socialization, especially during adolescence. Worse, further restriction is likely to backfire. Closing loopholes in age gates could harm kids by pushing them toward less-moderated, higher-risk corners of the web. It also degrades privacy and security for everyone by forcing people to submit sensitive personal information to prove their age. In our work, we’ve found that parents and kids both want smarter, more nuanced policies that protect children, families, and their rights. That can take the form of mandating stronger limits on data collection by companies, or giving families flexible tools to control content and access on their own terms. Age gates won’t bring about the desired change, nor will they simplify parenting in the digital world. At best, they will only raise thornier questions and postpone the conversation to a child’s 16th birthday. At worst, they’ll leave kids with no guidance as they make their way through the online world. When young people circumvent restrictions, they are often doing what we want developing minds to do and what we celebrate in other contexts: lean into curiosity, problem-solve, and exercise independence. It’s important for kids to do this online, an arena where they often develop social skills and refine their identities. Bans sweep these motivations under the rug, taking a paternalistic approach that doesn’t consider young people’s needs or the realities of what they do online. It also ignores what some parents want. When we examined social media safety interventions last year, parents told us that they often join their kids in lies that help them gain access to needed but gated services. This isn’t a new phenomenon; research all the way back in 2011 showed that parents taught their children how to evade Facebook age restrictions. Some may say these parents are oblivious to the harms posed by the web or just don’t care. But parents are not a monolithic group. Many simply see offering their children responsible access to social media as a way to help them connect with like-minded peers, find informational content, and learn digital safety skills early on. Many parents believe the benefits outweigh the risks. When parents don’t help provide access and kids are not able to get into the platforms they desire, they will likely move toward alternative online spaces, often ones with fewer guardrails. Sometimes kids wind up on fairly innocuous, if unconventional, platforms, communicating with each other in Google Docs or podcast comment sections. Other times they move to more dangerous platforms that can leave them vulnerable to abuse and predation. In the aftermath of the Australian social media ban, preliminary research shows that kids’ screen time has not meaningfully decreased but has sometimes been redirected, with some young users going to spaces that are unmonitored and unknown to parents. Just as cities are sketchiest when they are not bustling, the most risky platforms for young people are those with the fewest eyes on the digital street. Outdated, dying, and under-resourced platforms tend to attract both vulnerable youth and those who prey on them. Age gates come with dangers of their own. Some platforms automatically track users’ online activities to confirm their age. In doing so, they profile users—collecting and analyzing their data to make inferences about them, which can increase the risk that data will be misused or leaked. Companies may also ask for personal documents like driver’s licenses to verify age, which can lead to mishandling of sensitive documents or even identity theft. Data breaches happen: Last year, for example, Discord reported that one of its third-party services, 5CA, which is used to confirm age, had been compromised, exposing at least 70,000 images of users’ government IDs. It can take little effort to find ways to use that kind of information for malicious purposes by, say, connecting driver’s licenses to specific accounts and private online activity, which could reveal anything from users’ political opinions to their sexual preferences. Efforts to make it harder for kids to get around age gates have also led policymakers to clamp down on kids’ access to VPNs. State age verification bills in Michigan and Utah have tried to interfere with the privacy protections offered by these tools (the Utah bill is now blocked by a federal court), and Australia’s eSafety Commissioner is nudging social media platforms to detect and block their use. But requiring adult users to prove their age to gain access to a VPN fundamentally defeats the purpose of that privacy-focused technology. It can put many users, particularly journalists and human rights defenders working on sensitive issues, in danger of government surveillance and even retaliation. Rather than attempting to block young people from online services that allow them to access information and engage in age-essential communication, policymakers should focus on the incentives that drive companies to engage in behavior that creates risks for kids in the first place. For example, platforms should decrease what experts call unhealthy engagement or addictive activity by limiting the collection and use of data that companies use to target content and features to all users, including children. Nearly all parents and teens we spoke with want policymakers to impose such restrictions, knowing full well that the companies’ use of data is at the core of features such as targeted advertisements. Both teens and parents are also frustrated by features designed to keep us all online for longer than we want. Another approach would be to expand the availability of user-controlled tools so that families have easy, flexible, and user-friendly ways to limit access to what is undesirable. For example, keyword-based content filtering can enable a student who is targeted with a specific nickname or inside joke to block any post or comment containing it. Platforms are unlikely to know which word or term a child might want to block, nor can policy seek to address such dynamic types of harm. Policy can, however, offer tools that would put power back in users’ hands. Poor privacy practices, anxiety-producing content streams, and a near-inescapable need to use  digital technologies for everyday tasks, communication, and information-seeking have rightfully led parents and teens to want real change in how companies conduct business. Circumvention shows where policy falls short. Stricter enforcement will mean yet more data collection, and it won’t meet kids and parents where they are.
