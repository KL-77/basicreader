Quantum computers outperform classical ones, with results you can trust

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-07-30

Visit Original Website
There are many algorithms for which it has been mathematically proven that a quantum computer can generate results that would take a classical computer an unreasonable amount of time to generate. Unfortunately, today’s quantum computers either can’t run those algorithms or can only run simplified versions that classical computers can also handle. This has left the field facing a challenging question: Can we demonstrate the promise of quantum computers on today’s noisy, limited hardware? That’s a more difficult question than it may first appear. If you generate a result that’s out of reach of today’s regular computers, it may not be possible to verify that you got the right result. And given that today’s quantum computers are somewhat error-prone, getting the wrong result is a distinct possibility. Further, in the absence of a mathematical proof of the capabilities of quantum hardware, it’s possible that a better classical algorithm could outperform the quantum hardware. These issues inspired IBM to launch a quantum advantage tracker. On Thursday, the company announced three new entries that it says clearly show a quantum advantage, each using a different approach to overcoming errors and validating quantum results. “Trusted computing when you can do classical simulations is irrelevant,” IBM’s Jay Gambetta told Ars. “Trusted computing when you can’t do classical simulations is a big deal.” None of the results are immediately useful, but they hint that we might be heading in the right direction. At this point, there have been many claims of quantum advantage, and at least one has the potential to be useful. But in a number of high-profile cases, algorithm developers have developed optimized algorithms that have severely reduced the advantage, bringing classical computers back up to par. Another issue is verification. If your quantum computer is generating a statistical pattern by re-running variations on a single set of operations, a systemic error could potentially bias the output, and you couldn’t use a classical computer to check. These issues are typically handled by performing simplified calculations using fewer qubits and verifying the results on classical hardware. If that works, it’s assumed that the algorithm will continue to work when it is run with more qubits. But that’s not the only option. Some algorithms could produce results that are difficult to calculate but easy to verify—for example, factoring the product of multiplying two large primes. Unfortunately, if anyone has identified a calculation that could be run on today’s hardware, I’m not aware of it. Computer scientists, therefore, have had to get creative. And really, that’s what today’s announcement is about: three creative ways to handle the fact that today’s processors are error-prone. One of the new efforts was a collaboration among IBM, RIKEN in Japan, and a small company called Qedma, which develops software that helps mitigate errors in current quantum processors. The work focuses on modeling a Floquet process, in which a system oscillates while subjected to an external force that gradually alters its behavior. Think of a pendulum that gradually slows down due to friction. These sorts of processes can also occur in quantum systems, and the Qedma team modeled something called an Ising model, which you can think of as a hypothetical two-dimensional grid of magnets, where the orientation of each can affect its neighbors. After setup, the orientations will gradually undergo periodic flips as they try to find a low-energy configuration in which neighboring magnets have opposite orientations. The complexity of modeling the intervening states of the system during these flips increases as you add more magnets. So, Qedma chose to model something that wasn’t too large to fit into existing quantum hardware, then worked with RIKEN to run two different classical algorithms on the Fugaku supercomputer, which was the world’s most powerful computer five years ago. The classical algorithms clearly showed that something was wrong, as they produced answers that diverged over time (one showing net magnetism decreasing smoothly, the other showing it increasing). When run on an IBM quantum processor with Qedma’s error-mitigation software, the algorithm showed something else entirely: a gradual decrease in magnetism, with periodic oscillations. One classical algorithm got the oscillations but showed magnetism increasing, while the other showed it decreasing much faster without the oscillations. To demonstrate that this wasn’t the product of some consistent error in the IBM hardware, the team turned to a Quantinuum processor and confirmed its output. They also identified a problem in at least one of the quantum algorithms: to boost performance, it truncates some terms needed to produce the oscillatory behavior. Another new manuscript comes from a collaboration between IBM and researchers at the University of Chicago. It’s similar in principle to one of the algorithms mentioned above: It repeats variations on an algorithm multiple times to get a sample of results that give a sense of the statistics of different outputs. Because the algorithm is run on a quantum computer, different outcomes can interfere with each other, making it challenging to simulate on classical hardware. It’s possible to run it a few times on classical machines, but not enough to get decent statistics on the output. In this case, the team made a couple of key variations. The first is that it mostly performed what are called Clifford gates, which are relatively easy to simulate on classical hardware. But it sprinkled in a few non-Clifford gates (specifically T gates) of a specific type chosen in part because they are less prone to error. “Z rotations (including T gates) are special in our hardware: they are implemented by virtual frame tracking and do not add extra noise,” the paper said. But the T gates also help ensure that this is especially hard to simulate on a classical computer. “It’s got a stronger complexity argument because of the [T gates], which you can prove on average is exponentially hard to sample for a classical computer,” Gambetta told Ars. That will make it very challenging for classical algorithms to catch up. The work also linked in a few additional qubits around the periphery of those used for the algorithm, arranged so that gentle measurements performed during the operations could detect whether errors had occurred; if they were detected, the results were discarded. (Note that this would also throw out valid results that were flagged by an erroneous read of these additional qubits.) The result was an algorithm that “combines the broad output statistics of hard sampling problems with circuit structure that can be exploited for error detection and fidelity certification,” the team said. The last of the new results comes from quantum software developer Algorithmiq, which used an algorithm similar to the one in Google’s “quantum echoes” work. A set of gates first alters a quantum system, after which the process is reversed. Additional operations performed during the reversal prevent the system from returning to its original state—instead, the noise creates an imperfect “echo” of the forward process. As with one of the earlier works, attempts to simulate this on classical hardware require some simplification, and different simplification methods yield different results. By its nature, then, the problem is beyond the reach of classical hardware. The people behind the manuscript set to work removing all the noise from the system. They started by choosing a specific area of the quantum processor with the lowest noise and used neighboring qubits to determine whether any were inappropriately altered during the computation, indicating that something was going wrong. They also optimized the control signals sent to the qubits, along with other techniques that should suppress noise. They then intentionally injected noise, determined how it altered their results, and provided an estimate of the amount of noise present without it. They also repeated the entire process on a separate IBM processor, which should exhibit a different noise pattern. All of this allowed them to set an error rate on their calculation—there may be some noise left, but they know it must be below a value they can calculate. There are many ways you could view this work. Cynically, it’s definitely the case that none of these algorithms are useful in any real sense. Even those that model the behavior of quantum systems use incredibly simplified models that don’t reflect any real-world systems. It’s also worth noting that some past claims of quantum advantage haven’t held up, as researchers have improved on classical algorithms, something that may yet happen here. Finally, these sorts of algorithms—performing computations directly on hardware qubits with problematic noise rates—will fade in a few years as we start to get reasonable numbers of error-corrected qubits. In fact, a number of companies have indicated that they’re holding off on offering any publicly accessible hardware until we get there. The less cynical take is that the hardware is out there, and why wouldn’t computer scientists be interested in discovering its limits and pushing the systems as far as they can? While the algorithms themselves aren’t useful, the techniques developed for them—minimizing noise, understanding its nature, and identifying actions that make it worse—could still be useful for improving performance on hardware capable of error correction. And if we’re not developing algorithms for noisy machines, we’re unlikely to find out if there are any useful ones out there. Still, it’s probably best to view the results as part of a natural progression. The first wave of claims of quantum advantage came when researchers were simply excited to have hardware with enough qubits to do something that was difficult to do classically. These new efforts are part of a separate wave, in which people are now making an effort to rigorously ensure that the results are also high quality. The next step will be to explore ways to build on these newer efforts to find algorithms with real-world implications. “So you’ve got two extremes,” Gambetta said. “You’ve got the previous couple of results where they were just comparing real material to quantum computers, but in a region where classical methods still work. Now, you’ve got toy models in a regime where classical methods don’t work, but I can guarantee that I can trust the quantum one.” Taking the next step is what the field is most excited about. “The holy grail is to compare a real material or a real experiment to a quantum computer,” Gambetta said. You can find links to the three arXiv papers at IBM’s blog post about the results. These and other attempts to outperform classical systems are hosted on its advantage tracker site.

This week, the Model Context Protocol (MCP), an open source standard for how AI systems interact with external tools and data sources, saw its largest update since its introduction. Most notably, MCP’s protocol core is now stateless, so requests are no longer dependent on a session tied to an individual server instance. This change has the potential to address long-standing barriers to scalability. The blog post announcing the specification, written by lead maintainers David Soria Parra and Den Delimarsky (who both work at Anthropic), says: The highlight of this release is a stateless protocol core—MCP is transforming from a bidirectional stateful protocol into a request/response stateless protocol. It was one of the most highly-requested features from developers who were eager to get better reliability and scalability for their MCP servers. This update is “MCP’s most important since remote MCP first launched over a year ago,” Soria Parra also wrote. In addition, the update adds “Multi Round-Trip Requests, header-based routing, cacheable list results, authorization hardening, a formal extensions framework, and updated Tier 1 SDKs.” The hope is that the changes, detailed in the protocol’s documentation, will make widespread deployment in an enterprise context much easier. MCP started as something that simply ran on a local machine, connecting models to local apps, so the new spec is a major rethink of some of its foundations. There is also a new deprecation policy that ensures at least 12 months between when a feature’s formal deprecation is enacted and when the feature may actually be removed—with a narrow exception for critical security updates. This is again in keeping with the general “let’s make this work better at enterprise scale” theme of the new specification. MCP is managed by the Agentic AI Foundation (AAIF), which sits under the Linux Foundation. MCP was originally introduced by Anthropic, just shy of two years ago, and Anthropic still has significant influence over its direction. Nonetheless, it has grown beyond just Anthropic—OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, and Amazon also contribute to it—and it’s supported by many developer tools and, increasingly, a wide range of software and services used for other knowledge and creative work. The buck technically stops with individual maintainers, not any of these companies themselves, but as noted above, some of the principal maintainers currently work at Anthropic.

Mythos attack on 3rd-round PQC algorithm candidate puts it out of commission

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-07-29

Visit Original Website
A quantum-resistant cryptography algorithm that was under consideration as an official US standard has been taken out of the running after an Anthropic security model helped find a flaw that rendered it broken. The algorithm is known as HAWK. It’s a digital signature scheme designed to withstand future attacks from quantum computers. HAWK had survived two rounds of testing by NIST (the National Institute of Standards and Technology) for evaluating the security of PQC (post-quantum cryptographic) algorithms through widespread testing. HAWK was in a third round of testing designed to catch precisely the kinds of flaws Mythos helped uncover. Following Anthropic’s Monday announcement of the results, the developer of HAWK said Tuesday he was withdrawing it. Even before the development, Anthropic was hailing the results of the two cryptographic problems it threw at its Mythos AI security model. The model found weaknesses in the mathematical problems underpinning HAWK and, separately, the widely used AES cipher. Despite the withdrawing of HAWK, it’s hard to know how much of the company’s reporting is marketing hype, but the findings are still worth paying attention to because they could signal important advances in breaking cryptography that’s crucial to privacy and security. Before digging into the results, a few caveats. First, the outcomes are incremental. They don’t break any of the cryptosystems anyone relies on today. Instead, they reveal methods for moderately reducing the work that would be required to defeat the systems. Second, the cryptosystems tested were weakened versions of the ones defined in their formal specifications. Such “challenge instances” are provided by the specification authors for use in adversarial peer review. It’s standard to use the weakened versions in testing, but the real ones are considerably more robust in production settings. Third, even with the improvement, the underlying “primitives”—meaning the underlying mathematical problems that form the basic building blocks of cryptosystems—remain safe, at least for now. Lastly, both of the attacks use methods that would likely be infeasible outside of testing environments. Anthropic said its Mythos model—which currently remains available only to a select group of trusted users—was able to advance attacks against two cryptosystems. The first system is HAWK, a digital signature scheme designed to withstand future attacks from quantum computers. With about 60 hours of work and about $100,000 of compute cost, an Anthropic researcher with no expertise in cryptography prompted Mythos to improve the best-known existing attack on the algorithm that effectively cut its key strength in half. The math HAWK’s security relies on is the hardness of the Lattice Isomorphism Problem, which, unlike today’s most-used digital signature schemes, is believed to be safe from quantum computing attacks. The best-known classical computing attack to solve this problem works by finding what are known as automorphism symmetries. Mythos outputted a previously unknown method for finding such symmetries. The result cut the key length in half. The weakness can be mitigated by doubling the key size, but the added computation makes HAWK less desirable than available PQC signing algorithms. In academics cryptographic algorithms are considered broken when weaknesses allow an adversary to derive a key faster than is possible using a brute-force attack. Matthew Green, a Johns Hopkins professor and expert in cryptography, said the impressive thing about the discovery was its reliance on several existing methods that no one previously thought to put together. “What’s particularly concerning (and so especially ripe for AI) is that the attack does not invent fundamentally new mathematics,” Green wrote. “It simply extends a bunch of tools that were lying around and well-known, and gets a good result.” To find the attack, Claude Mythos Preview worked semi-autonomously in an agentic harness, with occasional human guidance and nontechnical direction. Mythos found the attack after an extensive literature review to understand the state of the art, and substantial mathematical reasoning and computational experiments. After finding the attack, Mythos implemented an end-to-end verification pipeline to convince itself—and the human operator—of the attack’s correctness. To find the improved method, Mythos deployed two separate agents that worked largely independently. One initially rejected the method as unworkable. The second found a way to make it work. The agents eventually worked in unison until they produced an agreement that the improved attack was effective. (As with the description of the attack against AES later in this article, the HAWK attack methodology has been simplified. For the full details, see the Anthropic post or two longer papers on the HAWK and AES attacks, respectively.) Sophie Schmieg, an expert in PQC at Google, said HAWK was already suspected to have weaknesses that would eventually be found. Still, the method for halving the key strength found through Mythos made the candidate algorithm less competitive than existing PQC digital signature schemes such as ML-DSA and FN-DSA. “Basically with this paper, HAWK is dead,” she wrote. The attack against AES produced less dramatic results. It’s based on an improvement found through Mythos for performing a “meet-in-the-middle” attack, which is used to derive a key under a chosen plaintext threat model, the best-known existing attack against AES. The technique inputs large numbers of known plaintext into the crypto system and analyzes the encrypted output for clues that, with enough inputs, eventually reveal an unknown key. Previously, the best-known meet-in-the-middle attack against AES required roughly 2105 plaintext inputs, a number large enough to make the method infeasible. Mythos helped to find a new meet-in-the-middle technique that relies on a Möbius Bridge, a more sophisticated fingerprinting algorithm used in meet-in-the-middle attacks. Using it, Green said, the code Mythos produced was able to reduce the number of required inputs to 289. Anthropic said that savings can reduce the time required for such attacks by 200- to 800-fold. The ability to produce that many inputs makes the attack beyond reach outside of the laboratory. Further, the actual speed-up is unknown, since the weakened AES algorithm tested used only seven rounds. Specification-compliant AES, Green said, uses 10, 12, or 14 rounds, depending on key size. Anthropic is careful to explicitly spell out most of these caveats. The Monday blog post goes on to argue, however, that the results are nonetheless meaningful and could ultimately fundamentally disrupt the process of cryptanalysis, or the adversarial testing of cryptosystems. “The cybersecurity community is now grappling with the fact that language models are able to discover so many bugs that the standard human processes (like vulnerability triage, verification, and remediation) struggle to keep up,” Anthropic wrote. “We predict that the same will soon be true in academic cryptography research. As language models increasingly produce novel research outputs autonomously, human researchers may become bottlenecked on studying and validating these results for technical validity, novelty, and utility.” Not mentioned in Anthropic’s report is whether its researchers used Mythos to attack more tested cryptosystems, such as elliptic curve cryptography and RSA. Attack improvements against these systems would be more impressive. By achieving the most impressive result against an algorithm still in its infancy, it’s not clear how much of an advantage Mythos truly provided. There’s no way of knowing if researchers using conventional cryptanalysis techniques were already close to discovering the same attack. Ultimately, the lesson from the research is simple. AI-assisted cryptanalysis remains untested, and providers of these platforms have a vested interest in exaggerating their benefits. At the same time, there’s growing evidence that LLMs may provide significant advantages in finding cryptographic weaknesses. It would be a mistake to conclude that LLMs won’t one day play an important role in the race between securing and compromising our most vital assets. Elon Musk has settled his lawsuit with advertisers that he once claimed should be criminally prosecuted for refusing to advertise on his social media platform, X. In a joint statement that reveals little about the deal, the World Federation of Advertisers (WFA) and X said that they were “putting the litigation involving the Global Alliance for Responsible Media (GARM) behind them.” X sued the WFA in 2024, shortly after a series of brands boycotting X caused the platform’s revenue to drop by $1.5 billion by the end of 2023. Musk became upset, declaring “it is war” on X about eight months after he told advertisers who refused to buy X ads to “go fuck yourself.” According to Musk, he had “no choice but to file suit against the perpetrators and collaborators in the advertising boycott racket,” who he tried to argue were violating antitrust laws by illegally conspiring to tank X revenue. A lot has changed since Musk filed the lawsuit. Most relevant to the lawsuit, GARM was quickly disbanded after Musk called out the small not-for-profit initiative for setting brand standards that X said led advertisers to steer clear of X. The point of the initiative was “to help the industry address the challenge of illegal or harmful content on digital media platforms and its monetization via advertising,” a now-shuttered website said. But Musk claimed GARM was instead trying to monopolize which content gets monetized online, and the effort’s dissolution will remain his biggest win in the case. The joint statement confirmed that GARM will remain inactive as a result of the WFA’s settlement with X. No other concessions were discussed. The rest of the statement claims that the settlement “resets the relationship between the two organizations,” which are now supposedly “fully aligned in the view that brands, platforms, and consumers will all benefit from brand-safety innovation.” Additionally, the WFA said it is aligned with X on “its commitment to freedom of speech,” which seems to be a nod to the controversial reports on X content that Musk said triggered his “war” with advertisers. Those reports found that as hateful content was increasing on the platform, X ad controls weren’t working to stop big brands’ ads from appearing next to posts touting Hitler and the Nazi Party. It’s unclear why advertisers settled, since a court ruled in March that the ad boycott was perfectly legal and X’s antitrust claims fell apart without proof of consumer harm. The next month, X appealed, but it appeared to be dragging its feet in filing a brief as an appellant, asking the court for an extension until August. Perhaps settlement negotiations were already underway. For Musk, the settlement comes shortly after the launch of X Money, a payments product offered through his social media platform that he hopes will help X be less reliant on advertisers for revenue. Whether X Money can succeed at the scale required to meaningfully rival X’s advertising business will likely depend on X removing barriers to adoption. Those include the possibility that X Money transactions could be declined if an automated support error mistakenly suspends a user’s X account without warning. There’s also the simpler problem that X Money isn’t available in all 50 states.

Who wins and who loses after US bans foreign robots?

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-07-29

Visit Original Website
Humanoid robots and four-legged robots made in China fall squarely under a new ban imposed by the US Federal Communications Commission on foreign-made robots. But the sweeping prohibition covers a wide variety of robots manufactured anywhere abroad, including the newest robot vacuum cleaners. The Trump administration’s FCC justified the ban on foreign-made robots by referencing cybersecurity vulnerabilities previously discovered in robots specifically made by Chinese companies. On July 28, the federal agency added “foreign-produced advanced robotic devices” to its Covered List of technologies “deemed to pose an unacceptable risk to the national security of the US or the safety and security of US persons.” However, the FCC cannot update the Covered List on its own initiative and is required by law to follow the direction of a “qualifying national security authority.” The agency describes the White House as having “convened an executive branch interagency body” that “included appropriate national security agencies,” which determined foreign-made robots as presenting a national security risk. So what foreign-made robots are prohibited? The ban defines “advanced robotic devices” as being “a mechanical mobile device, including autonomous mobile robots, humanoid robots, and quadrupeds” that can move around on the ground and operate either autonomously on their own or remotely under the control of a human. The definition also describes covered robots as having sensors capable of perceiving the robot’s environment, network connectivity such as Bluetooth or satellite connections with speeds surpassing 200 kbps in either direction, and software or AI models running onboard the robot or remotely from cloud computing servers. Such an import ban would apply to some of the most affordable robots primarily produced by Chinese companies, including Unitree’s humanoid robots that are used by robotics labs and researchers for tasks such as experimental robot surgeries. US consumers would also likely lose access to the newest robot vacuum cleaners that are mainly manufactured by Chinese companies such as Roborock. The ban also broadly applies to foreign-made robots produced by countries nominally allied to the United States, including Japan, South Korea, and Germany. But China’s Ministry of Commerce described the “discriminatory and suppressive” ban as mainly taking aim at Chinese robot manufacturers, according to an official statement covered by the New York Times. The ministry also criticized the FCC restriction as undermining China-US relations and disrupting global supply chains, while warning of possible retaliation from China. There are a number of exemptions. For example, the new ban defines covered robots as being any mobile device—potentially in combination with a docking station—weighing more than 4.4 pounds. That would permit US consumers to continue buying small robots, such as robotic toys, although most robot vacuums weigh more. The FCC ban also only covers new models of foreign-made robots. That means US consumers and businesses can continue purchasing existing robot models previously authorized by the FCC and continue to use previously purchased robots, but are blocked from purchasing newer, unauthorized models that come out from this point onward. The agency made clear that its foreign-made robot ban does not apply to trains, drones, underwater vehicles, and “connected vehicles” that drive on roads, such as robotaxis. The FCC already enacted an import ban on foreign-made drone models in December 2025. Similarly, it does not cover anything classified as devices by the Food and Drug Administration, including medical or surgical robots, external limb prostheses, and mobility assistive devices, such as wheelchairs. The FCC ban also specifically exempts fixed or stationary robots. That means US businesses can continue purchasing and using foreign-made industrial robot arms and other specialized, non-mobile robots. Notably, the US Department of Defense can determine that a foreign-made robot or class of such robots does not pose security risks—thereby exempting such robots from the FCC’s Covered List. The Department of Defense has been rebranded the Department of War under the Trump administration and is referenced as such in the FCC documents. The ban on foreign-made robots could theoretically encourage more US and foreign companies to set up manufacturing facilities in the United States. There are already multiple companies racing to scale up production of humanoid robots in US factories, including Agility Robotics, 1X Technologies, and Figure AI. Tesla has been attempting to shift production away from older electric vehicle models and toward its Optimus humanoid robot. Boston Dynamics has already been making its Atlas humanoid robot, along with its four-legged Spot robot and wheeled Stretch robot, at its main facility in Waltham, Massachusetts. The US robotics company is also planning to massively scale up manufacturing of the Atlas robot under South Korea’s Hyundai Motor Company, which gained full ownership of Boston Dynamics in July 2026. “This is one of the strongest technology-security actions in modern US history,” wrote Evan Beard, CEO of Standard Bots, in a LinkedIn post. “The message is unambiguous: robotics is a technology America must lead and own—and foreign-subsidized robots will not be allowed to unfairly dominate US robotics as they did solar.” Similar praise came from Rush Doshi, director of the Initiative on China Strategy at the Council on Foreign Relations, who, in a social media post, described the FCC decision as “one of the most significant actions taken so far in support of the US robotics ecosystem.” However, several robotics researchers and analysts interviewed by The Robot Report expressed skepticism about any potential boost to US competitiveness in robotics. Some even warned that the ban could prove counterproductive for US robotics efforts to develop humanoid robots. “In the near term, the measure could slow US physical AI innovation by cutting startups and researchers off from future low-cost Chinese platforms before comparable Western alternatives exist,” said Georg Stieler, a global robotics advisor and managing director for Asia at Stieler Technology & Market Advisory, in an interview with The Robot Report. US domestic production of robots lags behind China in terms of mass manufacturing at lower cost, said Rueben Scriven, a senior analyst at Interact Analysis. “This announcement is more likely to inhibit the US humanoid robotics industry, as the presence of low-cost Chinese humanoid robots has been helping educate the US market through promotional and entertainment use cases—an effect this policy risks undermining,” Scriven told The Robot Report. The outcome of previous FCC bans is also not necessarily encouraging. US government restrictions on drones made by Chinese companies such as DJI have failed to create a “competitive mass-market consumer-drone producer” based in the United States, Stieler pointed out. The Verge has also reported that newly created companies have popped up to sidestep the US ban on foreign drones by selling “barely disguised versions of DJI technology.” This story was updated on July 30, 2026 to include China’s Ministry of Commerce response to the FCC decision.

A lawsuit against Comcast’s cable division alleges that a store manager humiliated the lowest performing salesperson each month with a strange ritual in which a cream pie was smashed into the employee’s face. The lawsuit claims the store manager took videos of the assaults and ordered employees to participate in the assaults and record their own videos. A Comcast store in Plainville, Connecticut, “had a policy that the highest-ranked Retail Sales Consultant for the prior month was instructed by his or her supervisor—Ms. Peterson, the Comcast Store manager—to tie the lowest-ranked sales consultant for the prior month to a chair in the back office and thereafter assault that person by violently smashing a cream pie in their face,” the complaint alleged. Plaintiff David Figueroa’s lawsuit said he was hired as a retail sales consultant on February 2, 2026, and was supervised by store manager Sully Fuentes Peterson. Figueroa alleges that Peterson “designed and implemented” the pie-in-face ritual to meet goals related to sales and positive responses in customer surveys. “Defendant did not inform the Plaintiff prior to his acceptance of Defendant’s offer of employment that the Comcast Store has a policy of subjecting Retail Sales Consultants to public assaults by co-workers—at the direction of Ms. Peterson, the store manager—for the purpose of increasing Defendant’s sales and profitability,” the lawsuit said. Figueroa resigned on February 27, and he alleges it was a constructive discharge. The lawsuit says the defendant, Comcast, was negligent because it “reasonably should have known” about the store management’s policies and that the policies could harm employees. Comcast “failed to properly supervise the Comcast Store’s management team,” allowing store management to humiliate employees “for the purpose of promoting the Defendant’s revenues and profits,” the lawsuit alleged. Comcast is likely to dispute the negligence claim and may dispute at least some of the factual allegations. Comcast provided a statement to Ars today in which it said it disagrees with the complaint, but did not provide any details. Comcast also did not say whether it still employs the manager. “The Company has zero tolerance for harassment, humiliation, or any behavior that compromises a respectful and safe workplace,” Comcast said in the statement provided to Ars. “This matter is in litigation so we will not comment on the specific allegations, other than to say that we disagree with the claims in the complaint and its characterization of the alleged events, and intend to fully respond through the legal process.” Figueroa is seeking compensatory damages for “past and future economic losses” and for emotional distress. The complaint includes a photo of what is said to be the chart, which includes people’s first names followed by a month and a “pie face” emoji. “An objective, reasonable person in Plaintiff’s position would have felt compelled to resign his employment as a result of this work atmosphere that Defendant—through its employees, servants, agents—created,” the lawsuit said. “As a result of the pervasive, repeated, and ongoing threats of violence—and actual violence inflicted on co-workers not meeting sales goals—Plaintiff resigned his position with Defendant, resulting in his constructive discharge from Defendant’s employment.” As the case moves forward, the court could be shown video evidence of alleged pie assaults. The first animal narrator to make an impression on me was an amiable mutt named Harold, who, with remarkable lucidity, recounts the peculiar events of James and Deborah Howes’s 1979 novel, Bunnicula: A Rabbit-Tale of Mystery. The book, written for and beloved by children, boasts a winning premise by any metric: A fanged rabbit’s benign vampiric qualities inspire a series of animal hijinks that endanger Bunnicula and propel Harold to acts of interspecies mercy. Interrogations into rabbit vampirism aside, Bunnicula compelled me most because its narrator is a dog—one who, despite his aptitude for writing, cannot otherwise communicate with people. This textual sleight of hand ignited my fascination with a narrator’s uncanny power to describe reality as they experience it. Its impossibility (if only dogs could write books!) is what made this literary conceit so captivating—the kind of imaginative leap that most fiction makes in one way or another, although it is not always so conspicuous or playful. Every novel requires some suspension of disbelief, but a book narrated by an animal—a surprisingly common subgenre—raises unique questions about representation. We can safely assume that a human author is more qualified to render the emotional landscape of a fellow human than they are of a dog or a horse or a mountain lion. Yet what the best of these novels lose in verisimilitude, they gain in creative vision. Anna Sewell’s 1877 novel, Black Beauty, prevails as one of the standard-bearers; it adopts the first-person perspective of the titular horse to encourage more humane treatment of his species. Virginia Woolf’s motivations were more recreational when she published Flush: A Biography in 1933. Woolf, who desired a palate cleanser after finishing her experimental novel The Waves, focused her energies on the life story of the poet Elizabeth Barrett Browning’s cocker spaniel. Richard Adam’s 1972 novel, Watership Down, imagines an anthropomorphized society of rabbits with their own species-centered cosmology, who must brave the predatory wilds to make a new home. Most creature-oriented stories—no matter how comedic or frolicsome—will gesture, either explicitly or subtly, at the hazards inherent to animal lives. (Flush, for instance, was briefly kidnapped and returned for a ransom.) But in recent years, writers and filmmakers have magnified the peril that their nonhuman subjects navigate, laying bare the human-made disasters that now jeopardize their lives. The Academy Award–winning 2024 film Flow dramatizes the struggles of a tenacious black cat in a flooded world where all human life has vanished; the Polish movie EO tells the story of a donkey subject to agonizing abuses and fleeting joys, and culminates in a slaughterhouse scene that indicts factory farming. Among recent animal-narrated novels, the best may be Open Throat. Henry Hoke’s savage 2023 masterpiece is narrated by a queer, ravenous mountain lion who lives in the arid underbrush beneath Los Angeles’s Hollywood sign, near an unhoused encampment that he dutifully protects. Midway through the novel, a malicious hiker sets fire to the camp, forcing the mountain lion to seek refuge in the urban sprawl he refers to as “ellay.” Etna, Paul Yoon’s third novel, is the latest addition to the modern animal-in-peril narrative: the story of a military dog named Etna who is snatched up and discarded in the course of a human war. Like Open Throat, Etna excels as a keenly wrought hypothesis about not only how its chosen creature would perceive the world and its dangers, but also how those perceptions can be adapted to fiction. Although Etna tempers the exacting moral judgments at the core of Open Throat with a plot that bends toward empathy, Yoon’s critiques of humanity are just as pointed as Hoke’s, and they emerge from a similarly textured philosophy that acknowledges the fraught business of living, whether one walks on two feet or four. Like all weary, uprooted creatures, Etna longs for home, but unlike so many of the humans who commandeer his world, he possesses far less agency when it comes to finding his way back there. And yet there are compensations. Etna may lack the privilege of human sovereignty and the benefits of advanced intelligence, but he is free of the hubristic delusions that underwrite so much human-produced wreckage. As he observes to a fellow dog, “There’s no other place but here.” Read: Netflix captures the doom and dream of Watership Down In both Open Throat and Etna, brevity is central to characterizing animal consciousness. (Neither novel reaches 200 pages.) Both Yoon and Hoke also structure their works with staccato-like page breaks—a decision that cleverly evokes spans of creaturely silence. Etna is a sociable fellow, but his conversations with other animals never indulge in pleasantries. Nonhuman discourse is laconic yet earnest—and, as his narration explains, telepathic: Their common language takes the form of “dark shapes” that materialize “in a space between our eyes, shapes we could translate.” To struggle with silence is a uniquely human condition; the animal kingdom abides in it, until there is reason to sound out. For all its compactness, Etna has the shape of an epic. The story draws explicit comparisons to Homer’s The Odyssey: Its titular character tries to return to the farm where he was born after surviving a ravaging war. (The book also features a minor Greek character named Achilles.) An animal story set in motion by the basest human impulses—though also distinguished by some of our best—the novel is tied deeply into both ancient literary tradition and tragically perennial concerns. Etna prompts readers to imagine an experience of the world unbound from human social convention, but it is no less interested in the slippery distinctions between human and nonhuman desires for belonging, intimacy, and home. Yoon drew extensively on these themes in his earlier works—most recently in the novel Run Me to Earth and the Story Prize–winning collection The Hive and the Honey—and his oeuvre depicts war’s psychological and material calamities. He understands that one of the most violent disruptions a living being can sustain is sudden estrangement from their environment. And yet, in Yoon’s hands, a character’s suffering never overwhelms their capacity for tenderness. The author always insists on the enduring humanity of his displaced subject—even when they aren’t human. Nevertheless, Etna stands out among Yoon’s work; it is, first of all, a dog’s tale. How does Etna—who keeps close company with humans but is by definition marginalized by them—hold on to himself in the face of rank, incoherent brutalities? Perhaps he must become a storyteller. “I was born near water,” he begins, in the simple declarative assertion so common to famous literary beginnings. And then, also like his bipedal narrative forebears (David Copperfield, for instance), he quickly proceeds toward reflective ambivalence: “I would never learn what the farm was intending to do with me. Maybe nothing at all. Maybe I was supposed to grow old there.” Like any living creature, Etna can only speculate about the other shapes that his life might have taken. But Yoon is careful, with his characteristically spare, visceral imagery, to establish Etna’s early happiness. “Every day there was sand floating in daylight and the salt on our tongues. The sea wind over grass,” the dog says, in a manner both frank and wistful. He recalls his mother, steady as a planet, “her face looming over us in the sky, the tall triangles of her ears pocketing the smell of the coast.” Yoon’s fiction focuses primarily on Asian communities in Korea and Laos, and across the diaspora. Etna is set in a small, unnamed country, which makes sense; nationalism and border politics are alien to dogs. Invading forces threaten the country when Etna is still a puppy, and the looming war marks the abrupt end of his idyllic puppyhood. One frightening day, he is whisked away from the farm and sent to combat training by a military doctor named Soojin, who can communicate with certain “special” dogs by reading their minds. She is the one to bestow him with his name: All dogs, Etna explains, “had private names” that “our mothers called us by, but we never shared those.” The name Etna, volcanic in its association, aligns with the new life being imposed upon him. “You’re now war dogs, my darling,” Soojin tells Etna, as she drives him and his canine partner, Fuji, to a drop-off location. “You’ll make new friends and you’re going to work hard and save a lot of lives.” Read: How an epic and violent family history fuels fiction Etna is distressed by this turn of events—he wants to return to the farm—but he does not resist or try to flee. The novel elides any ham-fisted meditations on the dominant species’ impositions; although Yoon is clearly fond of his narrator, he nimbly avoids the tendency of many animal tales to traffic in neat parables or didactic moralism. (As a lifelong reader of Black Beauty, I freely admit that Sewell, like so many Victorian writers, does not manage this as well.) Nonetheless, Etna and his fellow creatures understand their subordinate position in the hierarchy. He and Fuji are assigned to a unit of American soldiers sent to bolster this country’s self-defense. They undergo an education in ruthlessness and become, as Soojin predicts, skilled war dogs. “I learned to be good at it,” Etna later tells another dog. “All of it.” He is not altogether regretful of this knowledge, for his adeptness brings certain pleasures: for instance, distilling the “strong clear line that was a single smell” from a landscape’s sensory chaos, even if it leads to explosives or human remains. But in the squall of war, Etna’s talents also beget inexorable demands. He is injured six times; gradually, his body accumulates “stripes of scars like old worms” that render him unrecognizable to himself. He loses his friends, both canine and human. And although Etna’s narrative voice—vulnerable, curious, endearingly direct—inclines the reader to envision the pluckiest good boy, humanity’s violence deprives him of a gentle youth. Like any soldier, Etna learns to kill on command. The war lasts four cataclysmal years. Etna’s country prevails but is demolished in the process; its people and animals are left wrecked, hungry, and homeless. One night, Etna rests near two of the surviving soldiers in his unit, eavesdropping on their boozy conversation, during which one contemplates shooting Etna as a gesture of mercy. “None of us were right in the head after what we saw and did and what our bodies and souls endured,” the soldier reasons. The dog takes no offense at this suggestion; these are “young humans,” he explains, who “had been good to their dogs” until they were lost in battle. “Everyone wanted to go home. And to go away.” Etna, like so many dogs, is an empathetic creature, and the grace he extends to these soldiers facilitates the epiphany that propels the rest of the story: He, too, wants to return home, and so he departs on a harrowing journey. In the midst of it, Etna befriends a small, eager canine, Semeru, who knows Soojin and helps Etna reunite with her. Semeru’s introduction as the sidekick brings a touch of comedic respite; he has two interspecies exchanges, with a cat and with a donkey, that made me laugh out loud. I suspect that some readers will regard his dynamic with Etna as cliché; after all, it is difficult to render an affectionate animal relationship without a touch of bathos. But Semeru provides a crucial intimacy for Etna, whose experience of the war has been one of agonizing isolation. This is, at times, a wrenching novel, strewn with broken bodies, but its atrocities never overwhelm its faith in creaturely communion, and in grief and longing that clarify, rather than obscure, one’s desires and selfhood. “What do you know about creatures?” Etna wonders to himself, about a group of missionaries he encounters on his journey. He might well ask his readers the same thing. In real life, our dogs cannot tell us what they make of the disasters we humans have wrought, nor can their fidelity absolve us. But we can see by their perked ears and wagging tails that they are waiting to see what happens next. ​When you buy a book using a link on this page, we receive a commission. For the past few months, Iran has intermittently fought a war with the United States, Israel, and its Arab neighbors. But throughout this period, it has never stopped waging war on its own citizens. The Islamic Republic is executing Iranians in record numbers and has sharpened its tools of repression. Such tactics have never succeeded in pacifying Iranian society for long. Today, some civil-society leaders, and even figures inside the establishment itself, fret that the regime’s heavy domestic hand is not only hurting Iranians but also weakening the country’s war effort. On Tuesday, the Iranian regime executed two young men. Abolfazl Sepahi, 23, and Amirhossein Safari, 28, had been arrested in Isfahan in January in connection with the nationwide anti-regime protests. Dozens of Isfahanis came out to protest the two men’s execution in the days leading up to it, but to no avail. On Monday, security forces dispersed the crowd with paintballs, tear gas, and shots into the air. Sepahi and Safari were hanged in the very same square where they had allegedly demonstrated. Back in January, the regime put down mammoth protests with particular brutality, killing thousands of people in the space of two days. In the months since, Iranian courts have sentenced dozens of citizens to death for taking part in the protests. According to UN estimates, 24 have already been executed. According to Amnesty International, at least 60 others, including some who were arrested as children, remain at risk of execution. Sepahi and Safari were among 60 people accused of killing four members of the police forces during the protests in Isfahan. The regime claims that the four officers were tied up in the city’s central square, stoned, and then burned alive. Earlier this month, two other young men—a 19-year-old Iranian and a 24-year-old Afghan national—were executed in connection with the same events, and a dozen more people face capital punishment for them. The regime media have distributed an unclear video from the incident and aired confessions of some of the arrestees. But no due process was available to the accused, and the regime media are known for airing forced confessions. Sepahi and Safari had access to no legal representation, not even court-appointed lawyers. They were reportedly tried in three virtual court sessions, each of which took about an hour and in which the accused phoned in from prison rather than appearing before the judge. Among those alarmed by the state’s mounting repression are civil-society figures who have staunchly opposed the war with America and Israel. It earned enough derision that El-Sayed put out a statement to his followers about how such comments were “unkind and unhelpful” and that if you supported him, “please stop.” (Bernie Sanders used to put out these statements when he ran against Hillary Clinton, too.) In another familiar move, the Stevens campaign was in the process of leaning into it, with Stevens staffers showing me the box of freshly printed “STICK IT TO ‘EM” signs her team had just produced. A friend had even made it her ringtone, she told me. “But, hey, whatever’s resonating, whatever brings a little bit of joy. Whatever brings a little bit of that Stick It to ’Em,” she said, doing the thing. Something felt disorienting about the event. On this valuable Saturday-morning time slot a couple of weeks before the primary, we were in a small flower shop in a pedestrian-unfriendly area with few, if any, walk-in customers to meet and greet. There were several campaign aides, though, as well as various campaign camerapeople taking photos behind us. Hours later, when I looked at Stevens’ X feed, I was surprised to see a photo of our interview, appearing as though we reporters were part of a bustling roundtable Stevens had held with voters. Absent the voters, and for the purposes of the photo op, we would have to do. Prior to this cycle, Stevens may not have had trouble getting through a primary. Michigan Democrats haven’t had a genuinely competitive Senate primary since 1994. (That was also the last year Democrats lost a Senate race in Michigan—which, move along! Nothing to see here.) She’s a loyal House Democrat, a pragmatist focused more on legislation than spectacle. That’s usually enough to clear the primary field. Democratic voters in competitive states, always on edge about electability, have tended to follow the cues from Washington. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, who supports Stevens, doesn’t dictate each race, and some of his preferences are made more overt than others. But for all the grief Schumer gets about this top-down approach (“treating politicians like trading cards,” as El-Sayed put it in a rally in Grand Rapids), it’s worked pretty well for Democrats. In three separate cycles since I’ve been covering elections—2010, 2012, and 2022—Republicans have blown good chances at the Senate majority by nominating fatally flawed candidates. Democrats, who have less margin for error given their structural disadvantage in the chamber, haven’t. What Democrats have blown, though, is two out of three races against Donald Trump. That has created a trust gap between the party establishment and many Democratic voters. And it’s opened up the market to new types of candidates—candidates who will fight, and candidates who will champion progressive causes that the Democratic Party as a whole has never jumped on board with, like Medicare for all. At Stevens’ next event that Saturday, a summer picnic put on by Democrats in her Oakland County congressional district, I asked her why she thinks a progressive has been able to give someone like her a run for her money. “I’ll just say this: I signed up for this election knowing it was going to be a primary, right?” she said. “I knew it was going to be a race, and I decided to run because it was not the time to be comfortable.” This was the most I would get, but it was enough. She was telling me that she wasn’t so naive as to believe that after 2024, with withering rage directed at the Democratic establishment, and a large Arab American community in Michigan that had protest-voted against the Biden administration’s Gaza policy, she—a card-carrying member of the Democratic establishment and close ally of AIPAC—would get a free primary ride. Maybe she didn’t get the best crowds. Maybe she was suffering an inordinate amount of vitriol online about her accent, her looks, her mannerisms, and her vast financial support from corporate PACs, wealthy donors, and the Israel lobby. But the immense national pressure of carrying a Democratic establishment to victory in a must-win state did not in any outward way appear to be breaking her spirits. Even though she was, by all accounts, struggling to do so. Before she gave a small speech at the picnic, Stevens took a few questions from reporters. As her team worked to assess which direction she should face for the best shot, she stood next to an unlit public charcoal grill and joked, “I’m hot, I’m hot!” Then, turning to me, she muttered, “Not according to the internet this week. I’m joking!” “She has events?” Abdul El-Sayed asked sarcastically the following day, in Grand Rapids, when I said I had attended some of Haley Stevens’ stops on the trail. I offered that they were certainly of a different nature from the rallies he was hosting. “She can’t have rallies of her own,” he said. “You have to have supporters to have rallies.” Stevens has supporters, though their idea of Saturday night may not involve going to campaign rallies. El-Sayed argues that the way she’s earning those votes, though, will never bring about real change. That’s because, stripped to their essence, modern Senate campaigns tend to be large cash transfers from wealthy donors to the owners of local television affiliates. The events on the ground are helpful for forming certain narratives in the press and earning coverage where you can. But campaigns rely on the tens of millions of dollars in advertisements to achieve the actual work of shifting poll numbers. As Stevens and I had been speaking at the flower shop, or the picnic, the real effort to get Haley Stevens into the general election was taking place on the small screen, through a constellation of super PACs. Chief among them is the United Democracy Project, AIPAC’s super PAC. Per the most recent Federal Election Commission filings, UDP has spent about $25 million in advertisements and direct mail. The second-largest super PAC, A Stronger Michigan, came into existence on June 1 and has plunked down nearly $17 million already. Per its mid-July filing, its funding had come almost entirely from a single nonprofit—making it “dark money” that doesn’t need to disclose its donors. A third, Unite to Win, has spent nearly $3 million. There are some smaller super PACs supporting El-Sayed. But all told, the pro-Stevens PACs were outspending their competition 12-to-1 as the race ramped up in mid-July. UDP’s ads don’t talk about Israel. One of its most discussed spots features former President Barack Obama praising Stevens’ work on the auto industry rescue, but the clip is from a 2018 rally—Obama has not endorsed in the current race. The Stevens campaign, though, is banking heavily on turning out Black voters, among whom she enjoys a significant advantage, in metro Detroit. El-Sayed told me that before this race, he didn’t “understand just how craven big money could get.” He knew that there would be big money at play in the race, particularly AIPAC money, as he’s a vocal critic of Israel’s war in Gaza. But since seeing that Obama ad? “You know, when you have AIPAC running ads pretending like President Obama endorsed their candidate, even though they spent the end of his presidency opposing his principal foreign policy goal”—the Iran nuclear deal—“it’s just craven. It’s cynical. I think a lot of the establishment is just showing just how cynical they are about democracy itself.” This isn’t the first time AIPAC has come to Stevens’ rescue. In 2022, the year in which AIPAC formed UDP and began intervening in primaries, the super PAC helped carry her across the line in a member-on-member primary against Rep. Andy Levin. But its massive intervention in this race is as much about stopping El-Sayed as it is about supporting Stevens. A swing-state Senate seat would be a groundbreaking development for those Democrats hoping to upend American policy toward Israel. And so, as El-Sayed put it to me, “they spend all this money to put my head on a pike.” But given Democrats’ turn against Israel over the past few years and AIPAC itself becoming such a lightning rod, El-Sayed has worked to use the group’s heavy-handed involvement as his principal argument against Stevens. It’s hardly a new technique for a less funded candidate to complain that their opponent is bought and paid for by special interests. Usually the complainer loses, and life goes on. This time, given the bright spotlight on the race, AIPAC’s new villain status among many in the Democratic base, and El-Sayed’s gifts as a communicator, progressives hope this race can chart a new course for defeating big money in campaigns by weaponizing it in return. “For many years,” Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez told reporters after the Detroit rally, “we’ve been chipping away at how to figure out how we actually break up the system of big money in politics.” She listed some recent races in which the progressive candidates had been outspent but won their primaries. “People are starting, and communities are starting, I think, to associate a lot of this large spending with the big money that requires it to be.” In 2020, four years after losing to Hillary Clinton, the presidential nomination was Bernie Sanders’ to lose. But South Carolina primary voters fled to the safety of Joe Biden, someone they, then the rest of the field and the party establishment, decided was “electable.” It was effectively over by Super Tuesday the following week. “Electability,” interpreted as risk aversion, has governed Democratic voting strategy in the high-stakes era of Donald Trump, standing as a bulwark against the inroads Sanders and his descendants have made into the party. Polls show Haley Stevens and Abdul El-Sayed performing about equally in hypothetical matchups against Mike Rogers, a noncrazy former House Republican who’s running again after he lost by only 19,000 votes to Sen. Elissa Slotkin two years ago. But Stevens more aptly fits the conventional understanding of electable, a point that her campaign is hammering home as its closing message. She’s not particularly ideological, and she stays on message about affordability and stopping Trump’s corruption. “All I can speak to is my record that I have won tough primaries, and I’ve won tough general elections, and I have done so based on my passion, my commitment, my Michigan way of campaigning, fighting, and delivering for people,” she told me when I asked what makes her more electable. The subtext of her argument, though, is that El-Sayed specifically isn’t electable. El-Sayed has a history, which UDP has been eager to point out, of calling to defund the police, for example. He also supports abolishing Immigration and Customs Enforcement, a position that still gives many Democrats the willies, even with all of the agency’s recent abuses. Republicans agree with the Stevens camp’s view that El-Sayed is the easier opponent. That’s why Senate Republicans ran a web ad in late June warning that El-Sayed was “too radical for Michigan”—a meddling tactic designed to boost his name recognition and make him seem especially cool to Democratic primary voters. And as CNN reported in late July, Rogers told supporters in a June phone call about El-Sayed: “If he wins, I think we’re in good shape.” El-Sayed isn’t really having it. “It’s funny watching folks make an argument about electability when electable candidates don’t need $50 million crutches,” he told me. “Just think about what this race would be if there was no outside spending at all. We wouldn’t be talking about any opposition. This would be such a lopsided race. Y’all wouldn’t be covering it.” But there’s another dimension around the “electability” conversation. El-Sayed is a Muslim and Arab American, and no such person has ever been elected to the U.S. Senate. El-Sayed and his surrogates insinuated that ultimately, this is what the “electability” talk is all about—a coded way to worry that his race and religion will prevent him from winning. “I want to tell you what they’re whispering about Michiganders out in Washington, because I’ve heard it myself,” Ocasio-Cortez told the crowd at the Detroit rally. “They’re saying a state like yours, and people like you, can’t elect an American like Abdul.” “They’ve taken to calling me ‘dangerous,’ ” El-Sayed said in his speech, before laying into some sarcasm. “Now, I don’t know why you would call somebody who looks like me, with a name like mine, ‘dangerous.’ It’s an interesting adjective to use. Nobody’s ever called somebody who looks like me ‘dangerous.’ ” I asked El-Sayed if, when people argue he’s unelectable, he felt they were talking about his ethnicity or his politics. He was clear that it’s the former. “When you call somebody dangerous, and then your whole ad campaign is about how you’re disrespectful to women, let’s ask what they’re playing to,” he said. “Part of me is just like, I’d respect you more if you just came right out and said it.” El-Sayed had just finished his two biggest rallies of his campaign and was about to start his third. He seemed boggled at how a candidate with thousands of people screaming for him could be deemed the less electable one. “I just think it’s really important to be asking, like, how do you win when you haven’t built a movement?” he said of Stevens. “You know, people are talking about electability as if it’s this thing on paper. Movements are electability.” But senators win without movements all the time. Just about all of them do, really. Elissa Slotkin won the same state, against the same Republican, in a worse environment, without a “movement,” just two years ago. For every Democratic voter who might attend an El-Sayed megarally, there are many who vote regularly but don’t insist upon, or want, radical change. They don’t need electoral politics to thrill them. They just need to win. The warm-up band at Abdul El-Sayed’s Grand Rapids rally with Bernie Sanders and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez was electric. It was a Latin-infused seven-piece ensemble that could (and did) range from “Oye Como Va” to “Don’t Stop Believin’.” It was enough to get El-Sayed himself to emerge and sing a few bars of “Bohemian Rhapsody” hours before he was set to speak. Shortly after this, I was led backstage to his greenroom for my interview. I expected to see him in similarly jovial spirits. Instead, he was hunched over in a single foldout plastic chair in an otherwise empty room, looking spent. The primary has been exhausting, especially given the pressure being applied by national political factions. (Both candidates said they didn’t feel the pressure. I didn’t believe either of them.) But if the candidates are exhausted now, just wait until the day after the primary. Not only will they have just three months to pivot to the general election and Mike Rogers, but they’ll have to work just as hard, and just as quickly, to repair the wreckage from this primary. Just as it would be a triumph to win the primary, so too would it be an embarrassment to go on to lose a Michigan Senate seat, in a good Democratic year, for the first time since the Clinton administration. The credibility of whichever Democratic faction wins this proxy primary is on the line. If Haley Stevens wins, a lot of progressive Democrats will, rightly or wrongly, feel disgusted, as if big money and AIPAC stole the election from the candidate who’d run the better race. The El-Sayed campaign’s rhetoric against Stevens would not make a postprimary reconciliation any easier either. I asked attendees at El-Sayed’s Detroit rally if they would vote for Stevens in a general election if it came to that. One, a 19-year-old student at Michigan State, told me he might, “but it’s just really hard because I feel like when I talk about Stevens, I’m talking about another Republican.” If El-Sayed wins, moderate Democrats in Michigan—who’ve never been in the position of having to hold their nose and vote for a progressive Senate nominee—will have to find some way to get comfortable with him. A lot of the reconciliation would be up to the candidates themselves. The last time I saw Stevens on the trail was at a Black church in Ann Arbor, where she spoke to a welcoming congregation about the “complex challenges before us that aren’t going to be met with simple solutions or simple slogans.” Addressing her target audience, she emphasized her role working with the Obama administration on the auto rescue and her support from the Congressional Black Caucus. She hung around the service for a bit after giving remarks, keeping her hand raised in praise—as best I could tell, she was the only congregant doing this—with her head bowed as the gospel choir got going. I asked her afterward if she would endorse and campaign for El-Sayed if she lost the primary. “Absolutely—I am on the team, I am so proud to be on the team,” she said. “You know, Mike Rogers wants to rubber-stamp Donald Trump. I’m going to do everything I can. That’s why I’ve been a part of this process right now. I’d be excited and honored to earn the nomination, but we’re going to finish this election, and we’re going to win.” When Bernie Sanders lost the Democratic nomination in 2016, he got a lot of grief for taking his sweet time to endorse Hillary Clinton. At least part of Sanders’ thinking, though, was that his campaign had portrayed Clinton as a model of corrupt, corporate, and establishment nefariousness, and it would take his supporters some time to come back for air. So he negotiated with the Clinton campaign on the party platform to show his supporters that he at least got something. Still, on the first day of the Democratic National Convention in Philadelphia that year, when Sanders convened his delegates to break the news that we “have got to elect Hillary Clinton,” he was roundly booed. So I asked El-Sayed the same question later that night: Would he endorse and campaign for Stevens if she lost? “I’m committed to beating Mike Rogers, I’ve said that so many times,” he said. I noted that that wasn’t precisely a yes. “As I’ve said, I am going to do everything I need to do to defeat Mike Rogers.” We may never learn what he meant by sticking to such a precise response. The polling has largely shown El-Sayed ahead of Stevens. That’s in line, frankly, with the developments in the race. Top state Democrats, like Sen. Gary Peters and Gov. Gretchen Whitmer, who had no interest in getting involved in this primary, have come off the sidelines in the final few weeks to endorse Stevens. The super PACs are not easing their spending. The Stevens camp, and the Democratic establishment generally, is unloading its arsenal. If Stevens loses, then El-Sayed will have done something unprecedented for progressives since Sanders offered the first draft a decade ago. He’ll have a chance to test-drive the progressive theory of electability in a prime swing election. We’ll finally get an answer. The only risk for Democrats is that the answer is no. Companies will typically hire teams of human testers to try to come up with novel attacks that break existing guardrails, a process known as red-teaming. Model makers also use LLM super-hackers (such as OpenAI’s GPT-Red) that find and exploit weaknesses in other models to automate parts of this process. The goal is then to take those attacks and train a new model to resist them and anything that looks like them. The problem, says Jasmine Cui, another independent researcher and coauthor of the paper, is that the approach amounts to giving the models a list of things they shouldn’t do. In June 2024, a small company called Zanskar purchased a geothermal power plant in New Mexico that was failing fast. The water coming from the underground reservoir was getting colder by the day, making the plant uneconomical to run. Now, two years later, that plant is running at full capacity again, thanks to a new well. With the help of advanced modeling and modern drilling technology, the company was able to identify a better potential well site, drill down thousands of feet, and revive the entire operation. As the world looks for more sources of emissions-free electricity that are available 24-7, Lightning Dock shows there’s still hidden potential deep beneath our feet. Conventional geothermal power plants rely on having the right underground conditions. Water flows through fractured hot rocks to harvest heat and then through a power plant to generate electricity. If the water isn’t hot enough or not enough flows, the power plant can’t work efficiently. Lightning Dock came online in 2013, and the site historically had two production wells that were used to feed the power plant. It’s common to see temperatures drop at a well site over time, typically at a rate of 1 to 2 °F per year. In the period before Zanskar took over the site, Lightning Dock saw temperatures drop by 50 °F over five years, a rate of 10 °F per year. When the company purchased the facility, the water going into the power plant was just 250 °F, while the plant was designed to operate at temperatures of at least 310 °F. When Zanskar mapped the underground conditions at Lightning Dock using advanced modeling techniques, they discovered that the wells were only hitting the very top of the reservoir there. Those production wells were quite shallow (at just 2,500 feet deep) and not in the best location, says Joel Edwards, Zanskar’s cofounder and CEO. The company’s modeling predicted that if the company were to drill another, deeper well in a new spot, the plant would be able to run efficiently. The team drilled a new well that reaches a depth of 8,000 feet and started operation in May 2025. After a full year, the well is still flowing at more than 4,000 gallons per minute. The plant has “completely turned around,” Edwards says. “It looks really exciting.” The data so far shows that the plant should be successful for years to come. “Ultimately you need to run these things for long time frames to get confidence in their performance over long time frames,” Edwards says. The progress at Lightning Dock could be good news for other geothermal sites too. The conventional wisdom in geothermal energy is that the deeper you go, the hotter it gets. But there’s usually a trade-off: With those depths come rocks that are packed tighter together. So drilling deeper could mean the hot water can’t flow as effectively, a problem for anyone trying to use it to generate electricity in a power plant. What the team found, however, was that the flow actually increased in the area where they drilled the new well. “That fundamentally changes how you think about not just Lightning Dock but all hydrothermal assets in America and what the potential can be for all of them,” says Ben Brenner, director of federal affairs at Zanskar. Over the past year of operation, Lightning Dock generated over twice the electricity it would have with the old wells. This is a relatively small power plant, with a capacity of 15 megawatts going to the local grid (about enough to power 11,000 US homes). Oil and gas developers have chased resources deeper underground over the past few decades. Operations started relatively close to the surface, but oil and gas production can stretch down 20,000 feet or more today. “I think that arc is going to play out in geothermal,” Edwards says. While typical geothermal well fields range from 3,000 to 5,000 feet deep, it could become more common to go deeper in the future, he adds. The company plans to do further development at the Lightning Dock site: with a few years of development and an upgrade to the power plant, it could get even more electricity out of this area, Edwards says. There’s been a lot of buzz and investment in enhanced geothermal systems—projects that aim to expand where geothermal resources can be used. Fervo Energy, for example, uses fracking techniques to open up rock that would otherwise be too closed off for traditional geothermal energy. But there’s still plenty of “low-hanging fruit” in the geothermal world, Edwards says. Many of these conventional resources have plenty of potential—they just need a second look.
