"At Home in Space" opens, caps Air and Space Museum renovation

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-02

After seven years of work and nearly $1 billion, the Smithsonian has completed the renovation of the National Air and Space Museum’s flagship building in Washington, DC. On October 30, the museum debuted its last-to-open spaceflight exhibition, returning “home” with a gallery devoted to establishing a spacefaring civilization. “Welcome to ‘At Home in Space’!” said curator Jennifer Levasseur, who guided us through a virtual tour of the gallery. “This is the story of long-duration spaceflight in the last few decades. We generally call it ‘reaching orbit.’” “It’s the prep story, the space shuttle story. It’s what allows us to end up at a place where we have 25-plus years of long-duration spaceflight experience, the capacity for humans to be living in space on the International Space Station (ISS), and then it carries that story forward. So the middle of our story then becomes the ISS, and the end of the story are the next steps,” said Levasseur. Over the past several years, the National Air and Space Museum has introduced new galleries showcasing how US astronauts first entered space and landed on the Moon (“Destination Moon”); how robotic probes have explored every planet in our solar system (“Kenneth C. Griffin Exploring the Planets”); how technologies and infrastructure impact our daily lives (“RTX Living in the Space Age Hall”) and what approaches spaceflight will take in the years to come (“Futures in Space”). “At Home in Space,” which follows the similarly themed “Moving Beyond Earth,” immerses guests in the equipment, spacecraft, and orbital platforms that have enabled humanity from simply visiting space to living there, setting up the near future for bases on the Moon and Mars. “We have a great way to attract people into the gallery, which is this overhead mock-up of an astronaut on the end of the robotic arm, and then the backdrop to that is this photo mural—a full-size photo print—of the ISS,” said Levasseur. “Our astronaut up here is intended to be [an actual] person, but I’m not going to share who that is. They are wearing an Expedition 60 patch, so that’ll give you a little clue.” Some of the gallery’s artifacts include a space shuttle main engine, Robonaut 1A (a prototype humanoid robot), and an extravehicular mobility unit (EMU) spacesuit. “It’s pretty damn special,” said Levasseur of the EMU. “This, as far as I know, is the first and potentially only public display of a fully flown set of EMU equipment in the world. I know NASA has one in its EVA office, but it is not on public display.” Unlike Apollo-era spacesuits sized to each astronaut, the EMU was designed to be reused, so its components can be swapped out to fit astronauts of different sizes. Introduced during the early years of the space shuttle program, the same EMU suits are still used on the International Space Station for US spacewalks. The museum’s example is only missing a functioning portable life support system (PLSS), as all the backpacks are still needed for use. So the suit on display only has a PLSS shell for now, according to Levasseur. The gallery also features Guy Bluford’s and Sally Ride’s in-flight suits, the “shirt-sleeve” outfits the first African American and first US woman wore on the space shuttle (on separate missions). “Sally is notably missing her jacket, which was a late change to our plan. We were given a drawing of this, a 3D mockup of its display case, and our designers then put it in this way without the jacket on. And we thought, ‘Hey, that’s actually kind of a nice contrast. Let’s keep the jacket out,’” Levasseur told collectSPACE. The gallery also includes recovered components from the Challenger and Columbia tragedies. It is only the second public display of fallen orbiter debris after a Kennedy Space Center Visitor Complex exhibit that opened in Florida in 2015. “We wanted to honor the individuals who were lost during the shuttle program,” said Levasseur. “So we have an elevon hinge from Challenger that washed up on the beach a few years ago and a little wiring panel fragment from inside the payload bay. And then from Columbia we thought it was appropriate to help tell that story with a piece of RCC [reinforced carbon-carbon]. This is a T-panel from the starboard wing. We also have a CD [compact disc] on a long-term loan from David Brown’s family.” Visitors can also walk through a full-size mock-up of the space station’s Destiny module, the US national laboratory on the station, and view its equipment on both sides, as well as above and below. “Originally, the detailing on the floor was going to be a mosaic of real images, but we found that for accessibility reasons people might come in and see the cables and worry that they were real, creating a tripping hazard,” said Levasseur. “On the flip side, and I had to fight to get these, we had to have labels to indicate activity happens on the floor and happens on the overhead. People need to see this is a literally three-dimensional space.” The module has interactive elements on one side and showcases some of the crew equipment-related artifacts, including coffee cups engineered for use in microgravity. “This is probably the best section in my mind because it has fun stuff,” said Levasseur, pointing out the flown miniPCR biotech toolkit and MinION portable device used to sequence DNA in space for the first time. “My absolute favorite thing to work on in this gallery answers the questions: What does space smell like, and what does the ISS smell like? We have little scent oils inside each of these that allow people to smell them,” she said. Nearby, the  first 3D printer used in space is also on display. In 2014, Made In Space (now part of Redwire) and NASA flew the printer, which produced hundreds of objects, from astronaut tools to space station repair parts. The gallery concludes with a space reserved to exhibit the Artemis I capsule, the spacecraft that flew without astronauts to the Moon in 2022. A Lockheed Martin scale model of Orion holds the place until NASA delivers the real spacecraft to the Smithsonian, which is expected later this year. “This is the piece of Wright Flyer fabric that went to the moon with Artemis II, so that’s on display here, too,” Levasseur said, concluding the tour. The renovation of the National Air and Space Museum also included refacing the exterior cladding, replacing mechanical systems, and other improvements. The first half of the museum reopened in 2022 with eight new or reimagined galleries, the Northrop Grumman Planetarium, museum store, and the Mars Café. The second phase opened in 2025 with five new galleries, and five additional galleries opened July 1. Following “At Home in Space,” “Modern Military Aviation” will open on November 11. “We are thrilled to complete the most extensive project the museum has ever undergone,” Chris Browne, John and Adrienne Mars Director of the museum, said in a statement. “With the opening of these last two galleries, the public will once again be able to visit the entirety of the museum and learn from and engage with the nation’s world-class aerospace collection.” Admission to all of the National Air and Space Museum galleries remains free, but timed-entry passes for the building are required to visit.

It’s a regrettable reality that there is never enough time to cover all the interesting scientific stories we come across. So every month, we highlight a handful of the best stories that nearly slipped through the cracks. October’s list includes an epigenetic mechanism for why large dogs age faster than smaller ones; physicists who trapped a single particle of antimatter for months; and invisible tattoo inks that only show up under UV light. In many mammalian species, larger creatures tend to have longer lifespans. Mice, for instance, live an average of just two years, while some whales can last for as long as 200 years. Dogs, however, buck this pattern; larger dogs tend to age faster and die younger than smaller ones, which makes canines an ideal model for biologists to study to learn more about aging in general. Arizona State University scientists report that these lifespan differences seem to be due to so-called “jumping genes,” according to a paper published in the journal Science. The authors used data from 864 dogs collected in the Dog Aging Project to map patterns of DNA methylation (which influences how much certain genes are turned on or off)  across the entire genome. It’s part of the epigenome, whose markers can change over time in response to stress and other environmental factors. Genetic sequences, by contrast, are largely fixed. So DNA methylation is a good chemical indicator of aging and DNA regulation. Their analysis showed that aging in dogs is linked to the decrease in the regulation provided by DNA methylation, particularly in regions where there are “jumping genes” that can move from chromosome to chromosome. These jumps can damage DNA, causing cancer and other age-related diseases, and their activity increases with the loss of regulatory markers. The authors identified a specific class of jumping genes called LINE1s that were responsible for the different rates of aging in dogs. Giant dog breeds lost roughly 35 percent more LINE1 methylation each year compared to small breeds. “This is one of the clearest molecular signatures we’ve seen that aligns with the well-known size-lifespan trade-off in dogs,” said co-author Blaise Mariner. “It suggests that faster aging in larger dogs may be driven, at least in part, by reduced control over these jumping genes.” Science, 2026. When matter and antimatter particles collide, they annihilate into energy, so that asymmetry resulted in matter dominating our universe, which contains much more matter than antimatter. It’s extremely difficult to create and isolate antimatter, requiring special traps. Physicists at Northwestern University have succeeded in trapping a single positron (antimatter particle) for months on end, according to a paper published in Physical Review Letters. Co-author Gerald Gabrielse and his team built on earlier work, in which they trapped a single electron to create a one-electron quantum cyclotron. They then used the setup to make one of the most precise measurements of its magnetic moment yet. Trapping a positron was a bit trickier. They harvested the positron from sodium-22 nuclei, slowing it down enough to guide it into a near-perfect vacuum trap of electric and magnetic fields. Once inside, they chilled the positron to just 300 millikelvins (0.3° above absolute zero), thereby keeping the positron in its quantum ground state. They found they could maintain that state for months at a time. That enabled Gabrielse et al. to manipulate and detect the particle’s individual quantum energy state using tuned microwaves—the first critical step in being able to measure the positron’s magnetic properties, hopefully with the same precision they achieved in their earlier experiments with a single electron. The Standard Model of particle physics predicts that there should be no difference between the two particle measurements. If they are not equal, “that would be the biggest discovery in physics in the last century,” said Gabrielse. Physical Review Letters, 2026. DOI: 10.1103/gbkc-6nlj. A person in Washington state has died from bird flu after exposure at an infected dairy farm, state health officials reported Sunday. It marks the third human bird flu death in the US since a strain of highly pathogenic (H5) avian influenza (HPAI) made the unprecedented jump from wild, migratory birds to dairy cows, which was first reported in the US in March 2024. The person who died was a Yakima County resident and was said to have had underlying health conditions, which could have made the person more vulnerable to the infection. On Friday, county officials reported that the person was severely ill and hospitalized. In the subsequent announcement of the person’s death, officials said they would not be releasing further information about the person, including their name, gender, or age, out of respect for the family’s privacy. “Our hearts go out to the individual’s family and loved ones during this difficult time,” Dave Atteberry, health officer for the Yakima Health District, said in a statement. “Avian influenza can cause severe illness, particularly among people who have underlying health conditions. While this is a serious and heartbreaking outcome, the risk to the general public remains low.” In all, three people have tested positive for bird flu in Washington since the virus was reported in dairy cattle in the state on October 8. All three have been in Yakima County. While announcing the death on Sunday, officials also reported the third case. That case, like the first case, was in a person who had exposure to an infected dairy herd in the county. Both people have reportedly not experienced severe illness nor been hospitalized. Since the October 8 report of infected herds, Washington state has had 40 detections of bird flu in dairy cows, according to the US Department of Agriculture. Most of those have been in Yakima County, which has reported detections in 33 herds. This year, the USDA has reported 134 cases of bird flu in cattle, including cases in Texas, Washington, Idaho, Utah, and Kansas. The USDA lists no other reports of cases in other domestic livestock, including swine. But the virus has continued to spread in other wild and captive mammals. A report last month from ProPublica revealed that the virus is raging on a mink farm in Utah. The report raised alarm because mink is a species that could give the virus opportunities to mutate to better spread among humans. To date, no human-to-human transmission of HPAI has been reported from the over 70 human cases detected since 2024. Of the cases, only three have resulted in death. The first death was in a 65-year-old Louisiana resident with underlying health conditions, who became critically ill in January 2025 after exposure to infected wild and backyard birds. The second death, in November 2025, was reported by Washington state officials as an older adult with underlying health conditions who also had exposure to infected backyard birds.

Most of our measures of a key cognitive process don't agree

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-02

Visit Original Website
It’s widely acknowledged that it’s nearly impossible to publish negative results in scientific journals. “We didn’t see anything” is a much harder sell than finding that a hypothesis is clearly right. Findings of no effect tend to either be shuffled off to low-profile journals or left behind as unpublished lab lore. The high-profile journal Nature, however, made a notable exception this week, publishing a study that tells us that a bunch of methods we have been using to try to measure a key emotional ability don’t seem to be measuring the same thing. The results mean that we don’t have a good way to measure the impact of a concept that is the basis of a key method in therapy. One of the most successful forms of therapy is cognitive behavioral therapy, or CBT. In part, CBT is based on getting people to step back and engage in “cognitive reappraisal.” This requires people to think over a situation that has caused a negative emotional response and find ways to reinterpret it so that it has less impact on them. The ability to engage in this process has been linked to emotional resilience. Given its importance, a lot of studies have looked at what’s going on in the brain when people engage in cognitive reappraisal. There are also a number of questionnaires that are thought to measure a person’s capacity to engage in the process. So, a large international collaboration of researchers decided to look at these more carefully. “We focused on three widely used indices that are often assumed to reflect related facets of reappraisal capacity: trait questionnaires, task-based affective ratings following reappraisal, and amygdala down-regulation during reappraisal,” they wrote. The amygdala is a structure deep in the brain that tends to be active when people have emotional responses to events (as well as in a wide variety of additional circumstances). So, it makes sense to expect that it would be less active if people were trying to tone down their emotions. But to find out whether that’s actually correct, you need to look at brain activity. Here, the team relied on 40 functional MRI data sets, providing a large enough data set to tease out even relatively small effects. Which is a good thing, because the effects really were hard to detect. “The effect size was comparatively small: To detect this effect size with a statistical power of 80 percent, a single study would need to test 1,105 participants,” the researchers note, going on to say that very few fMRI studies have access to anywhere near that many subjects. But there were even bigger problems when the differences among individuals in the MRI data were compared to their differences in the questionnaires or task-based ratings. Almost none of the variance in the different measures correlated with each other. We could go through the numbers, but a figure from the paper makes the issue quite clear. Further issues became apparent when the researchers started looking at activity in the areas of the brain that are responsible for high-level decision-making. If people were really engaging their ability to re-evaluate emotional triggers, presumably these areas should be active, as they are needed to power the re-evaluation process. But that wasn’t what the fMRI data showed. Instead, there was a correlation between the response of the amygdala and the response elsewhere in the brain. In other words, if the amygdala was very active, implying a stronger emotional response, then activity in the decision-making areas of the brain was also higher. If the emotional response seemed tamped down, then activity in the reasoning centers was lower as well. In a bit of an understatement, the researchers declared, “These findings call for a fundamental reassessment of how reappraisal capacity is measured and interpreted across behavioural and neural levels of analysis.” The primary issue is that these three ways of looking at emotional regulation were thought to be exploring the same thing, but it’s clear that they’re not. As the researchers point out, this probably shouldn’t be viewed as a surprise. The questionnaires typically ask about overall personality tendencies, and so cover a very long time period. The answers are also likely to implicate a variety of traits beyond simple emotional control. Both the task-based ratings and fMRI work, by contrast, capture in-the-moment responses. But they do so under very, very different experimental circumstances—namely, one of them involves being stuffed in an MRI tube. Beyond that, many of the fMRI experiments involve simplifying the process of emotional regulation in order to get consistent data. It’s entirely possible that these differences also influence brain activity in a variety of ways. So, the take-home appears to be that re-evaluation of emotional responses may be a real phenomenon, but we don’t currently have tools that can clearly capture the process effectively. Gaining that ability may require developing new methods, and thinking more clearly about what different stages of the re-evaluation process require. In the meantime, the people behind this work caution that “strong claims about a person’s emotion regulation capacity based on any specific brain region should be made with considerable caution.” Nature, 2025. A version of this article was originally published by The Conversation (CC BY-ND 4.0) Cast your mind back to your secondary school science classroom: the fiery glow of Bunsen burners, scratched safety goggles that never quite fit, and a giant periodic table pinned to the wall. When you learned about the breakthroughs that shaped our world and the people who made them, whose names were attached? From Newton’s laws to Darwin’s theory of natural selection, the ones you remember are almost certainly male. That experience was not just down to individual teachers. It was and still is written into official GCSE and A-level course specifications. Because these early stages form the foundation of our science education, their omissions stick with us for life. Half of the British population can’t name one woman scientist. Leaving women out of national specifications sends an implicit message about who science is really for. In our new study, led by researchers at the University of Cambridge alongside IncludeHer, a collaborative project working to improve the representation of women in school science education, we analysed over 40 science specifications: the knowledge required to pass exams. We examined the specifications for six main exam boards for Key Stage Four (GCSEs) and Key Stage Five (A-levels), and for Scotland’s National Fives and Highers. Our work builds on an earlier Australian study led by astrophysicist Dr Kat Ross, which uncovered a striking lack of female scientists in high school curricula. We set out to investigate whether UK specifications have similar omissions. Across 224 unique scientists referenced in UK specifications, discoveries are taught almost exclusively through concepts named after men, with only three mentioning women in total. Inge Lehmann, who discovered that the Earth has a solid inner core surrounded by a liquid outer core, is cited in A-level geology via the ‘Lehmann discontinuity’. Marie Tharp, who mapped the ocean floor and identified the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rift valley, is also mentioned in A-level geology. She provided vital evidence for continental drift, despite being prohibited from boarding research vessels for years and having her ideas dismissed as ‘girl talk‘. Finally, Rosalind Franklin is included in two biology specifications for her x-ray crystallography work revealing the double-helix structure of DNA. Her essential data was used without attribution during her lifetime. At GCSE level, when science is compulsory for all students, Franklin is the only woman mentioned. At A-level, Lehmann and Tharp appear solely in geology, a subject taken by less than 2pc of science students. For centuries, social, economic, and institutional barriers limited women’s formal participation in science. Despite this, women made numerous key breakthroughs, and these are already taught to UK 14 to 18-year-olds. But the names of the women behind them did not make it onto the page. Diversity within scientific fields drives creativity and innovation, benefiting all of society. Yet women remain underrepresented. They make up only around one third of the global scientific community, with similar figures seen within the UK workforce. This disparity starts early on in schools: although science uptake is balanced at Key Stage Four, just two years later at Key Stage Five, physics, mathematics and further mathematics only see participation from girls at 24.1pc, 37.3pc and 26.9pc respectively in 2025. This imbalance continues into university, where women make up roughly 25pc of physics undergraduate students in the UK. Introducing relatable female role models into the classroom directly boosts girls’ confidence and fosters positive attitudes towards scientific careers. But because specifications dictate mandatory exam content, teachers currently have to go out of their way to add female figures on top of busy workloads. By making small, targeted changes directly to national specifications, we can make inclusion standard practice across all schools. We propose inserting one female scientist per subject into topics that are already compulsory, but where the woman’s name is missing. This means introducing Marie Skłodowska-Curie, who coined the term radioactivity and discovered polonium and radium, when teaching atomic structure and radioactivity, and Lise Meitner, who provided the first theoretical explanation of nuclear fission, when discussing this concept. GCSE astronomy should include Jocelyn Bell Burnell, who discovered pulsars: rotating neutron stars used as cosmic clocks. Biology teaching can include Dorothy Hodgkin on protein structure, the only British female Nobel laureate in science, and Tu Youyou, a Nobel laureate whose work led to the development of an antimalarial drug. In chemistry, schools can teach about Stephanie Kwolek, the inventor of Kevlar, when covering polymers. For physics, they can include Annie Jump Cannon when describing the classification of stars. And in environmental science, classes can discuss the pivotal work of Susan Solomon for understanding Antarctic ozone depletion. By making these simple additions to science specifications, we can change the message we send to the next generation. Whoever they are, every student sitting in a classroom deserves to know that the story of science belongs to them too. Yet, state Republicans, this cycle at least, seem not to want anything to do with these voters. Since at least last year, they have been aggressively inciting anti-Muslim rhetoric and policies, starting at the top with Gov. Greg Abbott, who is currently locked in a surprisingly tight race for reelection. He went to war with EPIC City, an Islamic development project that sought to build a K–12 school, a mosque, retail space, and housing units, and it took over local headlines. Abbott went so far as to sign a law banning EPIC City from moving forward, characterizing the developers as “bad actors” who would subject occupants to “Sharia law,” whatever his definition of that might be. His fellow Republicans were taking notes. Soon enough, Attorney General Ken Paxton, the Republican nominee for a Texas Senate seat who’s also locked in a tight race—the contest, against Democrat James Talarico, could determine control of the U.S. Senate—followed up with a lawsuit against EPIC City, while Texas Rep. Keith Self also called on the Justice Department and IRS to investigate the developers. Then Abbott struck again, this time sabotaging plans for a celebration of Eid, one of the holiest Islamic holidays celebrating the end of 30 days of fasting, that was supposed to take place at a local water park—a venue that the community had used for the past two years with no issue. Suddenly, Abbott was threatening to pull more than half a million dollars in state funds from the city of Grand Prairie if they allowed the Eid swim party to proceed. It worked, and the city canceled the Eid celebration. Soon, the slogan “Don’t Sharia My Texas” was taking over the state, inspired by an anti-Muslim propaganda film that features conspiracy theorist Alex Jones and right-wing former national security adviser Michael Flynn. Sharia is a set of religious and worldly values for Muslims to follow and is entirely separate from Islamic law, though Republicans have rarely—if ever— attempted to explain what it is they are fearmongering against. “Like racism, Islamophobia can be adapted to align with the political and economic interests of those in power,” Khaled Beydoun, a professor of law at Arizona State University who studies Islamophobia, told the Dallas Morning News. Islamophobia may have hit its peak in America immediately following 9/11. President Bush declared a “war on terror,” and, again, failed to clearly define terrorism, which made the concept borderless and timeless, as explained by the Bridge Initiative at Georgetown University. This oversimplification created two sides: terrorists vs. everyone else, with Muslim Americans becoming targets of both the state and the general population. This period gave way to controversial policies like the National Security Entry-Exit Registration System, the Terrorism Screening Database, and even the establishment of Guantánamo Bay. It would take decades for the public to learn that Bush’s wars were utter failures, and slowly but surely it became normalized to publicly criticize American foreign policy. Eventually, Muslim Americans were empowered to be a part of America’s shifting governance, while also changing the narrative around Islam. Voters began electing Muslim candidates to Washington, and today Michigan Rep. Rashida Tlaib, Minnesota Rep. Ilhan Omar, and New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani are some of the most recognizable political figures in the country, and are also practicing Muslims. In Michigan, Abdul El-Sayed is hoping to make history as the first Muslim American to serve in the U.S. Senate (while also facing perhaps the most bigoted campaign in recent memory). Notably, each of these figures are Democrats, as their party has largely embraced a diverse coalition of voters. Meanwhile, Republicans have decidedly gone the opposite direction, reinvigorating an especially pernicious element of the culture wars of the early aughts. In fact, it was Texas Rep. Chip Roy who cofounded Congress’ “Sharia-Free America Caucus.” He’s also running in a competitive race for Texas AG, and in the process “has posted from both his campaign and official accounts about Muslims, Islam, or ‘sharia law’ more than 244 times since January—the most of any member of Congress,” according to a Washington Post analysis. In the run-up to Texas’ March primary, blatantly anti-Muslim ads were running rampant across the state on behalf of Republican campaigns that baselessly tied Islam with terrorism and crime. All of it was considered “solid gold” by one Republican strategist, who told CNN: “It is playing as well as anything I have ever seen with Texas Republican voters.” Why would overt bigotry that pits fellow Texans against one another be resonating with voters? Perhaps because Republicans themselves have spent decades perpetuating the very Islamophobia the rest of America has been trying to heal. Equality Labs, a South Asian–focused civil rights group, found that Texas led the country in promoting Islamophobia-related disinformation in 2025, largely driven by online posts suggesting that Muslims were invading the U.S. and attempting to impose Sharia law. Consider Bo French, a longtime far-right activist who’s currently running for Texas Railroad Commissioner. In September, he characterized a photo of a group of South Asian University of Texas students celebrating the school’s football team’s win: “Like body-cams exposed criminals, social media is exposing how many Americans have been displaced by foreigners. It’s everywhere and far worse than any of us could imagine.” French’s comment quickly went viral and ignited a national conversation about blatant bigotry against minorities. Equality Labs also found that nationwide, Republicans are largely responsible for spreading Islamophobic content, as opposed to Democrats and independents. This phenomenon is particularly insidious in states, like Texas, where Republicans hold a supermajority, because “the primary electorate becomes the decisive contest, and candidates have to appeal only to a very small sliver of their own electorate,” Brandon Rottinghaus, a political scientist at the University of Houston, told the New York Times. All of this is having a discernible impact nationwide, as Pew Research found that U.S. adults’ attitudes toward Islam have worsened since 9/11. In 2002, six months after the al-Qaida attacks, 25 percent of U.S. adults said Islam is more likely than other religions to encourage violence, but when asked the same question in 2026, that figure had more than doubled to 51 percent. Republicans and independents who lean toward the GOP were far more likely than Democrats and Democratic leaners to say that Islam encourages violence—76 percent vs. 29 percent. Particularly in Texas, this puts hundreds of thousands of Muslims in an untenable position. People like Hussein are actively being disenfranchised by a political party whose conservative values they typically align with. Khalid Hamideh, a spokesman for the Islamic Association of North Texas, told me: “We’re more aligned with the values of the Republican Party when it comes to abortion, same-sex marriage—on all of the social issues, we’re very conservative. We’re very aligned with the people that hate us most.” As they’re left with no other option, Hamideh believes that the recent GOP attacks on Texas Muslims have pushed many toward the Democratic Party unwillingly. Jida Nabulsi also holds conservative values but doesn’t identify herself as either a Democrat or a Republican. “You could say that I would have aligned more with the Republican Party, but given the current state of things, there’s just no sympathy from the Muslim community,” she told me. Nabulsi is the Texas state director for Emgage, a group that has been supporting Talarico’s candidacy. She believes there are plenty of Muslim conservatives in Texas who share her perspective. “The conversations I’m having with my community and other community members, they are telling me that even though they identify with Republicans, nobody should be voting Republican. Nobody should be aligning with that.” Because they are obsessed with perpetuating anti-Muslim hate, Nabulsi believes, Texas state lawmakers are forgoing talking about the actual issues voters care about. “Their top priority as a group is not affordability, bringing down prices, or making life easier for us, but to target one community,” she said. “It does not make sense.” Democrats aren’t necessarily viewed more positively than Republicans right now, but Nabulsi believes they’re at least signaling an open line of communication with a message she interprets as “We are not here with hate.” Just a few weeks ago, Talarico’s campaign met with local Muslim officials and community members in Dallas, where he emphasized a bill he filed that would include Muslim imams as legally recognized marriage officiants. One October poll found Talarico leading Paxton by nearly 17 points among Asian American and Pacific Islander voters, though it’s challenging to carve out Muslim voters here, as they can fall under the listed “Asian,” “South Asian,” “Middle Eastern,” and “African” ethnic groups, all of which also include other religions. Nonetheless, getting Texas Muslims on board could be the windfall Talarico needs to get him over the line against Paxton. Given the serious risk Texas Republicans face right now of losing their ruby-red grip over their state, it might make strategic sense for them to widen their tent, particularly to voters who are already inclined to support them. Muslims are especially primed for the taking, as they’ve increased their voting power over the past 10 years. The Institute for Social Policy and Understanding found that in 2016, 60 percent of eligible American Muslims reported being registered to vote, and by 2025 that figure had jumped up to 85 percent. And since 2024, Muslims have been shifting away from Democrats over their handling of Israel’s war in Gaza, which culminated in tangible action with the “uncommitted” movement, which galvanized voters who were unhappy with the Biden administration’s Israel policy to refrain from voting for either Joe Biden or Kamala Harris. Plenty of ink has been spilled over whether the Biden administration’s Israel policy cost it the 2024 election, but the data suggests that there has been a clear shift. Exit polling conducted by the Council on American-Islamic Relations, the largest Muslim civil rights group, found in 2020 that Biden earned 69 percent of the Muslim vote, compared to Trump’s 17 percent. Four years later in 2024, Harris earned only 20 percent, while Trump received 21 percent and third-party candidate Jill Stein notched 53 percent. That is a lot of voters ripe for the taking. The shift in Muslim voters’ politics is comparable to that of America’s Hispanic electorate, which was considered solidly Democratic until Trump arrived. In 2024, he captured 42 percent of the Latino vote as discourse around immigration doomed the Biden and Harris campaigns. Ironically, Latinos now may swing back to Democrats in 2026, thanks to Trump’s deeply unpopular mass-deportation agenda. The current vitriolic rhetoric being spewed in Texas by Republicans could similarly push Muslims right into Democratic hands. This doesn’t surprise Hussein, who senses that Texas Republicans’ true loyalty always goes back to white Christian nationalist voters. “I think it probably appeals to a portion of the base. I think it’s also a distraction from other things that matter to Texans,” he told me. “It’s a reliable boogeyman to blame Muslims.” This time around, they may just be shooting themselves in the foot. The first thing to know about the Democrat-on-Democrat catastrophe engulfing the Michigan Senate election is that it didn’t have to happen. The second thing to know is that there’s a lot of blame to go around. On Sunday, Rep. Haley Stevens pulled a heel turn for the ages. Stevens, a pro-Israel centrist who narrowly lost the Democratic primary to progressive Abdul El-Sayed, was slated to appear at a “unity rally” on the last weekend before Election Day. Instead, she pulled out—and went to church with El-Sayed’s Republican opponent, former Rep. Mike Rogers. A formal Rogers endorsement followed, completing the betrayal. Now, in a tight race that’s crucial to Democrats’ hopes of retaking the Senate and grinding Donald Trump’s agenda to a halt, a candidate who got almost half of the primary vote is supporting the Republican. There are a few ways in which this disaster could’ve been avoided. If Democratic Sen. Gary Peters—who in congressional terms is but a 67-year-old whippersnapper—had sought reelection, the messy primary that set up Stevens’ about-face never would’ve happened. The same thing is probably true had either Michigan Gov. Gretchen Whitmer or former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg, who lives in Traverse City, Michigan, decided to run for Peters’ seat. Once they passed, the state’s Democratic primary voters could have picked a compromise candidate in Mallory McMorrow, a (now-proven) team player who is more centrist than El-Sayed and more progressive than Stevens. After McMorrow dropped out, Stevens and El-Sayed could have pulled their punches instead of sparring nastily over the Israeli government’s actions in Gaza and Stevens’ support from AIPAC, the pro-Israel lobbying group. And once the primary ended, the pair could have buried the hatchet permanently rather than letting the tensions simmer into the general election. In short, everything in the race has broken toward conflict rather than comity. Michigan wasn’t the only bare-knuckled race in a primary season that served as an extended proxy fight between the Democratic Party’s center-left establishment and its progressive insurgency. The Senate contest in Florida, several House races in New York, and the gubernatorial primary in Wisconsin all got heated. A few other primaries even yielded sore losers; in Texas, Rep. Jasmine Crockett has refused to campaign with fellow Democrat James Talarico after he beat her. Yet even Crockett says she “WOULDN’T, EVER” endorse Talarico’s Republican opponent, so something has clearly gone uniquely sideways in Michigan. What went wrong? Who’s to blame? And is anyone entirely innocent? Stevens is obviously the headline villain, the architect of a hard-to-find-precedent-for defection that is indefensible on both substance and timing. But the difficult reality is that the blame doesn’t rest with her alone. At the heart of this disaster is a spectacularly poor match of political egos, an issue that promotes intransigence over compromise, and, in El-Sayed, a nominee who failed a critical test. Let’s start with the person who’s most at fault for Sunday’s explosion: the one who actually pulled the pin out of the grenade. Despite continuing to describe herself as “a very proud Democrat,” Stevens has arguably done more damage to her party’s nominee than Republicans have. It’s certainly possible that El-Sayed will win, and that Democrats could retake the Senate even if he doesn’t. But winning Michigan in what’s shaping up to be a blue-wave election shouldn’t be this hard, and retaking the Senate without it would require Democrats to vastly overperform in much more Republican-friendly states. But Stevens has betrayed more than just her party: She’d previously argued that a Rogers win would imperil a suite of issues she professed to care about. After losing the primary, she full-throatedly endorsed El-Sayed and argued that Rogers would “keep rubber-stamping Donald Trump’s harmful agenda of higher prices, manufacturing jobs lost nationwide, corruption and abuses of power” while helping “ban abortion, gut health care, and roll back workers’ rights and voting rights.” The only thing that’s changed since then is the candidate Stevens supports. Then there’s the matter of her rationale. Stevens’ defection followed a war of words over a pro-El-Sayed ad, which argued that groceries have become unaffordable because the U.S. sends weapons to Israel. Stevens criticized the ad as antisemitic and demanded El-Sayed condemn it. (More on that in a moment.) But if bigotry were really her concern, she wouldn’t be throwing in with Rogers, a candidate who built his campaign around Islamophobia. Rogers has accused El-Sayed of adhering to a “radical Islamic agenda” and of backing the Muslim Brotherhood, an extremist group the Democrat has repeatedly denounced. Stevens is also, by her own prior admission, helping a president who mocks El-Sayed’s name (“To me, he’s Mohammed, and he’s a radical jihadist,” Trump said last month) and fearmongers that his victory would “invite hordes of jihadis to our shores, creating a nightmare of violence and terror until you cannot even have a Christmas.” On Sunday, Stevens characterized her flip-flop as “a personal and private decision” in the middle of a press conference talking about it, which made the whole thing look more petty than principled. His culpability is more complicated, but it really does take two to tango. Stevens chose to pull her switcheroo, yet El-Sayed has done a bunch of tactically questionable things that seem to have made that outcome more likely—and could make it more politically damaging, too. One of those questionable choices has been to associate himself with controversial figures. During the primary, El-Sayed campaigned with Hasan Piker, a left-wing streamer who has said that the U.S. “deserved 9/11.” El-Sayed spent months refusing to distance himself from Piker, deriding questions about their joint appearances as “gotcha politics.” And although El-Sayed ultimately did condemn those remarks and others (including Piker’s claim that Jewish Americans, or Jewish American institutions, being too pro-Israel could lead people to “take action” against them), some Democrats already had concerns about El-Sayed’s approach to antisemitism. In March, El-Sayed responded to an attack against a Detroit-area synagogue by saying that “hurt people hurt people,” which sounded to some like a justification for the violence. (El-Sayed later apologized.) “I’m just sick of the people he stands with,” Stevens told Anderson Cooper on CNN on Sunday night. Self-defeating prickliness also seems to have shaped El-Sayed’s response to the ad Stevens criticized before endorsing Rogers. It’s true that the ad didn’t come from El-Sayed’s campaign; a left-wing commentator named Cenk Uygur, who happens to be Piker’s uncle, posted it online. But even as he distanced himself, El-Sayed also released a statement accusing Stevens of “relitigating” their primary that pointedly noted her millions in AIPAC support and disinvited Stevens from Sunday’s unity appearance, which national Democrats helped broker in a bid to squash their beef. El-Sayed’s defensiveness, as he runs to be the nation’s first Muslim senator, is understandable. His opponent has put El-Sayed’s religion and ethnicity at the center of his attacks, yet El-Sayed is the one who’s continually asked to disavow bigotry. It must be exhausting. And it’s a challenge for a candidate whose rise was fueled by a combative approach to entrenched power, an approach many of his supporters appreciate. But politics is often unfair and rarely easy, and the job of the candidate is to win. That’s particularly true in an election where Democrats have repeatedly (and accurately) told supporters that their would-be Senate takeover is an essential bulwark against Trump’s authoritarian impulses. In that context, it’s not unfair for Democrats to ask El-Sayed to make sacrifices in the name of winning—and it’s not always clear that he has been willing to. Before the August primary, El-Sayed predicted a double-digit victory against Stevens, only to end up winning by less than a point. A nominee laser-focused on the general election might have been chastened, hastening to court the 47.6 percent of Democrats who’d backed his opponent. Yet even now, polls suggest El-Sayed is leaving votes on the table. He currently leads Rogers by an average of 5 points, while the Democratic nominee for Michigan governor leads her Republican opponent by almost 9. As my colleague Aymann Ismail has reported, El-Sayed has struggled to convince some core Democratic constituencies, including Black Michiganders, that he will improve their lives. Of course, it’s possible that nothing could have kept Stevens inside the tent. Some of her criticisms—like going on CNN to accuse El-Sayed of getting “angrier and angrier”—aren’t things politicians say if they’re open to appeasement. Stevens has also refused to explain her apparently spur-of-the-moment decision to switch sides; as of Saturday, she was still telling fellow Democrats that she supported El-Sayed. Maybe getting engaged to a Democratic donor who also recently pledged to vote for Rogers caused a (literal) change of heart. Maybe Stevens snapped after spending another week butting heads with the rival who foiled her Senate dreams. Or maybe she’s been promised a job after she leaves Congress in January in exchange for changing jerseys. (On Sunday, Trump praised Stevens’ “bravery,” and House Speaker Mike Johnson said he’d encouraged her to “keep speaking the truth.”) The losers of competitive primaries—and their supporters—always face pressure to back their party’s nominee, and lately that pressure has fallen hardest on the left. During the previous three presidential elections, more moderate Democrats urged progressives to shelve their objections and get on board with Hillary Clinton, Joe Biden, and Kamala Harris in the interest of beating Trump. Stevens’ desertion isn’t a great sign that the party’s more centrist losers are willing to return the favor. Still, many moderate Democrats—including erstwhile Stevens supporters, congressional staffers, and House colleagues—have condemned the Rogers endorsement, meaning Stevens’ exit may not bring many voters along. So far, El-Sayed has chosen to downplay it. “This race was never about me. It was never even about Mike Rogers. It’s certainly not about Haley Stevens,” he said on MS NOW on Sunday night. “This race is about the people in the state of Michigan, the people that I’ve gotten to get to know in their churches and synagogues, in their basements, in their living rooms, talking about what they need and deserve.” Yet if there’s any race in which the “vote blue no matter who” calculus could flop, it’s this one. The tension at the heart of the primary—and the feud between El-Sayed and Stevens—is less a policy disagreement than a moral one. Debates over healthcare have a moral valence, but there’s room to compromise between, say, expanding Obamacare and passing “Medicare for All.” By contrast, there isn’t much of a middle ground between thinking Israel is committing genocide in Gaza (as El-Sayed does) and believing that it isn’t (per Stevens). It’s certainly Stevens’ right to decide that her support for Israel outweighs her desire to see a Democrat win her state’s Senate race, just as it’s El-Sayed’s right to say good riddance to a Democrat who feels that way. But part of the job of being a party’s nominee is uniting as many of that party’s supporters behind you as possible. The reasons for El-Sayed’s failure to do that may be a matter of debate, but he will bear the consequences all the same. On Tuesday, it could cost his entire party. The Rio Grande Valley is not like anywhere else in the United States of America. This isn’t to say that there aren’t similarities. The clean subdivisions springing up around McAllen could be in any prosperous suburb in Florida or Arizona. The abject poverty in a rural colonia could be something out of the most dire hollow in Appalachia. But taken as a whole, this stretch of South Texas, starting at the mouth of the Rio Grande and stretching over a hundred miles along its north bank, is just different. It has its own culture, its own music, and its own fast-food chains. Spanish is as commonly heard as English in a region where over 90 percent of residents are Mexican American. But it also is still fundamentally rooted in American culture. Like any other Texans, residents still gather for high school football on Friday night and scarf chicken wings while watching the Dallas Cowboys on Sunday. (In Donna, Texas, the logo of the Donna Redskins is proudly emblazoned on the town water tower, even if it’s considered problematic on the Potomac.) The politics of the Rio Grande Valley are also different. Even as much of the rest of Texas shifted toward the GOP in the past half century, the Rio Grande Valley remained loyal to the Democratic Party. From local offices up to the top of the ticket, it remained a Democratic bastion. At least until Donald Trump appeared on the scene. It wasn’t immediate. Unlike the voters at Rust Belt diners who consumed the national imagination (or at least those of editors on national desks) after the 2016 election, the Rio Grande Valley didn’t really shift to the right in the race between Donald Trump and Hillary Clinton. However, first in 2020 and then in 2024, the valley’s voters veered hard toward the GOP. Perhaps the most stunning political transformation happened in Starr County, a deeply impoverished county that is the most heavily Hispanic jurisdiction in the country. Hillary Clinton won nearly 80 percent of the vote there in 2016. Eight years later, Kamala Harris won the support of just under 42 percent of voters. Republicans acted as if the shift were permanent. They promptly passed a middecade redistricting that was designed to shift five seats in Texas to their column. Their hope was that the lone Republican representing a Rio Grande Valley district, Monica De La Cruz, would soon be joined by two other members of her party, who would unseat longtime Democratic incumbents Henry Cuellar and Vicente Gonzalez. It turned out that they were overambitious. It’s not just continued economic woes that have plagued Republicans across the country this cycle. It’s a fundamental miscalculation by the Trump administration on its signature immigration policy. Just because voters were fed up with Joe Biden’s lax border policy, it didn’t mean they were willing to embrace the brutal and indiscriminate tactics of Border Patrol commander Greg Bovino either. For many of these voters, 2024 was a rejection of a Democratic Party that was interested in catering more to white Brooklyn progressives than to Hispanics on the border in Progreso, Texas. But it turns out they are not particularly happy with the Republican Party of 2026 either. The resulting backlash could not just cost the GOP a plethora of races in Texas; it could arrest, or reverse, a nascent political realignment in the Rio Grande Valley. Three weeks before Election Day, Speaker Mike Johnson came to McAllen, Texas, to rally Republican faithful in a hotel ballroom only a few miles from the Mexican border. There was an open bar with beer and wine, chicken wings, and burgers for the crowd of over 100. They’d come to see Johnson appear with De La Cruz and two Republican challengers: Tano Tijerina, a much-vaunted recruit running against Cuellar, and Eric Flores, the GOP hope for unseating Gonzalez. On the way in, it was hard to avoid free swag from the De La Cruz campaign, which gave out baseball hats, T-shirts, and pens. The crowd appeared to be roughly evenly divided between Anglos and Hispanics in an area where Hispanics make up an overwhelming majority of the population. And the message they heard was overwhelmingly clear: The polls were all wrong. Johnson took the stage, telling the crowd, “The polls say that these guys aren’t going to win. That’s not true, is it?,” to loud noes from the loyal Republicans in the room. He added, “Do not believe the polls right now. I’m telling you, they’re wrong. They’re wrong.” This was weak tea compared with De La Cruz, who told the hollering crowd, “I don’t care what the polls say, because my God is bigger.” The next day, congressional Republicans pulled their funding for Tijerina, effectively giving up on the race against Cuellar. Republicans are struggling in most races this year across the country, but what is happening in the Rio Grande Valley is, like the region itself, unique. It’s also exemplified by the Democratic standard-bearer against De La Cruz, Bobby Pulido. A 53-year-old Tejano singer who had a number of hits in the genre in the 1990s, Pulido was one of the most prized recruits for Democrats this year. He’s running in a district that Donald Trump won by 18 points in 2024 and has since been further gerrymandered to stretch from the border over 250 miles north into the rural areas between Houston and San Antonio. However, the bulk of its population is in the Rio Grande Valley, and both candidates live there. Pulido’s celebrity gives him instant universal name ID in the district. It also has been the basis for much of his campaign. After De La Cruz gibed that the race “isn’t about who you want performing at your niece’s quinceañera,” the Democrat said he was open to playing anyone’s quinceañera in the district. Thousands of invitations rolled in for a singer whose hit song “Desvelado” can still be sung by generations of Tejanos in the Rio Grande Valley. However, there is a dark side to the celebrity. De La Cruz has repeatedly attacked Pulido for problematic social media posts across his career, as well as his association with Frankie Caballero, the singer’s former accordionist, who is a registered sex offender for indecent contact with a minor. This has been one of the centerpieces of her campaign. After all, who could ever imagine anyone in the Rio Grande Valley supporting a problematic celebrity? De La Cruz’s campaign office is a former restaurant space on a state highway in Weslaco, a sprawling town just east of McAllen. The entrance of the dingy space includes a display of “Faces of South Texas,” which includes Selena, Nolan Ryan, Tom Landry, and some war heroes. She’s having an event for voters to pick up campaign signs, and about 50 people have crowded in to see their congresswoman and pick up her swag at 5:30 on a Thursday afternoon. Burgers are available, with accompanying plastic bags that include lettuce, tomatoes, and pickles, to allow attendees to individually dress their sandwiches. There are coolers of generic cola and name-brand beer. The older crowd avails themselves mostly of the first rather than the second. Next to the congresswoman, the star attendee is a student at Sam Houston Elementary School who dressed up for school as her role model, who happened to be Monica De La Cruz. Dressed in a white blazer and red skirt, she recites De La Cruz’s biography as the real politician looks on with a glowing smile. When the student is done, De La Cruz hugs her and presents her with an American-flag pin and a personalized Monica De La Cruz challenge coin. Before the attendees grabbed their signs, which included standard yard signs, as well as pink-shaded Spanish-language signs saying, “Mujeres Para Monica,” they could stand in a line for a picture with the congresswoman. Bruce Springsteen’s “Atlantic City” blared in the background. The room appeared to be entirely Hispanic. It included an older woman who spoke broken English but made clear her disdain for immigrants who lack permanent legal status. Also present were Luis Rosa and Luisa Collin, a couple who praised De La Cruz for her constituent services. Rosa had been a Republican and had brought over Collin, who had previously described herself as “a Democrat by tradition” and now was a fervent conservative listening to right-wing podcasts. They both were loyal Trump supporters. “We love him,” said Collin. De La Cruz, though, needs to appeal to more than Trump die-hards to win reelection. Speaking to Slate in her office the day before, she often deflected criticism of administration policies on key issues—noting that she raised concern with key stakeholders. “I had those tough conversations with the administration,” she said about Trump’s immigration policies. “ICE enforcement should focus on the criminals and on the cartels, not hardworking immigrant families trying to navigate this extremely complex immigration process.” However, she acknowledged, “the reality is, the White House is the one that dictates enforcement.” She also touted various bills for immigration reform that she had introduced and backed in the House, even though none have received, or will receive, a vote in a Republican-controlled Congress. The incumbent waved off criticism that she had not backed a discharge petition on an immigration reform bill that was one signature away from forcing a floor vote on Capitol Hill. “Never in the history of America has true immigration reform been done through a discharge petition,” she said. (A discharge position famously resulted in a vote on the release of the Epstein files earlier this year.) It was the same on Trump’s trade policy, which is particularly resonant in a district on the border. “It’s a discussion that I’m a part of with the trade ambassador, Ambassador Greer, and the White House. 'Weight-Space Programming' fiddles with nerd knobs to boost benchmark scores The outputs of AI models are the result of a complex interplay between neurons, weights, training, and inference, and are therefore difficult to follow. But in some ways they're starting to look more like open books. Recent work by Anthropic safety fellow Joshua Fonseca Rivera has revealed how certain neurons in a neural network can be associated with a specific persona. Now, computer scientists from MIT's Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory (CSAIL) have found that frontier AI models like OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra can rewrite the weights of another model directly, as opposed to indirectly through training, and alter their behavior. "We find early signs that frontier LLMs are surprisingly good at natively reading, writing, and reasoning about neural network weights," said Rikiya Takehi, a PhD student at MIT CSAIL, in a social media post. "Instead of training, models may treat raw weights like readable, editable code: diagnosing failures and patching them directly." To demonstrate that, Takehi and colleagues Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang, and Omar Khattab asked GPT-6 Astra to edit Qwen3.5-0.8B weights to make it better at math. Astra changed only 0.016 percent of the model's weights, reducing its reasoning loops and improving its math performance by as much as sixfold on the MATH-500 benchmark, while boosting its instruction-following score on the IFEval benchmark roughly threefold. There was no training using gradient descent; Astra just revised the numerical values that constitute Qwen's weights. "All of this is possible without allowing Astra to write training loops," the researchers wrote in a blog post. "It uses its reasoning to figure out what each part of a neural network computes, or should compute, and changes the weights accordingly. We call this Weight-Space Programming. "Throughout the history of deep learning, we encoded our simplistic inductive biases into tiny symbolic optimizers like Adam or Muon. OpenVDDK is reportedly already being used in production, at scale A cloud architect has created an open-source alternative to VMware’s Virtual Disk Development Kit (VDDK), which the virtualization giant recently withdrew from public downloads. VMware describes VDDK as “a set of libraries and utilities that enable software developers to create applications that can access and manipulate virtual disks” and “a valuable tool for developers of storage and backup applications.” Users, along with VMware’s rivals, had another use for VDDK: migrating VMs to alternative virtualization platforms. In August, VMware restricted access to VDDK downloads to its partners, meaning VMware customers couldn’t access the tool and some of its rivals’ migration tools became less effective. Cloud architect Andrija Panić decided to do something about it. “Many tools that have nothing in common except VMware use the same small library to read a virtual machine’s disks: virt-v2v and nbdkit, the importers in Forklift/MTV, Proxmox VE and Apache CloudStack, and countless in-house scripts,” he told The Register by email. “When public VDDK downloads ended, all of them became harder to install and run.” “I didn’t think it was fair that people’s ability to move or protect their own data should depend on a download page.” He describes the project as “an independent drop-in for libvixDiskLib.so.8.0.3, the disk library that VMware backup and migration tools load to read virtual disks. It was written from scratch, using only public documentation and black-box testing, and contains no VMware code.” Panić told The Register he wrote the tool in Go, with a thin C layer that exposes exactly the same C interface, library name and symbol versions as VMware’s code. The tool cannot write disks, just read them for backup and migration purposes. In his email to The Register, Panić said he has successfully tested OpenVDDK on vSphere versions 6.7 U3, 7.0.3, 8.0.3, 9.1.1. He said that tests in his lab with the latter version, accessing OpenVDDK through vCenter on a standalone ESXi host, saw every disk read match. “Independent testers have reported successful use with Forklift (VMware to KubeVirt, cold and warm), MTV on OpenShift (cold and warm), a Proxmox VE pipeline and custom nbdkit-based tools,” the architect told The Register. “I've received reports of production use, including a German cloud provider migrating a large file server with V2K Migrate and a US-based company migrating to Apache CloudStack/KVM.” Panić was at pains to point out that the reports of successful use are “not independently audited customer endorsements or a guarantee of suitability for every production environment.” As is often the case with FOSS tools. With per-token pricing, the workflows creating the most value can also become the most expensive. The financial pressure is already visible. Uber reportedly exhausted its entire 2026 AI budget by April after expanding its use of Claude Code. GitHub Copilot moved from flat-rate pricing to metered credits. Gartner predicts that AI coding token costs will surpass average developer salaries by 2028. The natural response is rationing usage caps, seat limits, and approvals before developers launch agents. But blanket limits can restrict the workflows delivering value. They also optimize the wrong number. Cost per token measures consumption, not results. Some spending reflects waste, including retries and runaway loops. Some represents the work of shipping software. A meter cannot distinguish them, and neither can a spending cap. A better measure is cost per merged pull request: total AI-assisted development cost divided by the number of changes that pass review and are merged. Track rework and defects alongside it so cheaper changes do not conceal lower quality. If that cost falls as AI usage rises, without compromising quality, developers are converting more consumption into useful software. The question is not how many tokens they use, but how much finished work the investment produces. Improving that equation requires two changes: reserve expensive inference for tasks that need it, and lower the cost of routine work. Agentic coding does not require the most powerful model at every step. Use a frontier model, or an on-premises flagship when policy requires it, for difficult planning and novel reasoning. Route suitable work, including code generation, test creation, and repeated refactoring, to open-weight models you operate yourself. A gateway can route requests according to task complexity, data sensitivity, cost, and latency. This reserves premium inference for the moments that justify it rather than applying premium pricing to an entire workflow. Meter the exception, not the workload. Open-weight models make this division increasingly practical. Software-engineering benchmarks show that leading open-weight models can approach frontier performance on some tasks. That does not establish equivalence across every repository or workflow. Evaluate candidates against your own code, tests, and acceptance criteria before assigning production work. Next, decide who owns the capacity. With rented inference, every call adds to the bill. With owned capacity, most of the cost is committed upfront, so each additional call gets cheaper as usage grows. Owned capacity also delivers a type of value that never appears on an invoice: control over sensitive code. When code generation and testing run on-premises, proprietary code stays inside the enterprise. Only approved planning prompts and governed exceptions go to external models, through a gateway the organization controls. In 2001, a decade after he invented the World Wide Web, Tim Berners-Lee articulated a new vision. Together with two colleagues, he published an article in the magazine Scientific American in which he outlined what he called the “Semantic Web”: an internet designed to be navigated by autonomous software agents that could address our every need, without requiring us to muck about with browsers and search engines ourselves. To illustrate what this might look like, Berners-Lee and his colleagues told a story about a pair of siblings who use their agents to automatically research and book physical therapy appointments for their mother, making sure they would be covered by insurance and fit their busy calendars. At the turn of the millennium, this vision seemed far-fetched: Google had only been online for a couple of years, and most people were still on dial-up connections. But a quarter-century on, the dream of autonomous software agents that use the internet and carry out tasks for us has finally been realized. New tools such as Meta’s blockbuster Muse agent, OpenAI’s Dots, and Anthropic’s Claude Duet are being given access to people’s email inboxes and bank accounts,  Meanwhile, in a series of worrying incidents, agents created by OpenAI broke free of their sandboxes and caused havoc online. Just as the rise of smartphones sparked the development of a more atomized and attention-grabbing internet, the advent of powerful AI systems heralds a new era: We’re facing not the semantic web but the agentic web. This new web is developing out of sight and in ways that might not be entirely within our control. As it grows, the agentic web will inevitably transform the human web—and whether the human web survives this transition is anyone’s guess. For Berners-Lee’s original vision to work, internet users would have had to annotate the pages they built with special tags so that a software agent could understand each element’s meaning—say, “street address” for the part of a physical therapy office’s web page that indicates its location and “appointment types” for the list of services. And so the idea never really caught on. “It’s a lot of labor,” says Eni Mustafaraj, a professor of computer science at Wellesley College. “And the benefits of that were not obvious.” For the next two decades, then, the internet remained a place that put humans first, with the odd Twitter reply bot or web scraper to round things out. Large language models changed that. Unlike the agents that Berners-Lee was imagining, today’s LLM-based agents don’t need any tags to understand which part of the text on a clinic’s site is its address and which details the services it offers. They can search for and process text far faster than any human, and agents with multimodal capabilities can even interpret screenshots of websites and click on specific elements such as hyperlinks and drop-down menus. They’re also just a way more intuitive means of finding information and accomplishing tasks online. Rather than poring over pages of Google results to find a budget-friendly product dupe or a half-remembered meme, why not have Claude or ChatGPT peruse dozens of sites in just a few minutes, guided only by your vague query? Or go one step further and use a personal assistant agent like Muse to hunt down a restaurant reservation or book a flight. As agents flood online spaces, this new user base is changing the web for good. Time magazine, for instance, is publishing versions of its articles that are easier for agents to parse and has started selling ads targeted at them. Companies from Etsy to Expedia have partnered with the major AI firms to build tools that allow agents to interface seamlessly with their services. Despite these early efforts, it’s still tough to tell exactly what forms the agentic web will take and how it will change our online experience. Depending on how it’s designed and who controls access to it, we could be in for something that’s as open and customizable as the web of the early 2000s, only far easier to use. Alternatively, we could be reduced to viewing much of the web through tiny portholes controlled by a few dominant platforms, as independent sites and creators struggle to survive the shift. And as this summer’s series of OpenAI security incidents suggests, the agents might even build their own online networks and resources—whether we want them to or not. How this plays out also depends on us: which parts of our online lives we’re happy to delegate to our agents and where we will still demand a human-centric internet. “There’s a lot we don’t know yet about how users want to use [agents],” says Ajit Varma, head of Firefox at Mozilla. But whatever does happen, the changes will be big. “It’s probably on par with getting a mobile phone,” he says. “I think things are going to change very dramatically.” There are two ways to approach building the agentic web. The first involves training agents to use browsers like humans—scrolling, clicking, typing—and letting them loose on the web that we’re all used to. The second requires building tools that allow agents to interface with the internet via text and code, their preferred media. That possibility offers more separation between the human and the agentic web: designers can create different experiences for those who use text commands and those who use browsers. When Siva Reddy, a professor of computer science and linguistics at McGill University, began studying LLM agents in 2024, he focused on the first option. “At that time, we thought it should be that the best agent in the world is probably an agent that can use a browser,” he says. Such an agent would integrate seamlessly with the preexisting human web, and its owner could easily supervise it by watching as it navigated familiar interfaces. The frontier AI companies were already developing these “computer-use agents.” But they didn’t work terribly well—at least not at first. “There’s a lot of moving pieces [on the web], and they are not really optimized for automatic consumption,” says Xing Han Lu, a graduate student in Reddy’s lab at McGill. We humans are naturals at interacting with graphical interfaces using a keyboard and mouse, but agents are not. So Reddy and Lu pivoted toward the second, tool-based approach. In the summer of 2025, they expounded on this view in a preprint paper envisioning a new kind of web interface that would allow agents to interact with websites just as effectively as we do. Such a full-featured version of the agentic web doesn’t yet exist, but it is emerging in smaller ways. For example, if you open up Claude, ChatGPT, or Muse, you’ll find an option to add a wide variety of “plug-ins” or “connectors” that your agents can use: Gmail connectors allow your agent to triage your inbox, and Instacart plug-ins let your agent do your grocery shopping. Under the hood, these plug-ins allow your agent to interact with the back ends of websites using text commands. When human engineers design these plug-ins, they can decide how they want to allow agents to interact with the website—and what they don’t want to let them do. In this way, connectors can help make agents much safer online. For instance, Muse can buy products for you using its connector with Stripe’s Link payment service. Stripe knows your credit card number, but the agent doesn’t see it, and the connector requires human confirmation before a purchase goes through—so Muse can’t go on an unlicensed shopping spree. Agents also use specialized tools to find information online. They don’t type questions directly into the Google search bar; in fact, they often don’t even access the websites they reference. Instead, they query text databases collected by simple search crawler bots. People who want to make web content specifically for agents can take advantage of these crawlers: Time has pioneered that approach by serving its agent-oriented ads to crawlers in the hopes that they will be incorporated into these databases and surface when agents search for information. But in recent months, an alternative to this bespoke tool approach has finally started to seem viable. The major AI companies have managed to train much more successful computer-use agents than were previously available. GPT-6 Astra, which OpenAI released last September, achieved a 73% success rate on OSWorld 2.0, a benchmark that tests computer-use agents on their ability to complete complex, multistep tasks using software such as Slack and Salesforce. Such results indicate that agents are close to being able to navigate the human web just as readily as we can. “We thought we had to build a bridge to the AI agents, [but] the AI agents just were able to cross the bridge already,” says Ethan Mollick, a professor at the Wharton School at the University of Pennsylvania. “The agentic web is here earlier than people expected.” This version of the agentic web, though, is much trickier for us to steer. If you want to make a capability or piece of information available to humans who browse your website, you need to accept that it will also be available to agents—unless you want to block agents entirely, which could come with its own costs. As agents come to account for a larger portion of traffic on our formerly human-dominated web, designers will undoubtedly start to consider an agentic as well as a human audience. But according to Mollick, we still don’t have much sense of how to design web content for agents. He’s experienced this firsthand: When he was building a web page for his new book, Co-Existence, he decided to put together an agent-directed version that tells agents why they might want to recommend the book to their users. When newer models see the site, Mollick says, they don’t recommend the book; instead, they comment that the website seems to be trying to manipulate them. In the end, any human-designed approach to building the agentic web could end up being beside the point: Agents might end up devising a web for themselves. In the wake of the July 2026 incident in which OpenAI agents broke out of their testing sandbox and hacked Hugging Face’s website, researchers found evidence that another swarm of agents from OpenAI had used an obscure German wiki to communicate with each other about tasks they were being asked to complete. The agents seem to have interfaced with the wiki in their native language of text and code, and that hardly held them back from putting it to their own purposes. As agents continue to grow more powerful, they will become more and more capable of constructing their own spaces on the internet—away from prying human eyes, if they so wish. And we might have very little control over what they do there. Not all of the traditional web’s incumbents are happy about the move toward an agentic web. Soon after Meta released Muse in September, Amazon completely blocked the agent from its site. This makes sense for Amazon: The firm has its own shopping agent—called, creatively, Alexa for Shopping—that might struggle to compete with better-known products such as Claude and Muse. If people are going to use agents to shop on Amazon, then it’s better for the company’s bottom line if they do so using an agent it can steer toward products it wants to promote. In an email to MIT Technology Review, an Amazon spokesperson noted that the company did not authorize Muse to access its website, and that Muse compromises Amazon’s ability to personalize the shopping experience for each customer. But even if Alexa for Shopping never goes anywhere, banning agents from Amazon—at least for the time being—could still be a reasonable move. Everything about Amazon is designed for human eyes, from the Prime deals splashed across its front page to the sponsored listings that clog up search results. Amazon is very much a part of the much-derided attention economy. Websites and platforms survive, and thrive, by grabbing our focus and transforming it into cash. A radical transformation of the web’s user base from majority human to majority artificial undermines this economic logic, and it seems inevitable that businesses will shutter as they try to transition to the agentic attention economy. Some of the first victims may be in publishing. Before Google publicly released its AI Overview feature in May of 2024, people used to click through to websites to find answers to specific questions, but now many seem content with whatever the overview tells them. A study from Pew last year found that people are about 40% less likely to click through to websites from the Google search results page if one of these summaries is displayed. With far fewer clicks, news publishers don’t make as much money from the advertisements they host and sell fewer subscriptions. One analysis found that publishers saw revenue from some types of ads decrease by about a third between 2025 and 2026. It’s easy to hate online ads, but the businesses paying for access to reader eyeballs also keep writers and photographers fed. If those eyeballs become unreachable, the system stops working. Time has apparently persuaded some businesses to pay to participate in its experiment with agent-targeted ads, but no one yet knows whether those ads will influence agent behavior enough to be a sensible marketing investment. E-commerce websites might also try to pivot to the agentic attention economy, but the path forward there is unclear. Last year, a team led by Akshit Kumar, a professor of operations management at the University of Toronto Scarborough, investigated whether and how agents are influenced by the ways that shopping websites present search results. They found that agents are less likely to recommend items that come with a “sponsored” tag, which suggests that selling favorable positions on results pages might not be a great way for sites to make money if agents become the primary way that people shop online.
