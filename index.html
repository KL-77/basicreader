A 1,000-mile cloud on Mars may reveal physics never seen in nature

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-24

Visit Original Website
Every morning during the Martian spring and summer, a thin and extremely long cloud emerges, only to disappear again. The wispy formation appears near the volcano Arsia Mons and gradually elongates until it reaches over 1,000 miles—about the distance from New York to Kansas City as the crow flies—before dissipating. This cycle then repeats for several months. Since it was first recorded by a probe in 2018, scientists have been unable to find a plausible explanation for the existence of this cloud, often referred to as the Arsia Mons Elongated Cloud (AMEC). Until now. Recently, researchers successfully modeled the cloud’s formation for the first time, their results suggesting that it may be driven by a physical process that has never been directly observed in any planet’s atmosphere. “We found that we needed to include some exotic physics… physics that, while included in textbooks, is treated as theoretical and usually thought not to happen in nature. It certainly hasn’t been seen in action before,” Jorge Hernández-Bernal, a planetary scientist at Paris’ Sorbonne Université and lead author of the study, said in a press release. “Once we included this physics in our simulations, the AMEC emerged just as we hoped.” On Earth, clouds usually form when water vapor creates droplets or ice crystals in the presence of tiny particles, such as dust or salt. The new study, published in Nature Geoscience, instead suggests AMEC could be forming through so-called homogeneous nucleation, where vapor changes directly into ice crystals on its own without any help from debris. “It’s akin to droplets of condensation appearing in the middle of a room, rather than on a window,” Hernández-Bernal explained. “It’s wholly unexpected.” This phenomenon requires extreme conditions—a sharp temperature drop of 30° Celsius within 10 minutes and high relative humidity. The Arsia Mons volcano also plays a key role. When wind current hits the 65,000-foot-tall volcano, it creates a powerful wave that launches moist air high in the atmosphere in a matter of minutes. Scientists have assumed a similar phenomenon could occur in the upper atmospheres of other planets, including Venus and even Earth, but it has never been observed. That’s not too surprising given the extreme air humidity required for this phenomenon, which is more than 100,000 times greater than any value measured on Earth. Before this modeling, it was not known that these humidity levels existed on Mars either. “We don’t have nearly as much information about Mars’ atmosphere as we do about Earth’s, so reproducing the AMEC to this degree is a big success,” said Hernández-Bernal, adding, “the result is remarkable.” This story originally appeared on WIRED Czech Republic and Slovakia and has been translated from Czech.

Ice sheet expansion may have started 2,000 years ago, but we canceled that

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-24

Visit Original Website
Barnes Ice Cap sits on the central plateau of Baffin Island, in the Canadian Arctic. It is the last surviving fragment of the Laurentide Ice Sheet, the largest of the Pleistocene ice sheets, which at its peak buried most of Canada under two miles of ice and locked away around 80 meters of sea level. That remnant has now yielded direct physical evidence that ice sheets might have reversed their retreat and started to grow again thousands of years ago. In a recent Science study, researchers show that the Laurentide reached its smallest size about 3,600 years ago, then started re-expanding roughly 2,000 years ago, which the team interprets as the opening move of the next glaciation. Apparently, this brand-new ice age was on its way until about 1960, when human-caused climate warming reversed it. “Barnes is the last remaining piece of one of the domes of the Laurentide Ice Sheet,” says Nicolás Young, a geologist at Columbia University’s Lamont-Doherty Earth Observatory and lead author of the study. As that enormous dome thinned through the Holocene, its margin withdrew up onto Baffin Island, and what’s left today is the deflated core of something that was once two miles thick. The reason Barnes can testify about its own history, Young explains, comes down to the temperature at its base. Most glaciers are warm-based, meaning meltwater at the bed lets them slide, and sliding ice grinds up everything beneath it, erasing the record. Barnes is cold-based, and thus frozen directly onto the bedrock beneath it, which prevents it from sliding. “If there is an ice event where you live and you have to go and scrape off your windshield, that ice that’s plastered to the windshield of your car, that’s a good example of how cold-based ice operates. It’s sort of just stuck to what it’s on,” Young says. This stuck ice preserves what’s beneath instead of eroding it. As the Laurentide retreated across the plateau, tundra plants colonized the newly bare ground. When the ice came back later, it simply smothered them in place and held them there, intact, for two millennia. To pinpoint the exact moment of this turnaround, Young’s team used two independent dating methods. The first method measures beryllium-10, an isotope that accumulates in the top few centimeters of rock that is exposed to the sky. By sampling a boulder’s upper surface and counting the beryllium, researchers could learn how long it has been uncovered. That technique put a date on the ice sheet’s retreat. It turned out the Laurentide had withdrawn to within today’s ice margin by 3,600 years ago, probably shrinking further by around 3,000. The second dating method looked at the dead plants. “There were all these plants growing, just like today. And included in that are mosses, these little mosses that are rooted in the ground,” Young says. “So they’re there, they’re living their life, and eventually the Laurentide Ice Sheet starts to re-expand. And when that ice margin covers the moss, it literally kills it.” By radiocarbon dating the remnants of the moss, the team could pinpoint the moment it died, yielding what glaciologists call a kill date—the moment the ice arrived at that spot. By stringing multiple kill dates of moss samples taken from different spots, scientists reconstructed a map of an advancing ice margin. About 70 percent of the team’s radiocarbon dates cluster around 1800 CE, at the culmination of the Little Ice Age. “It’s all sort of the same thing,” he says. The Little Ice Age marks the point where Northern Hemisphere glaciers were at their most extensive in recent millennia. At Barnes, Young argues, it was just one late pulse within an advance that had been running for two thousand years. The most important plants for Young’s team, however, are still hidden under ice. Barnes’ margin has retreated 200 to 800 meters since 1960, exposing mosses killed from about 1000 CE onward. Anything older remains buried. To pinpoint the exact moment the ice started advancing, the team had to look at Barnes’ neighbors. Scattered across the same plateau are other small ice caps, close enough to share the same climate. Earlier studies collected nearly 200 kill dates from beneath them—not one is older than 2,000 years. “What that means is that all of those ice caps up there didn’t start advancing or re-expanding until about 2,000 years ago,” Young says. “Since the Barnes Ice Cap is right next door, it’s probably a good bet that it also started expanding 2,000 years ago.” Young argues that continued retreat of the Barnes Ice Cap should eventually reveal the decisive samples. “If we went back there in 20 years or something, as the ice margin has retreated a little more, we actually might be able to sample those 2,000-year-old plants,” he says. According to the study, the ice started re-expanding at this precise moment because two global forces were pulling it in opposite directions, and one of them eventually won. Solar energy reaching high northern latitudes peaked about 10,000 to 11,000 years ago and has declined ever since, pushing the planet toward the next ice age. Meanwhile, atmospheric carbon dioxide crept up by roughly 20 parts per million between 8,000 and 2,000 years ago, increasing the greenhouse effect that was heating the atmosphere up. “That drop in insolation and temperatures actually overwhelmed the small temperature forcing the little bit of carbon dioxide rise over the same time span,” Young explains. Eventually, 2,000 years ago, temperatures dropped far enough due to lower sunlight in the high latitudes, and the effects of carbon dioxide weren’t high enough, so ice sheets started to re-expand. This happened despite the fact that calculations of orbital forcings, together with modeling of how carbon dioxide interferes with glacial inception, have suggested the next ice age was postponed by tens of thousands of years. Young argues there’s really no contradiction between these models and the evidence his team collected in the field. “A lot of people have defined glacial inception as glaciers and ice sheets needing to be of a certain size—big enough that we’ll say, ah, yes, we are into the next ice age,” Young says. “And that’s perfectly reasonable, because until now there’s been no way to actually pinpoint when an ice sheet first turns around and starts to expand.” But a few geological moments after the Laurentide sheet started re-expanding, humanity entered the chat. Greenhouse gases had risen by about 12 ppm between 1750 and 1850, another 50 ppm by 1960, and a further 138 ppm by 2024. “Once we hit the Industrial Revolution, we’re now pumping way more carbon dioxide at a magnitude that has never occurred in the geologic record,” Young says. By 1960, Barnes’ mass balance went negative and its advance ended. Today, Barnes Ice Cap is dying. In the 1960s and ’70s, roughly 35 percent of its surface sat above the area’s snow line. By the early 2000s, about 3 percent did. Today, none of it does. “This means in the summer, it’s all melting. There’s no more accumulation zone. There’s no more new ice forming,” Young says. “An ice cap or glacier cannot survive if it does not have an accumulation zone.” According to his models, under any plausible emissions scenario, Barnes will disappear within a few centuries. So did we actually dodge the bullet by warming the climate so quickly? “Frankly, in modern times, no one wants to live in an ice age climate. Things would be quite hard and quite different,” Young says. He argues, though, that we did not exactly do ourselves a favor here. To get to a true, full ice age climate with big ice sheets, it would have taken several tens of thousands of years. “I would argue that the pumping of greenhouse gases into the atmosphere hasn’t really saved us from anything,” Young claims. What we have done instead is interrupt a cycle that was very old and very regular. “The pacing of ice ages over the last 2.5 million years has been one of the most consistent cycles in the Earth system that we have,” Young says. “We have evidence now that humans, and specifically greenhouse gases, have actually terminated one of the most consistent cycles on Earth.” The study is published in Science: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aed3379

NASA updates lander plans for Moon landings, finally solves long-festering Orion issue

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-23

Visit Original Website
During a lengthy news conference on Friday, NASA provided a detailed overview of how it plans to return astronauts to the lunar surface as soon as 2028 as part of its Artemis Program. Although much of the information behind these plans has been previously reported at Ars Technica and elsewhere, this marked the first time NASA has laid them out in detail and acknowledged some substantial changes in the architecture. Most notably, for SpaceX’s Starship vehicle, the revised plan calls for the Orion spacecraft to dock with the Starship lunar lander in high-Earth orbit before transiting to the Moon. And for Blue Origin, the mission architecture includes four launches of the New Glenn rocket, but no spacecraft-to-spacecraft refuelings. Additionally, the Blue Origin plans allows for the possibility of its Blue Moon lander rendezvousing with Orion in low-Lunar orbit. During the presentation on Friday, NASA officials said they continue to anticipate launching the Artemis III mission—a demonstration involving docking with both Blue Moon and Starship near Earth—by “summer” 2027. And they are still tracking toward a 2028 launch of Artemis IV, which would involve an actual landing on the lunar surface. “This is an achievable path we are on right now,” NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said. “We have the mandate, we have the resources, and the support from our stakeholders to be able to execute, and that’s what we intend to do.” It is commendable that Isaacman and his leadership have taken a hard look every element of the Artemis Program and sought to make changes where they can help the lunar lander companies execute and accelerate their plans. But can they hold to those dates? The biggest ask SpaceX engineers had in meetings with NASA in early 2026 was removing the requirement to dock with Orion in a near-rectilinear halo orbit around the Moon. This required more energy and had other difficulties associated with it. When NASA canceled the Lunar Gateway earlier this year, it solved this problem for SpaceX. Now SpaceX will fuel a Starship lunar lander in low-Earth orbit before docking with Orion in high-Earth orbit. The crew will fly to the Moon inside Orion before two of four astronauts transfer into Starship for a landing on the Moon. A key question surrounding SpaceX’s architecture is the number of refuelings required to top off a Starship for a lunar mission. On Thursday a NASA Safety Panel reported that the current number of tanker launches is “about 22.” SpaceX founder Elon Musk said that estimate was wrong, and that the actual number will be six to eight. During a question-and-answer session on Friday afternoon, NASA officials declined to put a precise number on the refueling launches required. A lot of uncertainty remains in the lift capacity of Starship, as well as in how much cryogenic methane and liquid oxygen will boil off in orbit and how efficient the process of transferring propellants from one ship to another will be. However, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said this of the estimate of 6 to 8 launches proffered by Musk: it was possible, but represented the “ultimate potential end state” of Starship. In response to NASA’s call for a faster approach to reaching the Moon, Blue Origin has modified its plans such that it would no longer require refueling between vehicles for initial missions. Under the concept of operations released Friday, a New Glenn rocket (this could be an expendable version of the smaller 7×2 variant of New Glenn, or a reusable 9×4 rocket) would launch three “transfer stages” to low-Earth orbit. These transfer stages are modeled after the Blue Moon Mark 1 lander, but have some significant modifications. Then New Glenn would launch the Blue Moon Mark 2 lander into orbit, where it would rendezvous with the transfer stages that will push the stack to the Moon. This is not new information, as Ars first reported this architecture in March. However, NASA did reveal a potentially significant change on Friday. The chart shared by NASA showed Orion docking with the Blue Moon lander in an elliptical lunar orbit, approximately 8,500 km above the Moon’s surface. However NASA is also working to have the option of docking with Orion in low-Lunar orbit. According to Jeremy Parsons, the manager of the Artemis Program for NASA, such an orbit a few hundred km above the Moon would significantly reduce the propellant needed by Blue Moon to bring astronauts down to the lunar surface and back up. This mass could be used elsewhere on the vehicle. The lower orbit would also offer more, and faster opportunities for astronauts to get off the lunar surface if needed. In short, it makes some aspects of a lunar landing significantly easier for Blue Origin. As part of this, NASA announced Friday that it is working with United Launch Alliance to develop an “extended” version of the Centaur V upper stage. In concert with the core stage of the Space Launch System, this upper stage would allow Orion to reach low-lunar orbit. This is a big deal. It has long been a point of embarrassment for NASA that Orion, with its low-performing Service Module, does not have the delta-V capability to launch from Earth, reach low-lunar orbit, and return to Earth for splashdown in the Pacific Ocean. But no one had seemed prepared to fix this problem before. “Getting Orion to low-Lunar orbit means solving propulsion and spacecraft integration problems that were left unaddressed for far too long,” Isaacman said Friday. Instead, NASA created workarounds like the Lunar Gateway, which was put in a near-rectilinear halo orbit that Orion could safely reach. The burden of performance to reach the lunar surface was pushed on to the lunar landers and the companies developing them. Given the urgency of the situation, NASA has been trying to help lunar lander companies in any way they can, Isaacman said. “We’re listening to the data, and looking at what’s happening around us,” he said. “What progress are our landing partners making? Where are they struggling? What can we do to be helpful? Their lives get easier if we can get Orion closer to the Moon, substantially. We could choose to continue on our track and say, maybe they’ll show up and meet our timelines. Or we can say well let’s start turning those knobs to help.”

Hegseth warns prediction market bets warp military judgment, endanger troops

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-23

Visit Original Website
Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth issued a memo warning military personnel against using nonpublic information to make bets on prediction markets, saying the wagers could give important clues to enemy countries. “Do Not Compromise Military Operations for Personal Financial Gain,” said the subject line of an October 20 memo published yesterday by CBS News. Hegseth’s memo was sent to senior Pentagon leadership, Combatant Command leaders, and Defense Department field activity directors. Our enemies watch movements in these markets. When an American Service member, intelligence professional, or other Department civilian engages in a financial transaction on a military outcome, they create a signal. That signal can be aggregated, correlated, and exploited by hostile foreign intelligence services to anticipate our movements, targets, and timing. The lives of our Service members often depend on the enemy not knowing what is coming. The men and women who carry our flag into harm’s way deserve a Force that protects them—not one that exposes them to the enemy for personal gain. In April, a US Army soldier was arrested and charged with using confidential information to make $410,000 in Polymarket bets on the timing of the military’s capture of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro. An August report by the Anti-Corruption Data Collective said the group found 152 suspicious wallets that placed longshot bets on military and defense markets and won a combined $8 million. Hegseth’s memo said the prohibition applies to “all financial platforms,” including traditional betting services and prediction markets such as Polymarket and Kalshi. Defense Department personnel may not make any financial transaction informed by nonpublic information, regardless of whether that information is classified or unclassified, Hegseth wrote. “All Department of War personnel are prohibited from engaging in financial transactions using nonpublic information, either directly or indirectly through a third party… Violations may result in administrative, disciplinary, and, where appropriate, criminal action under the Uniform Code of Military Justice and/or applicable federal law,” Hegseth wrote. The memo said betting on military matters related to a person’s job can warp one’s judgment. “Beyond operational security, financial stakes can warp judgment,” he wrote. “A logistician with money riding on a strike date has an interest in that date. An analyst with a position on an outcome has an interest in that outcome.” Kalshi and Polymarket both prohibit insider trading. But criminal charges of the sort described by Hegseth are sure to be a bigger deterrent than being banned from the Kalshi or Polymarket platforms. This was not the first warning issued internally by the Trump administration, The Associated Press wrote yesterday. “A group of new accounts on Polymarket made highly specific, well-timed bets on whether the US and Iran would reach a ceasefire just before President Donald Trump announced it in April, resulting in hundreds of thousands of dollars in profits for the new customers,” the AP wrote. “The White House warned staff against using private information to trade on prediction markets the same day the report came out.” In another incident, a Trump teleprompter aide reportedly made $100,000 on Kalshi by betting on what Trump would say in prepared remarks. Prediction markets have flourished under the Trump administration’s light-touch approach to regulation. The Trump administration is also trying to stop state governments from regulating the markets, arguing that state laws are preempted by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission’s exclusive jurisdiction. Courts have reached different conclusions, and state governments want the Supreme Court to uphold their right to impose restrictions on prediction-market gambling.

Using Google’s products and services has always been fundamentally precarious—doing so gives the world’s largest advertising company a lot of juicy personal data. Google has long said it won’t use the sensitive data from products like Gmail, Drive, and Photos for ad personalization, but a change to the company’s privacy policy appears to dismantle that firewall. On October 16, Google issued an update to its privacy policy, spotted by Android Authority. The ad personalization section used to include a clear limit on data from certain apps. “We don’t show you personalized ads based on your content from Drive, Gmail, or Photos,” the document said. Now, Google has updated it with the following language. This equivocation opens the door to using data from apps like Gmail and Photos in ads when it’s shared with other Google services, and it just so happens Google has a mechanism for doing that, with Personal Intelligence. The company released Personal Intelligence in early 2026, allowing users to hand data from multiple apps to Google AI products. The idea is that the added context from your existing data will lead to better AI answers, but it’s also proving to be a privacy minefield. Personal Intelligence connects your data to the Gemini app but also to features like AI Mode search. While the Gemini app does not currently have ads, you may see sponsored content in AI search tools. Google’s changes to the privacy policy appear to acknowledge that data shared with AI search via Personal Intelligence could, therefore, contribute to ad personalization. According to Google, this clarification still doesn’t mean ads are “based directly” on your personal data. Here’s the company’s full statement: “Connecting your apps is entirely opt-in. Ads will continue to be relevant to things like your query, the context of the response and your interests. Ads will not be based directly on your Gmail inbox or Photos library, and as always, sensitive information will not be used to tailor ads.” Google’s statement is essentially a roundabout way of confirming that Workspace and Photos data may inform ads. If you interact with AI Mode while Personal Intelligence is enabled, there’s a chance that summaries and insights from your data will inform the answers you get. Because that same data is used for ad targeting, it’s accurate to say that your personal data is an element of ad targeting in AI search. The privacy policy now makes that clear. A Google spokesperson tells Ars that its privacy clarification does not indicate a change in how it handles your data with respect to its advertising partners. It’s not scanning your entire inbox to enhance your advertising profile, and it’s not giving any of this data to third parties. But if you pipe personal data into an AI search tool, the ads it shows you could be based on that information. The core of the problem is that Google wants its products and services to talk to each other. Google sees that wealth of data as a key strength of its AI efforts. Some apps like Gmail are still given protected status in Google’s data handling, but there are now more avenues for that data to get out. The more data you share across Google’s ecosystem, the greater the risk to your privacy. We’ve seen a similar exception in the way Google uses your AI interactions for model training. While Google says it doesn’t use personal data for AI training, it does use your inputs and the model outputs for that purpose. With Gemini integrated with so many services and connected to them via Personal Intelligence, those outputs may very well contain elements of your personal information. Google has said it tries to filter that content from the user activity controls, therefore keeping it out of training sets. However, it doesn’t promise it will always be successful. Google is correct that Personal Intelligence is opt-in, but the company uses various prompts and pop-ups to entice users to turn it on without fully explaining the consequences. You may have turned it on without realizing. If you want to ensure ads never use your personal data in this way, you’ll need to make sure Personal Intelligence is turned off. The easiest way is to access the Gemini settings for Personal Intelligence and disable the connected apps. These settings are also a few levels deep in your main Google account settings under Data & privacy > Personalization settings > Manage Personalized Recommendations in Search Services > Connected Content Apps. From there, you can disable connections to Workspace and Google Photos.

Leaders in the US Space Force would like to have a new spaceport up and running in four years, adding a third heavy-lift launch site to relieve pressure from bases in Florida and California that have shot rockets into orbit for nearly 70 years. The obvious problem with that ambition is 2030 is four years away, and Pentagon officials have not determined where the new spaceport should go or how to acquire land for it. Air Force Secretary Troy Meink first mentioned the service’s search for a third launch base in May. A congressionally mandated study of the Space Force’s launch infrastructure identified the need for “another site that’s capable of heavy and super heavy launch capability,” Meink said in a House Armed Services Committee hearing. The Trump administration released a space transportation policy in August that endorsed the construction of more launch and reentry sites for commercial and military missions. This week, the Space Force general responsible for procuring launch services for the military laid out an “aspirational” timeline for a new launch site. “We need this capability by 2030, but I’m also a pragmatist,” said Lt. Gen. Philip Garrant, head of Space Systems Command. “That is not an inconsequential demand to build a spaceport when you factor in orbital regimes, safety, access to commodities, the Americans that live near it. In many ways, it’s much like an airport, but even louder. So there’s a lot of consideration.” But there’s no firm plan yet for when the military might get moving on actually setting up a new launch base. “It’s going to take a strong partnership with local and state governments,” Garrant said. “As far as decision timelines, we don’t have anything yet.” There were 175 orbital launch attempts last year from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station and Kennedy Space Center in Florida and Vandenberg Space Force Base in California. Space Force officials project the country’s established spaceports will need to support 700 launches per year by 2036. A third launch site would offload some of the launch demand from limited real estate at Cape Canaveral and Vandenberg. It would also provide what Space Force leaders call “geographic resiliency” to mitigate the risk of a natural disaster or military attack knocking the Cape or Vandenberg offline. The federal government already owns a third spaceport at NASA’s Wallops Flight Facility in Virginia, used for suborbital rocket tests and small- and medium-class satellite launches. Small rockets can also launch from Pacific Spaceport Complex, owned by the state of Alaska. There are also commercial options that might be available for a new military spaceport. Ars asked Garrant if the Space Force might consider leasing property from a privately owned launch base, such as the expansive spaceport SpaceX plans to build in southern Louisiana. “We’ve discussed that we need to consider all of those opportunities: public-private partnership, new federal installation, state land, leveraging commercial land—all of those are possibilities. They all have pros and cons. We haven’t made that decision yet, but we’re not discounting any of those options.”

France avoided a credit downgrade from Moody’s, but the ratings agency painted a bleak picture of the eurozone’s second-largest economy that highlighted the risks from its intractable politics. In an announcement late Friday, Moody’s kept France at Aa3—two grades below the U.S. and three below Germany—with a negative outlook, while ratings from peers Fitch and S&P Global are one notch lower. France still boasts a wealthy economy and competent public institutions, according to Moody’s, but the political landscape threatens to paralyze the government and prevent it from making painful deficit-reduction steps. The report flagged France’s divided parliament, which is often at odds with the government, as well as political and social instability. For over a month, the country has been consumed by protests that erupted into widespread riots last weekend. At first, students decried the lack of investment in public education, then others joined in to speak out against the high cost of living as fuel prices soar due to the U.S. war on Iran. Other European countries have seen similar protests in what’s been dubbed the “Oct. 17 Movement.” At the same time, France’s looming debt has sent bond yields to the highest levels in 24 years, flashing warning signs not seen since the eurozone debt crisis in 2011. France’s public debt is now a record $4 trillion, or 119% of GDP—the highest ratio since the immediate aftermath of World War II. With far-right and far-left candidates in the lead to become France’s next president in the spring, markets are losing confidence that either side will take the difficult steps necessary to restore fiscal order. A 2027 budget proposal that would modestly bring the deficit down to 5% of GDP from 5.4% is now on the table but has yet to pass amid political gridlock, and Moody’s warned recent unrest could constrain fiscal consolidation efforts. “The 2027 budget process coincides with some of the most extensive social protests France has experienced in many years,” it said. Moody’s expects the budget will eventually be adopted, but possibly by decree as the National Assembly dithers. And even then, the budget will likely do less than hoped, reducing the deficit to 5.2% of GDP due to weaker GDP growth than projected by the government and the political challenges of implementing real spending cuts. This too-little, too-late budget will not be the end of France’s debt woes. Moody’s said a “significant and sustained fiscal effort” is required beyond the presidential elections to stabilize the debt burden, which is on track to hit 122% next year. The ratings agency isn’t confident France’s leaders will meet the challenge. “While we expect that the next president and government will seek to continue to narrow France’s deficit, our baseline expectation is that they are unlikely to be able to implement rapid fiscal consolidation in light of social tensions, a political environment that is likely to remain fragmented and muted GDP growth,” it predicted. “As a result, the debt burden will continue to rise through the remainder of the decade.” There is still some time, as Moody’s pointed out that the average maturity of France’s debt is around 8.5 years. That means the recent surge in borrowing costs must be sustained over an extended period to meaningfully worsen debt affordability. But France is especially vulnerable to “event risk” driven by geopolitics as well as domestic politics and instability, Moody’s added, describing a situation where lawmakers will have trouble tacking the most pressing problems. “The negative outlook reflects the increased risk that the fragmentation of France’s political landscape will continue to impair the functioning of the country’s legislative institutions,” the report said. “This political instability, also visible in the current social tensions, risks hampering the French government’s ability to address key policy challenges such as an elevated fiscal deficit, rising debt burden, and durable increase in borrowing costs, thus leading to a more rapid weakening in the sovereign’s key credit metrics than we currently expect.” Thierry Wizman and Gareth Berry, global strategists at Macquarie, also drew a connection between the French debt crisis and the protests. In a note earlier this month, they warned that the longer the protest go on, the more likely it is that the government will cave and approve more spending, which would make the debt situation worse. On Wednesday, a decades-old open-source web technology called PHP almost broke the internet, thanks to two vulnerabilities that had sat dormant for years until researchers recently figured out a way to exploit them. The bugs are dubbed PHP2Shell, officially designated as CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619. In simple terms, the bugs allow anyone who knows how to exploit them to break into an online system running PHP, such as a website or internet-facing server, without needing a password to access or modify its data. As situations go, this was a pretty sizable one, forcing millions of people around the world to scramble to patch their systems before malicious hackers could abuse them. The PHP Foundation, which maintains the PHP web software, publicly released patches on Wednesday that fixed the bugs, which when installed prevent web-facing servers from being attacked. The foundation had already given some of the biggest internet companies and web hosts that rely on PHP an early headstart on patching. Thanks to weeks of backroom chatter and careful coordination across the industry, the world — so far — broadly avoided mass internet exploitation. From Wednesday, governments began urging citizens and companies with websites to take action to mitigate the risks on their ends. A wide range of industries shuttered their websites and pulled services offline to patch their systems. Banks, retailers, gaming sites, online stores, hospitals, and some government agencies took down their servers to prevent attacks. But security researchers are warning that the situation isn’t over yet, and that a large number of systems around the world remain unpatched and vulnerable to hacks and data theft. This is also unlikely to be the last major internet software scare, with some expecting the advent of AI to rapidly uncover as-yet-undiscovered vulnerabilities that are waiting to be found and abused. Security researchers at Calif found two bugs in PHP, a programming language widely used around the world for building websites and web apps, both on the internet and hosted locally on company networks, like intranets and internal sites. PHP is open-source, meaning it’s free and available to be used by anyone for almost any reason. As such, it’s become one of the most popular and broadly used web programming languages on the web today. The two bugs were first discovered in 2024 but weren’t considered exploitable at the time, and weren’t patched. Security researchers recently found a way to exploit them, prompting them to alert the makers of the software to get them fixed. Calif alerted the PHP Foundation, which began a period of fixing the bugs and coordinated disclosure. The foundation did this by offering the information and patches about the security issues under a tightly guarded embargo — essentially held confidentially until a specific deadline — to give big companies an opportunity to get ahead of installing the fixes. PHP2Shell is pretty serious because it can be abused remotely over the internet and without the victim having to click on a link or open a file. This is known as remote code execution, and it’s one of the more damaging kinds of bugs to exploit because they can grant a hacker anywhere in the world effectively full control over an affected and unpatched system. Worse, these specific PHP bugs make it possible to break into any unpatched system on the internet without needing a password or any other credential over the internet, known as unauthenticated access. This would allow a malicious hacker to then plant malware on the computer, such as to steal data or simply to maintain a long-term foothold that they can return to later. The bugs are particularly risky because PHP is wildly popular software, in part because it’s free and relatively easy to use, but also because it’s been around for decades. Around 70% of the web-facing internet relies on PHP, though its usage has been slowly declining over the years in favor of newer, more secure programming languages. Given its popularity, some of the world’s most popular websites are run on web software that rely on PHP, such as WordPress (which hosts TechCrunch!), as well as Drupal, Joomla, and MediaWiki, which powers online encyclopedia giant Wikipedia. PHP is just one of many aging but functional technologies that have survived waves of the internet and generations that are now so ubiquitous, they’re akin to the glue that holds much of the internet together. Oftentimes these tools are developed by open source maintainers, who are not paid for the work that they do, and as such cannot afford third-party security reviews — but are still widely relied on by companies who do not contribute financially to the projects. Security researchers at Calif were credited with discovering the bugs earlier this year as part of a security audit of PHP’s code. Calif was participating in the Hack the Planet initiative by AI model maker OpenAI and cloud security giant Wiz, which subsequently verified the proof-of-concept exploits that Calif made. In Calif’s write-up (featuring comics — it’s great, check it out), the researchers explained that AI was used in assistance with exploiting the bug, but that the bulk of the work relied on knowledge that the researchers already had, and that they knew PHP’s codebase and exploitation history well. “No model is simply capable of unearthing ‘all bugs’ with simple prompts. At least for now,”wrote Calif. Despite efforts to keep details of the bugs under wraps until Wednesday, it does appear that there was some PHP2Shell exploitation prior to the release of patches. Amazon, which has enormous visibility into online threats due to its size and scale, said in a blog post that it detected exploitation of PHP2Shell about a week before the PHP patches were available, suggesting that someone, somewhere, knew how to exploit the bugs before their details were broadly available. Amazon said it wasn’t clear how the hacker learned of the vulnerabilities, but that defenders should look for evidence of exploitation as early as October 17. There are a few theories as to what happened. Once a bug is patched, the details of the patch can become immediately apparent, especially if the code — like in PHP’s case — is open source, making it public and inspectable. Exploiting the bugs might not be easy, but knowing where the bug is and how it was fixed can allow malicious hackers to understand where to target and how to develop their exploits. It’s possible that there was a leak or a breakdown in communication somewhere during the embargo. The open source community is enormous and controlling information in this fashion relies on good-faith. It’s also possible that hackers discovered the bugs ahead of time, either independently or through espionage. The PHP Foundation blamed one company for pushing the patches live hours before the embargo period was up, which could have given some attackers a headstart if they obtained details of the patch before others. But this doesn’t account for the week-long activity detected by Amazon. Researchers say that bugs require a lot of work to become exploitable and weaponizing the bug has been a challenge — but not impossible — which explains why we haven’t seen a scale of mass-exploitation just yet. But as of late Friday, there are now confirmed compromises and post-exploit activity related to the PHP bug, according to security giants Palo Alto Networks and Trail of Bits. That means the bug was detected being abused to hack in and actively do malicious activity on an as-yet-unknown target. It’s still early days but it’s an encouraging sign that there haven’t been any signs of mass-exploitation — but we’re not out of the danger-zone just yet. Matthew Prince, the chief executive of network security and monitoring giant Cloudflare, which also has telemetry into a vast portion of the internet, said this week that it’s seen an exponential number of attempts to exploit the bugs following their disclosure. This gives some perspective as to how quickly malicious actors are seeking to abuse the flaws for their own gains. PHP isn’t just found on web servers, but also used in developer tools, command line and terminal packages, and other software components used by routers, storage devices, and enterprise security devices, like firewalls and VPNs. We should expect to see exploitation of PHP2Shell down the line as smaller IT teams and sole developers may be slower to update. There will also be a period in which various companies that rely on and integrate PHP into their own products and software, which will also need updating. As more devices and systems get updated, the attack surface will reduce in time. But the ripple effects of these bugs could still be felt for years to come, particularly in legacy systems that have no means to be updated. This was one of those rare internet-stopping days I’ve seen during my time as a security reporter. In 2016, a massive botnet flooded internet registry Dyn with junk traffic causing much of the web’s top services to crawl to a halt. In 2017, North Korea used leaked U.S. government hacking tools to infect the U.K.’s health system with ransomware. And in 2024, a faulty CrowdStrike update sparked a massive global outage causing most of the world’s Windows computers to crash at once. By all accounts with PHP2Shell, the internet escaped what could have otherwise been an extremely painful and damaging day. But it’s unlikely to be the last major incident to hit the web, and this may be the first in a trend of incidents that exploit weaknesses in legacy technology. Much of this is due to inherent flaws in aging but omnipresent software that haven’t yet been discovered, coupled with AI tools that are making it easier to find flaws. PHP, much like other web programming languages and technologies from decades ago, are “memory unsafe,” which means they can be attacked through an entire class of vulnerabilities that can force the software to leak or spill sensitive data from the software’s memory storage. The National Security Agency says around 75% of all actively exploited vulnerabilities abuse memory-related flaws, which is to say, hackers use them a lot. This has helped to prompt a move to using memory-safe languages, like Python, Rust, and Swift, which can’t be abused in the same way that memory vulnerabilities can be. As such, these languages make them broadly immune to a broad swath of vulnerabilities that still affect their predecessors. But that still leaves an entire chunk of the internet running a bunch of technologies, like PHP, media and video codecs, compression libraries, and other software from decades ago — which, when they break, will have massive implications for the rest of the web. Narayan, a Dalit tailor, insists on marking his own ballot in an upcoming election instead of allowing an upper-caste landlord’s men to vote on his behalf. Dukhi, his father, warns him against it.  “It is still my right,” Narayan replies. “And I will exercise it in the next election, I promise you.” “Life without dignity is worthless,” Narayan replies. I have been rereading Mistry’s novel over the past few weeks as a series of protests has turned October into a month of fierce battles over the integrity of India’s elections, the accountability of its institutions, the right to protest, and the soul of its democracy. A nascent Gen-Z movement, Cockroach Janta Party, which organized protests over education and unemployment in Delhi in June and July and forced the resignation of India’s higher education minister, gave the call for the October protests. Young protestors answered with an insurrectionary energy in the summer and soon found support from a broader coalition comprised of opposition parties, social movements and other pillars of Indian civil society. These assorted protesters see the stakes of the current moment as existential: the sanctity of the vote in India, the essential ingredient, in other words, of democracy.  Throughout October thousands joined the cause in Delhi and other Indian cities, their marches spurred by anger directed against an ongoing national exercise to overhaul the country’s electoral register. Known as the Special Intensive Revision, the exercise has, according to the protestors, resulted in more than 130 million voters being removed from electoral lists. Although electoral rolls have been revised periodically, this is the first time such an exercise has placed the burden on citizens to establish their eligibility, raising fears that millions of legitimate voters, particularly the poor and marginalized, could be disenfranchised. Seeking accountability, they have been calling for the resignation of Gyanesh Kumar, the country’s chief election commissioner, who oversees the Election Commission of India, the body charged with conducting and safeguarding the sanctity of elections across the nation. The protestors accuse Kumar of abandoning his constitutional obligation to act impartially. In their telling, the mandarin has set the table in a way to favor the interests of Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s Bharatiya Janata Party, a charge denied by those in power. Already shaken by the summer protests over education, this time the Indian government responded with coercive force, effectively turning central Delhi, the political and administrative heart of the capital, and the place where India goes to protest, into a garrison. On Oct. 10, it deployed tens of thousands of police and paramilitary personnel, restricted internet access, detained thousands of protesters, and sought to deny the protesters access to public spaces across the capital. The crackdown was intense. On the night of Oct. 16, there was a palpable sense of panic on social media in India as an urgent question was asked repeatedly: Where is Neha Bora? A 29-year-old doctoral student and left-wing activist, Bora has emerged as one of the most articulate and formidable leaders of these protests, which have rattled Modi’s government and—despite valiant attempts to construct a counter narrative by BJP leaders and other party apparatchiks—become a significant challenge to his popularity and legitimacy. Bora was detained by the police on their way to join the thousands of protesters who had converged on Connaught Place, a prominent commercial district in the capital, waving the national flag and reiterating demands to protect the right to vote and the sanctity of electoral democracy in India. For several hours after sunset, there was no news of the whereabouts of Bora, who uses they/them pronouns. Their lawyers spoke about the police refusing to reveal their location. Eventually, around midnight, their lawyers located them at a police station. Ritesh Shukla—Getty Images The emotional charge behind the protests has its roots in the birth of the modern Indian nation-state itself. India always took great pride in adopting universal franchise rights as the Constitution of India came into effect on Jan. 26, 1950. In its historic first general elections in 1952, every Indian above the age of 21, irrespective of caste, religion, gender, race or wealth, had their constitutionally guaranteed right to vote. It was, as many legal and constitutional scholars have since remarked, a colossal feat, and one that became a touchstone for the modern idea of India. Hence the current upheaval in the country. The Modi government may be reversing decades of democratic gains by recasting the act of voting as a privilege, conferred at the discretion of bureaucrats or as a favor from the ruling party, rather than a right guaranteed by the constitutional order. Now, every prospective voter must first complete an enumeration form, and many may subsequently be required to produce onerous documentary evidence, such as birth certificates and educational records to establish their eligibility to vote. Tens of millions of Indians from marginalized communities either lack documentary records or possess documents that are riddled with mistakes such as spelling errors, making it harder for them to navigate any process of bureaucratic verification. The fear is that India is—to put it bluntly—being yanked back to a darker time, one from which it emerged in no small part because of the work done over decades to firmly establish the credibility and broaden the depth of its democratic franchise. In the 1950s, India was an overwhelmingly rural country, with around 83% of its population living in the villages and only about 17% in urban areas. Its nascent democratic system confronted entrenched social hierarchies of caste and power. Early accounts of grassroots democracy in India describe how higher-caste landowners controlled the votes of their lower-caste dependents, sometimes even marking ballots that the voters had to authenticate with their thumbprints. The pioneering Indian sociologist M.N. Srinivas showed how politicians campaigning for votes treated villagers not as individual voters with agency but as members of “vote banks.” Politicians approached landlords or community leaders to deliver these blocs of votes in return for favors from the government, from “licenses for buses and rice-mills to seats in medical and technological colleges for their kinsfolk.” And well into the 1960s, millions of Indians were still unable to exercise their constitutional right to vote. In the eastern Indian state of Bihar, landlords used club-wielding enforcers known as lathaits to keep Dalits, members of the most oppressed caste, out of polling booths. “I remember watching members of my caste being dragged out of a polling booth,” an elderly Dalit man in Bihar’s Siwan district recalled in an interview with The Print, an Indian news site. During the state elections in 1967, Jagdish Master, a teacher turned activist, challenged the veto of the landlords over Dalit suffrage by escorting a group of Dalits to a polling booth in Ekwari, a village in Bihar’s Bhojpur district. The landlords’ henchmen brutally beat him and drove him out. He returned to the village as an armed guerrilla, recruiting Dalit laborers and other poor peasants in a Maoist-style rural insurgency that would flare across the region for decades. The power of the vote in Indian consciousness transformed radically after the “Emergency,” the period between June 1975 and March 1977 when Prime Minister Indira Gandhi suspended constitutional rights, assumed untrammeled powers, imprisoned opposition leaders and silenced the press. Much of India’s elite—bureaucrats, businessmen, newspaper editors and movie stars—bent the knee and welcomed Mrs. Gandhi’s promise of “order” and “discipline.” Wealth and connections offered protection from the state. India’s poor, stripped of their electoral power, suffered terrible abuses of an unaccountable bureaucracy and police from slum demolitions to forced sterilization of men. In the general elections held in March 1977 after the end of the Emergency, Mrs. Gandhi’s Congress Party was decisively defeated by the people and tossed out of power for the first time since Indian independence in 1947. The principal victims of abuses of the Emergency—Dalits, Muslims, and the poor of the populous northern Indian states—voted against the Congress party for the first time and contributed significantly to its removal from power The power of the vote has gradually forced India’s high-handed bureaucracy to recognize the human dignity and political citizenship of ordinary people. The electorate delivered its verdict on Mrs. Gandhi’s breach of the sacred covenant between the Indian state and its people: the people confer legitimacy on the state in exchange for their rights, including its protection of the sanctity of the vote. Having asserted their power, voters proved difficult to subdue again. As the political theorist Partha Chatterjee put it, it was the 1977 defeat of Mrs. Gandhi and the Congress party that established the “capacity of the vote.” India’s poor and marginalized took proper control of their voting choices three decades after independence. In the years after the Emergency, they leveraged the vote as a means to a secure livelihood or as a tool for ensuring justice. Politicians could no longer rely on local patrons to intimidate people into voting for them and had to compete on representing their interests. A new competitive politics emerged in the 1980s and led to an upsurge, as Chatterjee wrote, “of popular demands of a kind that had never before been allowed to disturb the order and tranquility of the proverbial corridors of power.” By the 1990s, political leaders from socially disenfranchised castes and Dalit communities were taking power in the populous, northern Indian states of Bihar and Uttar Pradesh. Political scientist Christophe Jaffrelot memorably described it as “the silent revolution.” This revolution, ushered in by the votes of the historically oppressed communities, did not quite transform the economic and social structures of the region. But it changed the everyday relations of power. Indrajit Roy, a scholar of development studies, recalled a refrain he heard frequently during his fieldwork among Dalits in Bihar in the late 2000s: “There has been development, change has come. Today, we can look the landlord in the eye and speak as equals.” Vote banks had thus turned into vehicles of collective political assertion. Marginalized communities had become adept at pooling their votes to secure political favors and advance their shared interests. They chose their own patrons, preferring their own caste leaders to represent their interests and negotiate with the state on their behalf. Over time, the clientelist politics of vote banks became associated with an entrenched culture of corruption. This helped pave the way for Modi’s successful national campaign in 2014, in which he cast himself as a populist strongman who embodied the aspirations for “development” of all Indians while portraying the Congress and regional parties as beholden to their respective vote banks. Bangladesh borders the Indian state of West Bengal and Modi reserved his sharpest, xenophobic barbs for Bengal’s Muslims, whom he tarred during his 2014 campaign as “Bangladeshi” and “illegal immigrants,” establishing a template for their vilification that persists to this day. Yet the Indian government has used unfounded speculation about “illegal” Bangladeshis to overturn the fundamental logic of the country’s electoral registration system, shifting the burden of ensuring registration from the Election Commission to the voter herself. The commission was responsible for ensuring that every citizen made it onto the electoral rolls. The exercise has already disproportionately disenfranchised the poor, migrants, religious minorities, women—and is bound to continue doing so. “The majority of voters in Bihar who were excluded from the list were in rural areas,” said retired Maj. Gen. Anil Verma, who heads the Association for Democratic Reforms, a nonprofit group that challenged the electoral rolls revisions in the Supreme Court. “Those worst affected were the poor and the marginalized—they had no documents.” Opposition parties have also alleged that the electoral roll revisions tilted the electoral field in the BJP’s favor in the West Bengal election held earlier this year, helping the party win power in the state for the first time. But that claim is difficult to reconcile with the disproportionate representation  of certain  groups among those deleted from supplementary lists issued for some constituencies after an adjudication process. For instance, although Muslims make up around 23% of the population of Nandigram, a constituency in West Bengal, the SABAR Institute study found that they accounted for 95.5% of the deletions across seven supplementary lists released by the Election Commission for the constituency. Voting carries an almost existential significance for Indians whose equal citizenship has not been fully recognized by the institutions of the state. Indians see the vote as an affirmation of their existence. If the vote is no longer a gift freely given or withdrawn by the people but a resource controlled by the state, it reduces ordinary voters once again to supplicants dependent on the favor of political elites. The electoral revisions and mass deletion of voters have provoked such widespread revulsion because many Indians see it as an assault on their identity as citizens and a threat to the social recognition they have won through a long, arduous struggle. Many Indians might have forgiven Modi for his numerous missteps but they are determined not to let him devalue their voting power. The young protesters moving through Delhi’s barricaded streets carry a palpable sense that they are living through a historical moment. References to the Quit India movement against the country’s erstwhile British colonizers and the protests around the Emergency have become commonplace. Memories of seminal events of the past offer a lens through which to consider the present. The future seems open to change through collective action. This sense of a turning point imbues individual actions with a larger purpose and drives protesters to confront a police force intent on clearing the streets by whatever means necessary. On Oct. 9, a day before Delhi was placed under a lockdown, Bora invoked India’s struggle against British imperialism to denounce the pre-emptive detention of protest organizers. “History is repeating itself. Just like in 1942, the arrest of leaders could not stop the Quit India movement; this movement will also not stop,” Bora said. “This is nothing but a state of open emergency in the country.” The convergence of young people’s anger over education and employment, fears of authoritarianism, and concern for the sanctity of the vote poses a serious challenge to the Modi government. It could forge a broad popular coalition of unemployed young people, urban poor, Dalits and religious minorities, united in challenging the legitimacy of the government. Unless the government retreats on electoral roll revisions, sustained protests could drain its political capital. One of Indian democracy’s great achievements is that even its poorest citizens have proved adept at recognizing when the power of their vote is being wrested away, gauging the consequences and organizing to resist. A government in India can retain its legitimacy despite failing to provide functioning schools or decent employment. It cannot do so if it also violates the sanctity of the vote. As I scan my feedback on a recent social media post, my brain just can’t seem to help registering the number of “likes.” I’m not alone. Under a recent settlement that Meta recently reached with states, Facebook and Instagram are about to look different for minors Among the new protections, one seemingly modest change caught my eye. By default, users under 18 will no longer be able to see likes and other reactions in response to their posts or those of others. The reforms are designed for children and teenagers, but touch on a psychological phenomenon just as relevant to adults. As a psychiatrist, I found myself wondering: Why does that little number beneath your post matter so much? The answer has less to do with social media and more to do with an ancient human tendency: social comparison. Social comparison is the psychological process by which people assess themselves, including their own abilities, opinions, and achievements, in relation to those of others. Likes can both reward us and rank us. They show us that people are validating our posts and also quantify that validation. We know how much approval we receive relative to what we might have expected, what we got the last time we posted, and what others receive. Feedback is scored. Likes are just one example. Follower counts, views, and shares are other metrics that make social information measurable. When social approval is numerically valued, comparison becomes harder to avoid. From an evolutionary perspective, social comparison may have served an adaptive purpose. Humans evolved in social groups. Knowing where you stood relative to others might have helped you identify what behaviors were approved, whether you belonged, and how to navigate hierarchy. But moving beyond prehistoric time to the last century, comparison still had constraints. Your 25-year high school reunion? It happened once in your lifetime, maybe in a city far away from you, with only your fellow classmates in attendance. Now, the high school reunion lives in the device in our hands, capable of giving anyone quantitative signals from a vast reference group at any moment on our personalized scoreboards of social approval. If the subjective question is whether we belong, what can feel like an objective answer is the 100 likes our post gets from 2,500 followers. The scale, frequency, quantification, and accessibility of social comparison have all changed. Our brains weren’t built for this much comparison. Neuroimaging studies show that our brains allocate value to how we believe we stack up next to others. Downward social comparison, in which we learn someone performs worse than us, can engage neural circuits associated with reward and valuation. Upward comparison, in which we learn someone performs better than us, can engage neural circuits associated with processing unpleasant emotions. These are ways in which our brains process social outcomes much like other kinds of gains or losses. But our brains don’t just register numbers; we also tend to evaluate their meaning. In one study, likes functioned as a type of currency that adult users compared themselves on, shaping how they felt in that moment. In another set of studies, adults who made more frequent and extreme upward comparisons reported an immediate decline in self-evaluation and cumulative declines in self-esteem and life satisfaction over a browsing session. Research has also found that across generational groups, greater user tendency to engage in social comparison, together with greater maladaptive ways of coping with emotions, is associated with more symptoms of anger, anxiety, and depression. Social comparison isn’t inherently harmful. But as repeated opportunities to compare mount, the mental health effects can add up. With just days to go before November’s midterms, the Department of Justice ended this week by launching its most desperate and unprofessional assaults on the American election system yet. On Thursday, the DOJ filed an absurd lawsuit seeking to block a declaration of a winner in Alaska’s U.S. Senate race until the courts relitigate the eligibility of one of its candidates. This comes the same day as an audacious DOJ effort to preemptively push a California election case to higher courts before lower courts have even had a chance to hold a hearing. The executive branch’s continued election interference has not been deterred by going zero for 26 in cases seeking unredacted state voter lists and losing badly at the Supreme Court in a case that would have shut down mail balloting this election. From Alaska to California and who knows where else, DOJ has been filing cases to interfere with how states count and certify votes. The agency’s latest moves—as factually unsupported as they are legally vacuous—demonstrate that for all the fear about DOJ coming in with guns blazing to try to overturn the results of the elections, it is most likely to be shooting blanks. The main reason that DOJ has been losing election-related lawsuits is that the Constitution does not give the president or the executive branch a role to play in running elections. That matter is left to the states, subject to congressional legislation that can alter the rules in federal elections. So when President Donald Trump issued a March 2025 executive order soon after coming back into office that would have changed the federal voter registration form (mandated by Congress) to require it be accompanied by documentary proof of citizenship like a birth certificate, a federal court shut it down. It ruled that, “Put simply, our Constitution does not allow the President to impose unilateral changes to federal election procedures.” And when the USPS followed Trump’s second executive order by announcing a rule that would have made the agency a federal gatekeeper over state mail ballots, the Supreme Court ruled that USPS was likely to lose on the merits because its muscling into state election processes exceeds its power. DOJ’s latest moves in California and Alaska are even weaker than DOJ’s earlier attempts to assert power over elections. In California, DOJ demanded that California and Los Angeles County go through a 20,000-person list of what it termed “likely” or “suspected” noncitizens (it vacillated on what to call them), take their mail-in ballots out of the pile, and segregate them so they won’t be counted. It first provided the information on a thumb drive with the password on a Post-it note. After California officials complained that using such a drive would breach security protocols—would you put a Trump-issued thumb drive into your PC?—they provided a spreadsheet. The spreadsheet appears to be wildly inaccurate; more than half from a sample of 200 looked at by the county were people not registered, and many were U.S.-born—a great indicator they are citizens. This is no surprise. As I recently recounted in Slate: When the Department of Homeland Security made unsupported claims that states were allowing thousands of noncitizens to remain on their voting rolls, state and local election administrators demanded evidence. After Nevada got the data and investigated, the DHS list of 16,000 potentially ineligible Nevada voters fell to zero. Even weaker than DOJ’s factual claims are its legal arguments. The sole basis that DOJ relied upon in seeking this extraordinary relief from California is a federal statute that makes it a crime for a noncitizen to vote in an election. That statute gives the federal government zero right to tell California what it can or cannot do in counting ballots, and indeed, another federal statute, the National Voter Registration Act of 1993, prohibits states from systematically removing ineligible voters in the 90 days before an election. That’s to prevent the inadvertent disenfranchisement of eligible voters. Undeterred by the legal and factual weakness of the case, DOJ asked a federal district court to issue a temporary restraining order to make Los Angeles County segregate out those ballots. The district court set a hearing on the motion for this upcoming Tuesday. DOJ then “requested” that the court rule by noon on Thursday. When it didn’t, DOJ tried to appeal the non-ruling to the United States Court of Appeals for the 9th Circuit. This was a Hail Mary procedural play in a Hail Mary substantive case. TROs are not appealable, and the judge has not even ruled yet, so there is no order to appeal from. DOJ is trying a back-door attempt at “mandamus,” a court order to a lower court or other government official to make them do something they have been dilatory in doing. Except, the California district court has barely had time to consider the matter, so claiming it has been dilatory is a shocking abuse of the court docket. It’s not likely to fly, but soon the case will likely end up at the Supreme Court. They gave the New York Post an exclusive look at a pre-dawn raid with helicopters and armed federal officers to take into custody a Trump-supporting elderly gentleman who recently had six surgeries. This political theater was a show of force aimed at giving the impression of a scourge of noncitizen voting. Things are even more bizarre in Alaska. It argued that Alaska somehow was violating federal law when it allowed a second candidate named Dan Sullivan to run against the incumbent with the same name and Democrat Mary Peltola. (The federal government earlier convened a grand jury to look into criminal charges against the second Dan Sullivan.) DOJ has no power to decide which candidates can appear on the ballot. The sole basis that DOJ asserted for trying to block the state’s certification process is the National Voter Registration Act’s preamble that reads that the act is designed to “protect the integrity of the electoral process.” That preamble of course gives no power whatsoever to the federal government to do anything. It does not give DOJ the power to determine ballot access or block certification of a federal election. The Alaska Supreme Court decided the state law ballot access question back in June, allowing the second Sullivan to appear on the ballot. So shoddy was the DOJ’s work in the Alaska case that it made the most absurd typo possible in its prayer for relief. DOJ requested: Defendants from certifying any result that may be impacted by voter confusion, as defined above, pending an investigation to determine whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan were meant to be cast for Senator Sullivan. Note the lack of a verb specifying what the Trump administration is actually asking a judge to do, which is to issue an injunction “blocking” certification. Suffice it to say, the best and brightest are not litigating these cases at DOJ, and they are both likely to fail and fail badly. To say these are press releases masquerading as lawsuits does not convey how weak these cases legally are. Maybe they are just meant to please Donald Trump in his voter fraud crusade, or to remind Alaska voters of the two Dan Sullivans on the ballot. They are not serious legal documents. Of the many things that could go wrong in November, I worry most about DOJ seeking to interfere with the tabulation and certification process, especially in a place like Alaska that may determine which party controls the United States Senate. The situation in Alaska is specifically frightening. Due to its complex voting procedures, strange time zone, and remoteness, in normal years it can take a particularly long time to count all of Alaska’s ballots. These types of delays are what Trump has seized on in the past to most successfully attack elections, specifically the COVID-delayed election counts of 2020. I am particularly concerned DOJ could try to seize ballots or voting machines. If these were my election law students they would fail the course. Although thousands of lawyers are rightly preparing around the country for a potential Trump-inspired onslaught of federal actions challenging election results, the DOJ’s blazing incompetence puts me a little bit more at ease. The next competitive advantage in enterprise AI has less to do with which model you choose than with what you build around it. That’s an important shift. For the past several years, much of the AI conversation has centered on model capability: bigger models, better reasoning, larger context windows, higher benchmark scores. Those advances have been extraordinary. But as AI moves from experimentation to production, the question facing enterprise leaders has changed—it’s no longer, “what can the model do?”—it’s now, “Can we deliver that intelligence reliably, economically, and at scale?” Increasingly, the answer depends on the serving harness—the engineering system surrounding the model, including the architecture, memory management, scheduling, and optimization decisions that determine how efficiently model intelligence becomes a production capability. For technical executives, that changes where AI investment should go—and what they should demand from the architecture supporting it. There is no universally “best” model or AI architecture. There is only the right solution for the business problem. A customer-facing assistant optimized for fast responses has fundamentally different requirements from a document-processing pipeline that can run overnight. An internal knowledge application and a high-volume customer service platform may use the same foundation model but require very different serving architectures. The workload defines the constraints. The constraints should determine the architecture. That sounds simple, but it runs counter to the way many organizations have approached early AI deployments. During a proof of concept, model accuracy naturally dominates the discussion. Production introduces constraints that are much easier to ignore when only a handful of users are involved: latency, concurrency, memory pressure, GPU utilization, infrastructure cost, and unpredictable traffic. A model that produces marginally better answers is not necessarily the better production choice if it doubles inference costs or cannot meet the application's latency requirements. Not every workload needs a Ferrari. Sometimes the smarter engineering decision is the model that delivers exactly the performance the business needs without paying for capability it doesn't. The objective should be to deploy the right intelligence at the right cost for the workload. That makes serving architecture more than an engineering concern. It becomes an investment decision: where to spend on compute, where to optimize, and where additional model capability simply isn't worth the cost. Once AI reaches production, seemingly isolated engineering decisions begin affecting one another. Serving architecture influences memory utilization. Memory management affects concurrency. Concurrency shapes batching and scheduling, and scheduling affects GPU utilization and latency. Only then can techniques such as quantization, speculative decoding, or optimized attention mechanisms deliver their full value. This interdependence matters because organizations can spend considerably more on infrastructure without solving the constraint limiting performance. GPU memory is a good example. During inference, the model's weights remain fixed, but the key-value (KV) cache grows with active requests and context length. As concurrency increases, a system can run short of usable GPU memory even when compute capacity is still available. Adding more compute without understanding that bottleneck may simply make an inefficient system more expensive. The lesson for technology leaders is broader than memory management: Measure before you optimize. Identify the dominant constraint. Address it. Measure the result. Then determine if another bottleneck emerged. Repeat. Don't think of optimization techniques as Pokémon cards: The goal isn't to collect them all. The goal is to apply the technique that removes the highest-impact constraint while preserving the application's business requirements. For technology leaders, that demands a different governance question. Instead of asking which AI capabilities the organization can deploy, ask which constraints stand between a proven capability and sustainable business value. Perhaps the most important reason to invest in the serving harness is that today's leading model will probably not be tomorrow’s. Models will improve. Hardware will evolve. New optimization techniques will emerge. The architecture surrounding them should make those changes easier to absorb, not force the enterprise to rebuild its AI platform every time technology advances. That makes adaptability an increasingly important outcome of AI strategy. Organizations that build serving systems around workload requirements, clear service-level objectives, and measurable production economics gain the ability to evaluate new models and infrastructure against what the business actually needs. A model change can become an evaluation and configuration decision rather than an architectural reset. HHS has never told healthcare to stop faxing. Its guidance says the Privacy Rule permits physicians to disclose PHI to another provider by fax for treatment, as long as reasonable and appropriate safeguards are in place. Take two identical referral forms sent the same morning. One moves through an encrypted, logged channel covered by a signed business associate agreement (BAA), and OCR software indexes it into the chart. The other sits in an output tray in a shared hallway. Same document, same law, two very different compliance positions. Does a fax need to be HIPAA compliant? For a covered entity or business associate sending protected health information, yes. But a fax machine, an app or a cloud service can’t be compliant on an organization’s behalf. The rule asks whether the organization has reasonable safeguards around each disclosure. Covered entities, HHS says, “must have in place reasonable and appropriate administrative, technical, and physical safeguards” for PHI sent by fax, such as confirming the recipient’s number and choosing to pre-program frequently used numbers. Once a faxed page becomes a digital file, the Security Rule’s safeguard standards apply as well. “Most people start by asking whether a fax service is HIPAA compliant, and that’s the wrong place to start,” says Adam Korbl, Founder and CEO of iFax. “HIPAA doesn’t certify a fax machine or an app. I’d rather people ask who can open the document, whether anything done to it is logged, and whether the BAA covers the software reading the page, not just the line carrying it.” Korbl’s questions all concern the workflow. A fax that arrives intact but sits in a shared queue, or gets filed to the wrong chart, can still expose a patient’s information, and the industry’s breach numbers are already high: The HIPAA Journal counted 772 large healthcare data breaches on the OCR portal for 2025 as of early January 2026, a record. OCR engines, extraction models and routing rules all handle the page now, and each of those steps carries the same obligations as the transmission. Encryption is the obvious place to begin. Under the current Security Rule it’s an “addressable” specification, a label The HIPAA Journal notes has led some covered entities to treat it as optional when it never was, and for cloud fax it should reach the OCR text as well as the stored images, at rest and in transit. Every send, receipt, view and routing decision should also leave an entry that someone can pull up later. The BAA holds the arrangement together legally. HHS’s sample BAA provisions outline what the contract must require of a vendor, including Security Rule safeguards, breach reporting and matching terms for subcontractors. Under HHS cloud guidance, a cloud provider storing ePHI is a business associate even without the key, and the conduit exception “is limited to transmission-only services.” By that logic, a phone carrier moving the fax signal counts as a conduit, while a cloud fax platform storing copies generally counts as a business associate. If OCR or data extraction runs at a separate company, that company needs coverage too, usually under a subcontractor BAA with the vendor. HHS’s advice on misdialed fax numbers applies to routing rules as well. If a rule misfires and files a referral under the wrong patient, the result is no different from a misdialed number. Staff should look over uncertain matches before anything gets filed. Here a vendor can do a lot of the work. iFax says its healthcare platform pairs AES-256 encryption in transit and at rest with signed BAAs, role-based access and audit logs, and runs OCR inside the same controlled infrastructure. That handles the technical side. Policies, staff training and the risk analysis are still the covered entities to manage. Access control rounds out the list, and skipping it has a documented cost. The 2024 Change Healthcare attack began with compromised credentials on a system where MFA wasn’t turned on, and it ultimately affected approximately 193 million individuals. HHS has started writing AI into the rules, though the biggest changes are still proposals. The Security Rule overhaul published on January 6, 2025 would make encryption of ePHI mandatory with limited exceptions, require multi-factor authentication and call for an annual technology asset inventory and network map. It also names AI directly: a risk analysis “must include consideration of, among other things, the type and amount of ePHI accessed by the AI tool, to whom the data is disclosed, and to whom the output is provided.” Timing is uncertain: after nearly 5,000 comments, final action is now targeted for July 2027. OCR’s own estimate put first-year compliance costs at $9 billion, and the proposal would give organizations 240 days after the final rule to comply. None of that is likely to change who does what, though: vendors provide the tools, and practices still run the program. “We can give a practice the encryption, the audit trail and a controlled place for OCR to run. The survey also found that 28% of organizations have agentic AI in production already, while 44% are piloting or testing it. A practice can buy a compliant document stack. Compliance isn’t sold that way. It has to be run from inside the practice, with written policies that get updated and staff who are trained on them. A fax vendor that can’t produce encryption, access controls, audit logs and a signed BAA shouldn’t make the shortlist. For the AI layer, buyers should push further and find out where the reading actually happens, whether the BAA extends that far, whether automated actions are logged and who checks the matches the software isn’t sure about.
