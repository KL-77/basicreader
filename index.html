The reactor meant to showcase nuclear’s comeback keeps hitting problems

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-23

Visit Original Website
US regulators have issued a warning to a nuclear project critical to Donald Trump’s efforts to revive the industry, after a contractor fell into a reactor cavity and a bundle of fuel rods nearly tipped over during refueling. The US Nuclear Regulatory Commission told Holtec International, a company racing to reopen a shuttered reactor at the Palisades nuclear power plant in Michigan, to address a “chilled work environment” that has made staff hesitant to raise safety concerns without fear of retaliation. The warning has prompted concerns among critics that financial and political pressures to revitalize nuclear power as an answer to booming AI-driven energy demand are increasing risks of accidents. The NRC performed an inspection at Palisades in July over concerns about safety awareness. It followed an incident last year in which a worker fell into the reactor cavity, ingested some water, and was sent off-site for medical attention. The individual was decontaminated by radiation protection personnel but had 300 counts per minute detected in their hair, according to an incident report. In a second incident in August, a bundle of nuclear fuel rods tilted over during a refueling operation, delaying what was anticipated to be the first reopening of a shuttered nuclear reactor in the US. The NRC also said nine of the 15 reactor operators at Palisades failed the test for an initial licence examination administered in July and August. Six individuals were issued operator licenses. Holtec, which has never operated a nuclear power plant, has secured about $2.2 billion in government support for a project that has been touted as a symbol of US nuclear resurgence. The owners of two other nuclear plants slated for decommissioning—Three Mile Island in Pennsylvania and Duane Arnold in Iowa—announced restarts soon after Palisades began work. The Trump administration has promoted nuclear energy as an edge in the AI race against China. It has slashed regulation, provided tens of billions of dollars in funds, and promised to reverse a culture of “risk aversion” at the NRC to accelerate projects. The warning issued to Holtec by the NRC last week landed as investor interest in the nuclear sector has faded, driven by concerns the AI boom is slowing. More than $30 billion has been wiped off the value of five of the largest US-listed nuclear energy companies in the last year—Oklo, Centrus Energy, BWX Technologies, NuScale, and Nano Nuclear. Last month, Holtec was forced to postpone a planned initial public offering that was expected to raise $900 million and value the New Jersey-based company at up to $10 billion. Palisades first became operational in 1971. It was decommissioned in 2022 but received a $1.5 billion loan from the Department of Energy in 2024 to finance its restoration. “The situation at Palisades is that you have a power plant operator newbie in Holtec trying to restart a nuclear reactor, and they may be under a lot of financial and political pressure to get this done speedily,” said Allison Macfarlane, who served as NRC chair from 2012 to 2014. “The ‘chilled work environment’ warning issued by the NRC raises concerns that staff don’t feel like they can speak up to raise safety concerns and prevent accidents,” she added. “That perhaps reflects the new culture sweeping through part of the industry—move fast and break things. But to ensure safety, nuclear needs a culture based on going slowly and carefully.” Holtec faces a March 2027 contractual deadline to begin supplying power to customers, who secured an extra $1.3 billion in government funds to buy power from Palisades. In a statement to the FT, Holtec said it took the issues identified by the NRC seriously and was addressing them to ensure the plant runs safely. It said the fuel assembly that tilted over was successfully recovered without spilling in a “remarkable demonstration of technical ingenuity.” “A strong safety-conscious work environment is fundamental to our culture,” the company said. “The assertion that we have rushed has directly been countered by the work that has been accomplished.” The NRC told Holtec its concerns about workplace culture must be addressed promptly to prevent further “cultural decline” within the affected departments. NRC has asked Holtec to correct the problems and will review the company’s plan within 30 days. However, the NRC downplayed any immediate risk to the public, saying mitigating measures ensured plant safety was not compromised. Holtec said the worker who fell into the reactor cavity last year had sustained minor injuries from the fall but quickly returned to work. Holtec, which is trying to transform itself from a supplier of nuclear components and services into a reactor design and plant operator, has faced governance questions in the past. It has been the target of a whistleblower lawsuit and legal disputes involving former executives, relating to allegations of financial misrepresentation, executive retaliation, and fraudulent practices. Ukraine learns to fight without Starlink as Musk plays politics with satellites

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-23

Visit Original Website
Elon Musk’s Starlink satellite service has played a pivotal role in supporting Ukraine’s successful resistance to Russia’s full-scale invasion for years. But with Starlink having become increasingly unreliable due to Musk’s political inclinations and the latest surprise disruptions to service in Ukraine, the Ukrainians and Europe as a whole are now facing urgent questions about how to move forward without it. As the world’s largest communications satellite network, Starlink can typically support high-speed Internet and data streams for any user or vehicle equipped with a relatively compact Starlink terminal. This has enabled Ukraine to maintain vital communications for both civilian purposes and military operations—and has also expanded the Ukrainian military’s capability to remotely control or provide guidance to drones used in both air defense against Russian attacks and for striking back at Russian targets. “Starlink currently offers some of the best satellite coverage available, with virtually no competitors when you consider the combination of cost, availability, and ease of use,” according to the head of hardware R&D at the Ukrainian defense company BlueBird Tech, who provided comments to Ars anonymously for security reasons. “In practical terms, the main limitation is the need to position the antenna so it can receive signals from the satellites.” The US government and European countries such as Poland and Germany have paid SpaceX for tens of thousands of commercial Starlink terminals to be sent to Ukraine, as well as covering subscription fees. The US government has also given Ukraine’s military access to a military-grade version of Starlink known as Starshield, which Ukrainian defense companies can also test in their technologies in cooperation with the military, according to the BlueBird Tech representative. Starlink terminals have also been deployed on Ukrainian ground robots and drone boats operating in the contested Black Sea, in addition to supporting many different aerial drone operations on the battlefield. But even before the latest Starlink disruptions began on October 13, Ukrainian companies and their Western partners had already begun deploying more drones that reduce or eliminate communications reliance on Starlink. The Starlink service provided by Musk’s company SpaceX has proven so invaluable to modern battlefield operations that even Russian military units were using smuggled Starlink terminals—at least before SpaceX shut off access to any Starlink terminal within Ukraine not on a whitelist in February 2026. That shutoff severely disrupted Russian battlefield operations in the early part of the year. But access to Starlink has become a double-edged sword for Ukraine as well. Early warning signs arose in February 2023, when SpaceX President and COO Gwynne Shotwell confirmed reports that Musk’s company was taking steps to block Ukraine’s military from using Starlink with drones. That followed Musk’s social media comments about not allowing Starlink to be used for “long-range drone strikes.” The top-down Starlink restrictions returned on October 13, 2026, when Ukrainian government officials and military forces reported that the military-grade Starlink service was being throttled for terminals exceeding speeds of about 99 miles per hour (160 kilometers per hour). This latest restriction was imposed by SpaceX reportedly at Musk’s order and potentially in consultation with President Donald Trump. Musk and the Trump administration have both denied responsibility while pointing fingers at each other. In practice, the throttling of Starlink in Ukraine has made the satellite service unstable at even lower speeds like 80 miles per hour (130 kilometers per hour), said Michael Kofman, a senior fellow in the Russia and Eurasia Program at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, in a social media post on October 19. Such throttling reportedly disrupted multiple Ukrainian drone missions when it began without warning in the middle of the night. Ukrainian officials and advisers have described multiple impacts on drones that supply frontline units, reconnaissance missions, and mid-range drone strike missions that attack Russian military units and supply lines in Russian-occupied Ukrainian territory. The new Starlink speed limits have even reportedly interfered with Ukraine’s air defenses that use fast-moving drone interceptors to take down Russian one-way attack drones, according to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. The Starlink disruption coincided with some of Russia’s largest drone and missile attacks yet on major Ukrainian cities, with Russia having stepped up targeting of Ukraine’s power grid, data centers and even blocks of residential apartment buildings. Despite the value of Starlink, Ukraine’s military and defense companies have already been figuring out ways to fight on without the satellite service. This is in part because Russian forces have done their best to interfere with Starlink signals on the battlefield, including recently deploying cumbersome jamming systems to create zones of disrupted service within the Russian-occupied parts of Ukraine. “We don’t tie our products to any single technology, and we don’t manufacture systems that are critically dependent on Starlink,” the BlueBird Tech representative told Ars. “We’ve long been aware of the risks of Starlink jamming along the front line, which is why we’ve avoided using it as the sole means of control and telemetry.” BlueBird Tech has integrated Starlink terminals in several of its ground-based and airborne systems, but its representative described this as a “small percentage” of its overall product lineup. For example, the company’s Zhakh FPV drones are among the many Ukrainian and Russian drones that can maintain physical communication links with human operators by unspooling a long fiber-optic cable—making them impervious to electronic warfare countermeasures. The Ukrainian company produces multiple types of drones for reconnaissance and strike missions, along with a ground robot, drone detection systems, and electronic warfare systems. It has also begun developing an air-defense interceptor drone. There are still “certain product configurations for which no direct replacement for Starlink currently exists,” because the satellite service’s “technical capabilities and accessibility make it difficult to substitute in some applications,” the BlueBird Tech representative said. But they also described the company as “actively exploring ways to add redundancy and potentially replace these components in the future.” Some of the most promising wireless approaches include using ground-based LTE or 5G wireless networks or even turning to laser-based optical communication. BlueBird Tech is also examining radio-based mesh networks that allow ground transmitters or even radio-equipped drones to act as relay nodes within a larger decentralized communications system. This approach has been increasingly implemented by Russian drone strike missions on Ukrainian cities, as detailed by The Kyiv Independent. Another Ukrainian defense company, Buntar Aerospace, described having always accounted for the possibility that a communications service like Starlink could become available because of battlefield conditions. “The recent Starlink and Starshield disruptions did not affect our operations because we had prepared for this scenario in advance,” said Bohdan Sas, cofounder of Buntar Aerospace, in an email to Ars. “Losing access to one communications service does not necessarily mean losing the ability to conduct a mission.” Buntar Aerospace has provided Ukrainian military forces with fixed-wing reconnaissance drones that can fly up to 62 miles (100 kilometers). The drone’s operations benefit from AI-driven software called Buntar Copilot that can supposedly adapt flight plans for factors such as weather, signal quality or interference, and battery life. The company describes each data stream from its Buntar-3 drone—such as flight sensor data or its camera video stream—as being transmitted over separate channels to reduce the impact of one dropped stream. Although the drone incorporates a Starlink terminal, it also has alternative communication methods in case any service is disrupted. “Commercial communications services can provide valuable capabilities, but defense systems must account for the possibility that those services may become unavailable,” Sas told Ars. Western companies providing drone technologies to Ukraine have also incorporated similar battlefield lessons to reduce reliance on Starlink. For example, German defense company Quantum Systems described its Vector reconnaissance drones deployed in Ukraine as able to communicate with ground control stations via encrypted radio links. It also has “onboard AI and alternative navigation technologies” to perform missions despite GPS jamming or electronic warfare measures. The SPYS drone interceptor—developed by the company’s German-Ukrainian joint venture Quantum WIY Industries—also uses a ground-based beacon system to navigate without GPS and has AI-based target recognition capabilities coupled with heat-seeking sensors to home in on enemy drones. This particular interceptor does not use Starlink at all. “As a global neo-prime with European roots, we want Europe to remain fully capable of operating alongside our allies while retaining the ability to act independently when necessary,” said Paul Strobel, a spokesperson for German defense company Quantum Systems, in an email to Ars. “This means Europe must be able to develop, operate and maintain critical technologies, data and capabilities independently.” The German government has provided funding to supply tens of thousands of these interceptor drones to Ukraine in 2026 and 2027. Given their governments’ funding for Starlink service in Ukraine, German and Polish leaders have demanded that the Starlink restrictions be removed and asked pointed questions about whether Musk is “playing politics.” But Europe has also been accelerating development of its own sovereign alternatives to Starlink that may prove useful for Ukraine in the future. The Ukrainians already use Eutelsat’s OneWeb satellite service as a main alternative for some communications, but Eutelsat has just over 600 satellites in orbit compared to Starlink’s 10,000 satellites. This means it cannot support nearly as much data streaming or as many customers for Ukraine or other regions. The OneWeb terminals are also significantly bigger than Starlink terminals while costing about 45 times more than the latter, The Economist reported. Caleb Henry, author of a book about the history of OneWeb, previously told Ars that the bulkier OneWeb terminals complicated Ukrainian efforts to mount them on drones. The European Union has already spent six years planning to launch the IRIS² constellation of 348 communications satellites starting in 2029, although that is again a mere fraction of the Starlink constellation’s size. European Union leaders have also been seeking to speed up antitrust approval for a satellite venture called Project Bromo, which could create a large European company to rival SpaceX, Bloomberg News reported. Separately, Germany plans to build its own communications satellite constellation, SATCOMBw 4, for its own military and NATO. This constellation aims to start with 50 operational satellites by 2029 before expanding to hundreds, according to Euronews. Just weeks before the latest Starlink disruptions in Ukraine, Finland’s satellite company ICEYE and communications technology firm Nokia also announced plans to build a communications satellite constellation for Europe, according to Bloomberg News. The companies expect to launch up to 50 low-Earth orbit satellites annually from 2028 onward. But as with Starlink, enemy countermeasures could also disrupt signals from these communications satellite services on modern battlefields. So Ukrainian defense companies and the military will likely keep backup systems and avoid overreliance on satellite signals whenever possible. “The main lesson is that communications resilience is part of military autonomy,” said Sas at Buntar Aerospace. “Defense technologies must be designed around the realities of the battlefield, where connectivity is never guaranteed.”

Review: Crystal Lake suffers a bit from its obsession with Pam Voorhees

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-23

Visit Original Website
Peacock series Crystal Lake doesn’t feel like a Friday the 13th movie when you first press play, even though it’s set up to be a prequel to the original film. (This review includes spoilers of the original Friday the 13th movie but avoids major Crystal Lake spoilers.) Instead, Crystal Lake strives to build a drama that’s half homage, half uncharted territory, while giving a long-awaited backstory to Pamela Voorhees. The original villain in the first film, Pam Voorhees, shocked audiences in 1980 by popping up out of nowhere in the midst of a brutal camp counselor killing spree, then confronting the final girl with a Norman Bates-like delivery of the movie’s chilling tagline, “Kill her, mommy,” using the voice of her drowned son, Jason. Although the hockey-masked killer, Jason Voorhees, became the horror icon at the center of the franchise, his mom, Pam, continued to fascinate fans, who knew very little about her. Beheaded in the first film after just 13 minutes onscreen, her spirit would occasionally appear in later films, but the original actor, Betsy Palmer, who portrayed Pam refused to return, largely because the character has no depth. Ars dug into news archives and found interviews where Palmer explained that the later films did nothing to expand the character, offering only “stupid” lines. There was no reason for the actor—who at the height of her fame dated James Dean and was often typecast as the sweet-faced girl-next-door—to stoop to stepping back into Pam’s shoes. She famously only agreed to do the first movie because her Mercedes broke down on the Connecticut Turnpike and she needed a new car. She worked 10 days to capture her scenes, got paid $10,000, bought a new car, and expected nobody to remember her performance. “I read that script and thought ‘What a piece of junk,’” Palmer said in 2005. Palmer often criticized the later movies for bringing back Jason’s mom but giving her nothing to do. So, while some die-hard Friday the 13th fans may find Crystal Lake slow and foreign in the first few episodes, it’s worth sticking around for the first stab at fulfilling Palmer’s dream of Pam being more than just a helicopter mom of a vengeful boy living at the bottom of a cursed lake. Crystal Lake showrunner Brad Caleb Kane—who previously produced HBO’s Welcome to Derry, which gave Pennywise from Stephen King’s IT a riveting backstory—took many liberties when approaching Pam’s backstory, including shifting the timeline of events. In the original movie, Jason dies in 1958, but Kane wanted to root the story in historical events that resonated with the themes from the movie, so he shifted Jason’s drowning to 1971–2. He told Daily Dead that it was more important to him to do a “character-rich,” “emotionally resonant” show. By having Jason conceived in the early 1960s, he could draw on actual experiments on mothers during that time and invent a corporate enemy whose experiments on Pam set her up to become the type of fiercely protective mother who would kill for her son. Talking to Esquire, he explained that “continuity is wonky, anyway” across the movies. And by shifting the timeline, he told the Daily Dead that he got to weave in a scientific “Douglas Sirk-meets-David Cronenberg” body horror episode that you’d never expect would come out of a Friday the 13th franchise installment. Without spoiling plot details, I’ll note this backstory injects much more tension into the moment that Jason drowns at the camp. It also makes Pam’s desperation to believe that Jason’s still alive—and needs her to kill counselors for his protection—much more relatable. In interviews, Kane confirmed he wanted to add context to key moments in the original movie without feeling bound to perfectly tie in the series. In interviews, Palmer confirmed that she invented her own backstory for Pam that went back to conception, too. In her version, Pam gets pregnant in high school and decides to have him anyway, which is why she’s so protective of Jason. In 1982, Palmer said that she hoped through her intense acting that viewers would sense that there was more good in Pam than viewers knew, saying, “I can never play a part where I think there’s nothing good about the person at all. You hope that acting can explain why people do some of the things that they do—to show some truth.” Kane told Daily Dead that when he spoke to Linda Cardellini about portraying Pam in Crystal Lake, she agreed that the character must be played in “protective mama bear mode” because “it all goes back to Jason, her love for Jason, and her dedication to Jason.” For Friday the 13th fans who early on might feel impatient for Crystal Lake to stop talking about evil corporations and self-help gurus and get back to the camp slayings they came for, don’t fret, you won’t have to wait that long. By the third episode, murderous Jason begins to more prominently feature in episodes, and the gory deaths begin, as a swarm of innocent hippies ignore warnings and are drawn into the camp, like lambs to the slaughter. This is the turning point where the TV series starts to feel more familiar and picks up the pace measurably. Although the hockey mask never appears, Kane lovingly re-creates exact scenes from the original movie to offer flashes of eye candy to Jason fanatics. There are Easter eggs all over, and the series offers a good excuse to rewatch the original movie to catch even more and compare the gorgeous haunting lake views. Kane is hoping that the series, which finally shows the horrifying but oddly relatable depth of this unshakeable connection between mother and son, will be a hit so it can possibly run for multiple seasons, he told Esquire. When pitching the series to A24, Kane knew he wanted to do his own thing, while maintaining that “what was sacred to me is that a certain segment of the fans feel like I was doing a loving homage to the movies,” he told Daily Dead. On this mission, the series weaves in town folk names and lines from the original movie, as well as, of course, bringing back Pam Voorhees’ iconic cable-knit sweater. The series also seems to nod to Palmer’s past in making Cardellini’s Pam a well-known singer who styles her hair similarly to how young Palmer did in her starlet days. Growing up the kind of kid who read novelizations of horror movies, Kane also made sure that the special effects makeup is next-level. He worked with “very detail-oriented” artists who leave little to the imagination, as hippie numbers dwindle, eyes are gouged, and mother and son “go full Friday the 13th” by the series end.

PLD Space says its Miura 5 rocket, due to undertake its inaugural launch next year, is nearing final integration, Aviation Week reports. The rocket is one of three Miura 5s the company has already manufactured, with the other two designed to qualify key system elements, CEO Raúl Torres said in an October 19 program update. PLD Space now targets a first flight of the small-lift vehicle before mid-2027. Fully integrated stage … “Manufacturing these three vehicles has allowed our teams to work continuously across different stages of production and integration, applying the lessons learned from each qualification campaign directly to the launch vehicle,” Torres said. One of three prominent Canadian launch startups, Reaction Dynamics, said it is seeking to raise $350 million to grow its staff from 68 to more than 350 people, spaceQ reports. The company is developing the small-lift Aurora rocket, which Reaction Dynamics hopes will reach orbit in 2028. Reaction Dynamics also announced plans to build a medium-lift rocket, Borealis, for a debut by 2031. You’ve got the aurora, and the borealis … With a lift capacity of 6 tons to low-Earth orbit, Borealis would carry a little under half of the advertised payload capacity of Rocket Lab’s Neutron vehicle. “We founded Reaction Dynamics because we believe Canada should be able to reach orbit on its own terms,” the company’s founder and chief executive, Bachar Elzein, said. “Aurora is how we get to orbit in 2028. Japan launched a $578 million mission Monday on a five-year expedition to land on the largest moon of Mars and retrieve samples for return to Earth, Ars reports. The robotic Martian Moons eXploration mission, known as MMX, lifted off at 3:41 pm EDT Monday (4:41 am Japan Standard Time Tuesday) from Tanegashima Space Center in southern Japan. In September, a Chinese rocket took off from a remote spaceport in the Gobi Desert, released 10 satellites into orbit, then reignited its engine and put itself on a trajectory to rain down debris over the American Midwest, Ars reports. The rocket’s flight on September 15 lasted between 75 and 80 minutes, and it came down without completing a full orbit of the Earth. Social media posts at the time showed streaks of fiery debris falling from the sky, heading north over Kansas toward Minnesota. The overnight spectacle was also visible on the western horizon from Wisconsin. A surprising find … The exact time, location, and direction of the debris allowed experts to connect it to the launch of a Zhuque-2E rocket, a medium-lift vehicle built by a Chinese company called LandSpace. The rocket had launched from Dongfeng, a commercial launch zone co-located with the military-run Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center in northwestern China, less than two hours before. The mood at the Vivek Ramaswamy rally was upbeat—confident, even—but the cracks started to show before it began. The town hall in the small Cleveland suburb of Parma was entitled “Pierogis, Pabst, and Patriots,” and there were plenty of all three on hand at the Union House Bar and Restaurant. But we weren’t really supposed to be here. We were supposed to have convened at the Cleveland Harley-Davidson outpost for a “Christians, Bikers & Patriots” campaign event, before a sudden cancellation necessitated a shift to this woodsy sports joint. There was no word on what had gone wrong with the bikers. Still, the energy was real in this crimson-tinged bar, bustling with elderly Ohioans in OSU jerseys and MAGA merch alongside watery tubs of PBR. (Among the T-shirts: a red-ink outline of Charlie Kirk raising his fist, and a Trump-Vance shirt where the year was crossed out and replaced, in Sharpie, with 2028.) Alfred, a 60-year-old Filipino immigrant who was raised in Shaker Heights and works in the Parma public school system, told me he didn’t think, whatever the national narrative, that too many of his fellow conservatives were disaffected with Donald Trump or the current Republican Party. He loved the way Immigration and Customs Enforcement was operating, and anyway, as devout Catholics, he and his wife would never vote for a pro-choice Democrat like Amy Acton—Ramaswamy’s opponent in the bitter race for the Ohio governor’s mansion. Others I spoke to were similarly unbothered. Mark, a retired Air Force technician sitting at the bar, was planning on attending church that very evening and claimed to be far more concerned about property taxes than Ramaswamy’s Hinduism, one of the many issues that may work against the candidate in this very Christian state. To him, Ramaswamy’s religious objectors were just “morons who say stupid shit.” Once the actual event kicked off, however, even the speakers couldn’t help but strike notes of concern. “If you choose not to decide in this election, you are supporting a cast of characters that wants to take our state in a fundamentally dangerous direction,” said Secretary of State Frank LaRosa, invoking Rush’s “Freewill” as he opened for the guest of honor and promoted his own campaign for state auditor. “The Democrats are feelin’ chippy,” he warned. “But there will be no blue wave. There will be a bright-red Ramaswamy tsunami!” Ramaswamy finally took the stump to raucous applause and harped on his finely honed campaign planks—improving public schools, reducing property taxes and everyday costs, welcoming legal immigrants—while firing some culture-war drive-bys at the evils of “wokeism” and “transgenderism.” Former Ohio Treasurer Josh Mandel subsequently took the mic and felt the need to emphasize Ramaswamy’s devotion to God. “I’ve been with Vivek at churches all over the state. There’s this band of Christian warriors who are following and supporting Vivek because he supports them,” Mandel stressed. The sentiment proved to be a bellwether for the audience Q&A. An answer Ramaswamy gave condemning sanctuary cities wasn’t enough for a hoarse-voiced attendee who repeatedly interrupted: “Damned immigrants are hurtin’ everybody! We gotta get all of ’em out of here!” Ramaswamy stumbled for a moment, responding that legal immigrants were still cool, which failed to satisfy the heckler. “All of ’em!” the attendee yelled. “Send in the military!” Not long after, a separate dissident followed up, skipping over the queue to ask, twice, “Do you believe Jesus is God?” “Sir, we’re going to ask you to be respectful,” Ramaswamy replied. “It’s a simple question!” the old man yelled back. “We don’t reward those who are just screaming for attention,” Ramaswamy replied, turning away from the troublemaker. The supportive crowd handed another microphone to a Republican-voting teacher who’d earlier objected to one of Ramaswamy’s screeds about the current state of education in the state, and asked a question about his commitment to ensuring that all Ohio kids, including those with disabilities, learn to read. Here, despite the criticism, the candidate sounded a warmer note. “We’ll be respectful to anyone who has a tough question,” Ramaswamy declared. “If I can’t stand up here and answer a tough question, then I have no business running the state.” The problem for him is that there are nothing but tough questions, and he may not have good answers for many of them. If you believe the polls, it seems clear that Ramaswamy—a Cincinnati-born disciple of the MAGA political school, biotech entrepreneur, and one-time Republican presidential candidate—could be the first Ohio Republican to lose a gubernatorial bid in 20 years. The once swingy Buckeye State has only reddened further since Donald Trump flipped it in his first presidential bid, and the MAGA movement’s heir apparent, Vice President J.D. Vance, famously hails from here. Yet this could be the state where Ramaswamy, who once pitched himself as a younger and more capable Trump, ends up buckling to Acton, a doctor and first-time candidate who led Ohio’s Department of Health in the early days of its COVID response. There are a lot of reasons for this: He’s a pro–data center billionaire running for office at a moment when even Republican super PAC ads are pillorying billionaires and data centers. His Harvard-and-Yale pedigree and debate-me-bro verbosity make him come off more like a snob than a man of the people. He gets heckled a lot, even amid ostensibly friendly crowds. The social media posts where he does try to present himself as down-to-earth are frequently subjected to viral mockery. He doesn’t seem to be able to activate Republicans in a year when Buckeye Dems are actually motivated to hit the polls. The messages from his campaign, and from his well-funded supporters, have taken some incredibly vicious tacks—including, most recently and notoriously, an ad featuring the support of Acton’s estranged mother, who stayed married to a man whom the Democrat accused of sexually abusing her as a child. And then, most obviously of all: Ramaswamy is a dark-skinned Hindu with a lengthy (and self-admittedly “funny-sounding”) last name who’s vying to be the standard-bearer of a party whose members increasingly present themselves as anti-Indian, anti-Hindu warriors for the Christian white identity project. In Ohio, I was trying to surmise just how much Ramaswamy’s ethnicity and religion were helping usher in his political demise in this GOP. What I saw surprised me. There were scenes like the one at the rally, where some Republicans’ anti-immigrant, pro–Christian America rhetoric played out more familiarly. But among Ramaswamy’s fellow South Asians, a demographic that votes broadly Democratic but has drifted a touch rightward in recent cycles, there is also widespread doubt and suspicion—inconvenient for a campaign that needs every vote it can find. The community ties and social connections that Ramaswamy and his family have established across Cincinnati and Columbus could be helpful, in theory. But for many of his would-be constituents, the question is what those connections mean when the candidate has embraced a movement that sees them as the enemy. If you want to understand why Ramaswamy seems on course for a historic crashout in Ohio, those voters are a good place to start. In 2024, Amjad Baig, a real estate agent based in the Columbus suburbs, joined an unusual coalition of Americans in making a counterintuitive choice: He voted for Donald Trump. Baig, who supported Hillary Clinton in 2016 and organized a Pakistanis for Biden group in 2020, considers himself a fervent patriot. After emigrating from Lahore to the United States in the early 2000s and settling in the Ohio town of Dublin, the 54-year-old father of four got involved with its burgeoning South Asian blocs, eventually becoming president of the local Community Association of Pakistani Americans, and partnering frequently with the Ohio branch of the Federation of Indian Associations. I met him in September for dinner at Neehee’s, a regional Indian chain, where he recounted his Trump-era political journey. “Joe Biden should have resigned earlier. Six months before the election, they decided Kamala Harris would run as a candidate. There was no primary,” he said. “We could have had a better candidate. So I encouraged people in my network to vote against Harris.” They weren’t hard to persuade, Baig added: Many South Asian Ohioans were already angered by the Biden administration’s indifference toward the atrocities in Gaza, and partial to Trump’s publicly professed skepticism of Middle Eastern wars. That’s not quite how things have turned out two years later. Israel is still bombing Gaza, despite the ostensible ceasefire with Hamas; the war in Iran and its price shocks continue, hitting everything from fuel to food; Trump 2.0 is persecuting immigrants far more thoroughly, and aggressively, than even in his first term. The normally independent-minded South Asians across Ohio’s cities, Baig told me, are so furious that they’re again ready to vote against an Indian American candidate, just to hold Trump to account, even though many of them liked Ramaswamy when he first emerged on the scene. This western stretch of the Columbus metropolitan area, the fastest-growing and most economically vibrant region in the Buckeye State, is not coincidentally home to its largest South Asian populations. Twenty years ago, as Baig related to me, the Noor Islamic Cultural Center had opened its doors in Dublin, welcoming the burgeoning South Asian American groups who had settled in and around the state’s capital. About a 15-minute drive away stands Ohio’s oldest Hindu temple, founded by white Hare Krishna disciples, now home to hundreds of worshippers of Indian origin. The Noor’s opening similarly attracted an influx of Ohioans with family roots in Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Afghanistan. It’s a collective of freethinking, independent, educated constituents who, across their respective backgrounds and countries of origin, have a lot of thoughts about the fact that one of their own is running to become governor of their state. He was distressed about quite a few matters: the economic burdens facing Ohio’s small-business owners, national and local restrictions on the voting process, state-level efforts undermining the local hemp industry. Vivek is brown, and we are all citizens with the same rights, but he doesn’t care about that,” she said. “I’ve met with Amy. She listens to us. She feels our pain. And I don’t think these Republicans understand, they’re losing a large chunk of votes the way they’re going.” Aziz Taqdeer, an Afghan immigrant and social worker who’s lived in the United States since 2015, told me he’s hitting the polls for the first time ever because of his concerns around the Trump administration’s immigration crackdowns. “Over the past three years, it’s become impossible to get by. I’ve never been more worried,” Taqdeer said. “I know people who’ve been detained by ICE, and it does something horrible to your mental health, to know these agents can come for you at any time.” Also weighing on Taqdeer’s mind: inflation, his children, the driver who yelled at him the other day to “go back to your country,” and the ongoing betrayal of the many Afghans he knew who assisted U.S. forces against the Taliban. Across differences in politics and identity, South Asians I spoke with around Columbus also shared the same affordability concerns acutely felt by Americans everywhere. It’s making things harder for all us.” Rajnikanth Katte, a Hindu Indian immigrant who serves as chairman of the Federation of Indian Associations in Ohio, agreed with this diagnosis and suspected that Democrats would win, even as he proudly displayed to me a photo of himself standing with LaRose, the secretary of state, at a recent federation event. “So many people are struggling and hurting right now,” Katte said. “I’m always in favor of whoever can help us make incremental improvements—whoever it is, whether that’s a Democrat or a Republican, I’ll support them.” This will be the first election in Ohio’s history in which neither of the major-party gubernatorial candidates is Christian. Over the past few months, an upstart political polling firm kept delivering good news for Democrats—and especially those on the left. Across more than a dozen surveys, it found progressives leading in key primary races and showed other Democrats in striking distance of winning traditionally Republican states. Except, it turns out, the surveys were fake. The deception, perpetrated by an organization called Wedgewood Polls, doubles as a cautionary tale about how people who follow politics should think about polls—not just fake polls, but also ones that are on the level. A data-journalism outlet called FiftyPlusOne contacted Wedgewood Polls this week with some troubling allegations: The firm, which claimed to have started conducting polls over the summer and didn’t list any employees on its website, appeared to have manipulated survey data to produce fake results. The details of the deception are a little in the weeds, but Wedgewood ultimately came clean. In a public statement on Tuesday, the firm described itself as a “fictional entity” that had deliberately published “fabricated numbers.” It then deleted its social media accounts and website. Yikes. But why would someone want to fake poll results? Wedgewood claims it did so as part of “an experimental project to demonstrate the vulnerabilities, lack of oversight, and overall low barrier to entry in the modern political polling industry.” Yet Wedgewood also lied to media outlets that did ask probing questions about its methods, so that explanation is hard to take at face value. Money is one potential motive. Wedgewood appears to have charged clients to conduct surveys on their behalf, a standard practice for aboveboard polling firms that could in this case amount to fraud. We still aren’t 100 percent clear on who the people behind Wedgewood are, but they may have wanted to make money by manipulating betting markets on the races they claimed to survey. Wedgewood claims its deception “was never intended to sway an election or permanently mislead the public,” but it may also have wanted to make certain Democrats look more electable—either to boost progressives or get Democrats to spend valuable resources in red states. Did Wedgewood’s surveys affect those races? Polls can influence who voters, donors, and party committees decide to support, and there’s some evidence that Wedgewood’s did. Mallory McMorrow, a Michigan Democrat whose U.S. Senate campaign flopped, says a Wedgewood survey showing her support cratering helped throttle donations and increased calls for her to drop out. “It killed us,” she wrote this week. On the other side of that coin is Francesca Hong, a democratic socialist who nearly won the Democratic primary for Wisconsin governor. Before her narrow loss, Hong referenced a since-deleted Wedgewood poll in a fundraising pitch to argue that her campaign had “momentum.” And as Politico reports, Democrats stepped up funding to Scott Colom, who’s running in deep-red Mississippi, after a Wedgewood poll last week showed him within 4 points of Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith. OK, but how much impact can one fake polling firm really have? Actually, Wedgewood’s deception is part of a pattern. A group called Median Strategies also confessed to fabricating survey data earlier this year, similarly describing itself as a “social experiment examining how purported polling information could enter and spread through the political information ecosystem.” And on Thursday, FiftyPlusOne uncovered a third political polling firm that may have cooked some of its data. That firm, SoCal Strategies, has since deleted its website and social media accounts. So does this mean political polls are just made up?? That’s going a step too far. It’s true that political polling has fallen on hard times. Americans are increasingly less likely to answer surveyors’ cold calls, making high-quality polls more expensive. Major polling misses in recent elections have also caused some universities, survey firms, and media outlets to abandon political polling entirely. But does that mean most polls are fake? Emphatically no. Legitimate pollsters strive to be transparent about who’s conducting them, who’s funding them, and the methods they use. They’re also among the people most upset about what Wedgewood did. “People like the idiots behind Wedgewood, and, to a lesser extent, Median, make it harder for the rest of us who are absolutely busting our butts to do good public opinion research,” the polling expert Natalie Jackson wrote on Substack today. But if that’s true, does this Wedgewood incident really matter? What’s the bigger picture? Unless you’re some kind of oracle, election outcomes are unknowable in advance. In that sea of uncertainty, it’s natural to grab onto any shred of data that seems to offer clues, and polls have become an outsize part of how many Americans think about politics. It’s also becoming more common for campaigns to blast out poll results—sometimes of questionable provenance—on social media or in fundraising emails. Some of that is understandable. When it comes to predicting elections, polls remain much better than person-on-the-street interviews, lawn signs, or crowd sizes at rallies. But polls have always been imperfect, their accuracy subject to reaching a representative sample, assumptions about who will actually vote, and mathematical laws of probability. None of that justifies Wedgewood’s deception, and it’s a frustrating reality that the stakes of American politics have gotten higher even as the availability of high-quality polling data has declined. But the next time you see a polling result that seems too good to be true, just remember: As the cliché goes, the only poll that really matters is on Election Day. In an unsigned order on Wednesday, the Supreme Court denied the Trump administration’s request for permission to implement what is, even by the Trump administration’s standards, a remarkably barbaric element of its anti-trans agenda: a blanket denial of gender-affirming healthcare to transgender people in federal prison for the first time in three decades. Only Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito noted their dissent, because for them, nothing is more important than their solemn duty to give their favorite president whatever he wants. Shortly after taking office in January 2025, President Donald Trump issued a sweeping anti-trans executive order aimed at purging the government of “gender ideology.” This case, Trump v. Kingdom, is a legal challenge to guidance issued by the Bureau of Prisons that would effectuate the part of Trump’s order forbidding the use of federal funds for “any medical procedure, treatment, or drug for the purpose of conforming an inmate’s appearance to that of the opposite sex.” Needless to say, losing access to care was devastating for incarcerated transgender people, who in court filings described experiencing anxiety, panic attacks, and suicidal ideation as a result of their suddenly untreated gender dysphoria. The policy of providing these inmates with the medical care they need has been in place since the first Bush administration. Fortunately for anyone who believes that the state should not have the power to torture people in its custody, lower-court judges have repeatedly blocked the new guidance from taking effect. By electing to leave these orders in place, the Supreme Court indicated that, although it has been willing to sympathize with the anti-trans movement in many other contexts, sanctioning the denial of lifesaving care to incarcerated people is, at least for now, a proverbial bridge too far. By putting their disagreement on the record, Thomas and Alito are making clear that they are as eager as ever to drag the court across it. The justices do not always make their votes public in shadow docket cases, which means it is possible that one or two more justices sided with Trump here and simply chose not to say so. But through their conduct in Kingdom and other cases like it, Thomas and Alito continue to distinguish themselves as the court’s two most strident reactionaries, reflexively endorsing whatever harebrained legal theories conservative lawyers offer in service of their shared policy agenda. Whenever the other conservative justices decide that a given argument is too extreme or too ambitious, these two are right there to explain why the argument is actually correct, and to encourage activists to try again soon. Thomas and Alito have long been on the right flank of the court’s right flank: consistent opponents of abortion rights, voting rights, gun safety, and so on, and outspoken proponents of incarceration, punishment, religious supremacy, and the like. During the court’s most recent term, they voted together in 94 percent of cases on the merits docket. In their occasional speeches and public appearances, they come off as pretty generic Republican men of a certain age, airing all the usual grievances about the perils of cancel culture, the evils of progressivism, and anyone who would have the temerity to question their integrity in public. What distinguishes Thomas and Alito from the other conservatives, though, is how frequently they end up castigating their more junior colleagues for their timidity. In April 2025, for example, when the court blocked the Trump administration from deporting hundreds of people without notice to a torture prison in a country that wasn’t even home, Alito, joined by Thomas, wrote a dissent blasting the court for “hastily and prematurely” granting “legally questionable” relief “literally in the middle of the night.” In May 2026, they were the only justices who dissented from the court’s stay of a lower-court order that would have severely limited the availability of abortion medication. Interesting new project from the minds behind SparkyLinux SparkyOS is a novel distro from the folks behind SparkyLinux: it's a version of Arch that can you can use without installing it at all. SparkyOS is a new distro from the team behind the Debian-based SparkyLinux, but it's quite different. SparkyOS is based on Arch, and it runs entirely from USB. It's not a conventional live medium – this is a full local install, but shipped as an image of a USB key that you can boot and run. It comes with the Xfce desktop and an array of apps. The idea is that you can boot and run it on any x86-64 UEFI computer, entirely from a USB key, without touching the hard disk at all. This is quite different from its forerunner, which is a relatively conventional Debian-based distro, one which The Register has mentioned a few times over the years: first back in 2015 in a lightweight distro roundup, and more recently in 2024 when we praised its array of desktop environments. Earlier this year, we noted that the latest SparkyLinux featured a complete 32-bit edition, even though its parent distro has dropped its 32-bit x86 kernel. This is also not your typical live distro. Unless you're running on some Arm computer with nonstandard firmware, most Linux distros install from an image written to a USB pendrive, the same way that they did decades ago: in the noughties when they installed from a DVD, and back in the 20th century, when it was a CD instead. Most optical media use some form of the ISO standard 9660 filesystem, which is quite limited – despite multiple extensions, the core is a basic structure comparable to the ancient MS-DOS FAT. That's not much use for Linux, which needs long filenames, permissions, and metadata, plus things like symbolic links. So, to this day, most live Linux media have a few boot files and the OS itself is kept inside a big compressed file called a SquashFS, inside which is a real Linux filesystem. However, because it's inside something akin to a Zip file, it's read-only. You can't update the OS or add new software. Some live media optionally offer persistence, which stores changes in another separate file. That works, but if you update the OS, all the original pre-update files remain untouched, and new ones are stored in the persistence structure in addition to the space still consumed by the originals. So, it's slow, and it's very inefficient; it takes substantially more space than a conventional disk install. So, although there are dozens, maybe hundreds, of live Linux media images out there, most fall into one of two types of single-purpose product: either stripped-out special-purpose distros, such as SystemRescue, GParted Live, and Grml, or Rescuezilla, or Clonezilla. Alternatively, it's a full OS, but only meant as a sampler, not for daily use, with limited and inefficient tools for updates and changes. We're not sure if twice counts as a FAQ, but there are questions on StackExchange on how to install Ubuntu to USB as well as how to install Debian to USB. SparkyOS is an answer to this question. It's a complete, pre-configured installation of Arch, directly onto a USB key. That does impose some limits, so for instance, like the Desktop Classic System we looked at in January, it only includes UEFI boot files. You can't use it on a computer with just a "legacy" BIOS, and it requires format conversion to use it in a VM. The current SparkyOS version 2026.10 is the first release, but there are already some How-to documentation pages covering things like expanding the filesystem to fill the USB medium, changing its settings, and how to crowbar it into a VM. The next competitive advantage in enterprise AI has less to do with which model you choose than with what you build around it. That’s an important shift. For the past several years, much of the AI conversation has centered on model capability: bigger models, better reasoning, larger context windows, higher benchmark scores. Those advances have been extraordinary. But as AI moves from experimentation to production, the question facing enterprise leaders has changed—it’s no longer, “what can the model do?”—it’s now, “Can we deliver that intelligence reliably, economically, and at scale?” Increasingly, the answer depends on the serving harness—the engineering system surrounding the model, including the architecture, memory management, scheduling, and optimization decisions that determine how efficiently model intelligence becomes a production capability. For technical executives, that changes where AI investment should go—and what they should demand from the architecture supporting it. There is no universally “best” model or AI architecture. There is only the right solution for the business problem. A customer-facing assistant optimized for fast responses has fundamentally different requirements from a document-processing pipeline that can run overnight. An internal knowledge application and a high-volume customer service platform may use the same foundation model but require very different serving architectures. The workload defines the constraints. The constraints should determine the architecture. That sounds simple, but it runs counter to the way many organizations have approached early AI deployments. During a proof of concept, model accuracy naturally dominates the discussion. Production introduces constraints that are much easier to ignore when only a handful of users are involved: latency, concurrency, memory pressure, GPU utilization, infrastructure cost, and unpredictable traffic. A model that produces marginally better answers is not necessarily the better production choice if it doubles inference costs or cannot meet the application's latency requirements. Not every workload needs a Ferrari. Sometimes the smarter engineering decision is the model that delivers exactly the performance the business needs without paying for capability it doesn't. The objective should be to deploy the right intelligence at the right cost for the workload. That makes serving architecture more than an engineering concern. It becomes an investment decision: where to spend on compute, where to optimize, and where additional model capability simply isn't worth the cost. Once AI reaches production, seemingly isolated engineering decisions begin affecting one another. Serving architecture influences memory utilization. Memory management affects concurrency. Concurrency shapes batching and scheduling, and scheduling affects GPU utilization and latency. Only then can techniques such as quantization, speculative decoding, or optimized attention mechanisms deliver their full value. This interdependence matters because organizations can spend considerably more on infrastructure without solving the constraint limiting performance. GPU memory is a good example. During inference, the model's weights remain fixed, but the key-value (KV) cache grows with active requests and context length. As concurrency increases, a system can run short of usable GPU memory even when compute capacity is still available. Adding more compute without understanding that bottleneck may simply make an inefficient system more expensive. The lesson for technology leaders is broader than memory management: Measure before you optimize. Identify the dominant constraint. Address it. Measure the result. Then determine if another bottleneck emerged. Repeat. Don't think of optimization techniques as Pokémon cards: The goal isn't to collect them all. The goal is to apply the technique that removes the highest-impact constraint while preserving the application's business requirements. For technology leaders, that demands a different governance question. Instead of asking which AI capabilities the organization can deploy, ask which constraints stand between a proven capability and sustainable business value. Perhaps the most important reason to invest in the serving harness is that today's leading model will probably not be tomorrow’s. Models will improve. Hardware will evolve. New optimization techniques will emerge. The architecture surrounding them should make those changes easier to absorb, not force the enterprise to rebuild its AI platform every time technology advances. That makes adaptability an increasingly important outcome of AI strategy. Organizations that build serving systems around workload requirements, clear service-level objectives, and measurable production economics gain the ability to evaluate new models and infrastructure against what the business actually needs. A model change can become an evaluation and configuration decision rather than an architectural reset. Enterprise AI can generate recommendations, automate tasks, and help organizations make decisions faster. As companies look to move AI from experimentation into everyday operations, the challenge ahead is giving technology the context to act usefully within complex business processes. For Devavrat Shah, chief scientist of enterprise AI at Celonis, that context is central to what he calls “decision intelligence.” AI agents can support individual tasks within larger decision-making processes, he argues, but they need to understand the role each task plays and the business conditions surrounding it. “You can't just do that in a vacuum,” he says.
