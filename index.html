Chainalysis says hackers stole credentials and tokens for all of its customers

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-06

Visit Original Website
Blockchain analysis firm Chainalysis said that hackers have obtained sensitive personal information—including names, usernames, password hashes, and live tokens—for all of its users following a breach of its network security. The accomplishment could be a major coup for the attackers. Both Chainalysis and public sources have revealed a long list of high-profile customers, including government agencies such as the FBI, US Immigration and Customs Enforcement agency, and the US Air Force, as well as private companies such as Microsoft, Coinbase, and Barclays. It’s unclear if the data theft exposed customers to additional threats. Last Friday, Chainalysis said it had been the target of a cybersecurity incident in October that was similar to the hacks of other platforms during the same month. The external actor used AI agent-assisted offensive techniques to gain unauthorized access to Chainalysis systems and internal tools. An email the company sent to affected customers said some “non-sensitive customer data, such as user IDs, full names, email addresses, and organization role labels for users provisioned on our platform were accessed.” It’s not clear why the company considered such information “non-sensitive.” In a Wednesday update, the company said that part of the “ongoing multifront cyberattack” involved an “on-path attack” that exposed a subset of customer passwords, and that the attack vector had been identified and fixed. Also known as an adversary-in-the-middle attack, on-path attacks happen when a hacker gains access to a piece of infrastructure sending traffic back and forth between two parties. Chainalysis has yet to say where the infrastructure compromise occurred and if the data obtained from it was TLS encrypted. The company said later that day that an investigation two days earlier (on Monday) had revealed that “certain customer information including names, usernames, password hashes and live tokens for all Chainalysis users were exposed to a malicious actor.” It also said it had detected “abnormal activity” and, in response, took down its login page and know-your-transaction service, which monitors threats to cryptocurrency holdings in real time. The company has also removed access to its Reactor and Data Solutions services. In an update on Friday, Chainalysis said it had begun phasing in customer access to KYT and Address Screening and expected more customers to follow. It asked customers to submit their trusted IP addresses so they can be added to an allow list and to prepare to reset credentials once they regain access. The credential rotation is mandatory. The company says its investigation has yet to reveal any evidence that any investigation data has been accessed or Chainalysis attribution data has been manipulated. Several important questions remain unanswered. As noted, the source that made the on-path attack possible is still unknown. It’s not clear how useful the information obtained in such an attack would be unless the attackers also had the ability to decrypt the traffic they were monitoring. If that were the case, the attackers might have gained broad control of the company’s infrastructure. Chainalysis also didn’t explain why it waited until Wednesday to disclose the data theft of all customers’ personal information when its investigation discovered the exposure two days earlier. Further, the company has provided no details about the AI agents used in the attack and how they worked. A Chainalysis representative said the company wasn’t commenting beyond the statements in its blog post.

Earth's churning mantle may have created the Great Unconformity

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-06

Visit Original Website
In the Grand Canyon, there is a line in the rock where time seems to have gone missing. Ancient crystalline rocks mark its bottom, while sandstones laid down as life started to diversify during the Cambrian explosion mark the top. “It’s called the Great Unconformity, and it’s a gap in the geologic record,” says Nicolas Flament, a geoscientist at the University of Wollongong, Australia. “This gap can be anywhere between 200 million years, which is not that impressive, to more than a billion years.” In a recent Nature study, Flament and his colleague Santosh Dhungana argue that this gap was carved with help from deep within the planet. According to their models, slow currents of hot rock in Earth’s mantle pushed entire continents up for hundreds of millions of years, letting glaciers grind them down and then let them sink, allowing fresh sediments to bury the scar. The largest hole in the rock record might be a fingerprint of processes happening deep in the planet’s interior. The oldest sediments resting on the Great Unconformity in North America are between 635 and 482 million years old. Geologists have traced it across most of North America and parts of northern Europe and northern China. “Something seems to have happened, in particular in North America,” Flament says. The leading explanation was glaciation. Between roughly 720 and 635 million years ago, glaciers reached the tropics, and some scientists think the entire planet froze over, a scenario called Snowball Earth. “Ice sheets are very good at grinding, scouring, scraping the surface,” Flament explains. He said that glaciers are about 10 times more effective than rivers at eroding rock away. But if the whole planet froze, the Great Unconformity should be found everywhere. And it’s not. Other explanations included rifting, pulling and stretching of Earth’s crust, and a global drop in sea levels. But rifting acts mostly on the edges of continents, and a sea level change should have affected all continents alike. “There’s no doubt that there was extensive glaciation. There’s no doubt that there have been sea-level changes throughout time. The question is, was that enough to put the Great Unconformity puzzle together?” Flament says. A fourth idea held that the mantle itself was moving continents up and down, a process called dynamic topography. “It had been proposed, but it hadn’t been quantitatively tested,” Flament says. So, his team set out to do so. From roughly 100 to 200 kilometers beneath the surface down to nearly 3,000 kilometers, Earth’s mantle slowly convects. “An analogy would be a lava lamp, where some stuff comes up and some stuff comes down,” Flament explains. “Cold rock sinks and hot rock rises.” The mantle is solid, yet a piece of rock can slowly travel from the surface to the deep Earth over some 200 million years. That motion tugs on the surface. Where cold slabs of ocean floor sink into the mantle at subduction zones, the ground gets pulled down; where hot mantle rises, it gets pushed up. The amplitude of this up and down motion is still debated, though. “The short of it is several hundreds of meters, and it’s still to be worked out exactly what that [precise value] is,” Flament says. Dhungana and Flament ran six simulations of mantle convection going back 1 billion years, driven by reconstructions of how tectonic plates moved over that time. Then they grouped regions where the ground rose and fell in similar ways to find where the unconformity should have formed, and where it should have survived to the present. Flament, however, says the starting configuration of these simulations remains uncertain. Magnetic records in ancient rocks reveal how far continents were from the equator, but not their longitude. “It’s possible that some continents may be in the wrong hemisphere,” Flament admits. The ancient ocean floor, long since recycled into the mantle, had to be invented entirely from scratch in the models. Still, the simulations produced a consistent story. Laurentia, the ancient core of North America, had little or no subduction along its margins between about 930 and 510 million years ago, much like Africa today, so it sat above some rising mantle and was pushed up. Five of the six models showed this pattern of Laurentia seeing long uplift followed by sinking in the Cambrian. For northern Europe and North China, this pattern showed up in four. But the uplift alone, Flament’s team concluded, was too gentle to strip away enough rock to create the Great Unconformity. “The uplift could create a tilt for the whole continent, but it’s not great at creating a steep slope,” Flament says. The missing piece of the puzzle, he argues, were glaciers that ground through rock even on low ground and gentle slopes. The models show the uplift was still building when Snowball Earth hit. “This gentle uplift that’s continuous, and then that glacial erosion that comes on top, work together,” Flament explains. “They’re not coupled. They just happen to coincide in time.” When the continents sank in the Cambrian, seas flooded in and buried the eroded surface. The models made by Flament’s team also explain why the gap is so obvious in North America but hard to find elsewhere. Compared with records of sediment preserved on each continent, North America and Eurasia show one long rise followed by one long fall, which the researchers interpret as the ideal recipe for the Great Unconformity. South America and Australia, on the other hand, went through two cycles. “This would form potentially two unconformities, and then the Great Unconformity would be more difficult to see,” Flament says. Africa was a different case altogether—hemmed in by mountain belts at the time, its erosion and sediment were shaped by the mountains rather than by the mantle beneath. But if the mantle goes through such up and down cycles on a regular basis, the remaining question was why we can see only one Great Unconformity, and not many. The team says the Great Unconformity is unique in the geological record because of a combination of a rise-and-fall cycle lasting over 900 million years, a near-global glaciation that happened at the right moment, and rock records that get harder to read farther back in time. Flament suspects there may be an older candidate, tied to a glaciation 2.4 billion years ago, but rocks that old lack the fossils that make younger sediments easier to date. “Having life in sediments makes it a lot easier to have a sense of time,” he says. His work, however, may help explain what happens right after the Great Unconformity formed. The intense erosion that carved the Great Unconformity washed rock material into the oceans, possibly fertilizing them just before the Cambrian explosion, when calcium became abundant in seawater. “That’s when life learns to mobilize calcium and create skeletons and shells,” Flament says. He admits, though, that mantle dynamics likely wasn’t the only process that enabled the Cambrian explosion. “I think it would be misleading to say the mantle set the pace here. But I think it was one of the forces that worked together.” Nature, 2026.  DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-11156-5

A shipment of Nvidia hardware valued at $11.4 million vanished in transport between Oakland and Texas over the summer, and more than two months later, police have announced no arrests or recovery of the cargo. As reported by The Mercury News, six pallets of unknown Nvidia equipment left Oakland on August 29 on a truck bound for Fort Worth, Texas. The hardware was reportedly picked up as scheduled by a truck bearing the logo of a food transportation company before vanishing off the grid. The theft wasn’t noticed until early September, when the buyers realized that the shipment had never been received. Police attempting to trace the truck’s route found it had made several multi-hour stops after picking up the hardware before losing the truck somewhere in San Bernardino County, California. Oakland police confirmed that there was a grand theft report filed on September 13 and that the investigation remains ongoing. Authorities have not said what Nvidia hardware was stolen or exactly how the theft was carried out. The theft in Oakland is the latest in a wave of increasingly bold hardware heists. As Ars’ Ashley Belanger reported recently, thieves have made off with more than $100 million in similar shipments since June. According to investigators cited by NBC News, the criminals have shown planning and sophistication by employing techniques like GPS location spoofing and even arranging collisions with police escort vehicles. Stolen components can be stripped down and sold in gray-market transactions or shipped overseas where access to US tech hardware may be restricted. Nvidia’s hardware has been a specific target. In 2025, police investigated burglaries of Nvidia hardware, valued at more than $7.5 million, around Fremont and Union City, California. As reported by The Mercury News, police believe the thefts were carried out by a group that obtained vehicles for the heist in Southern California before traveling north to the Bay Area. Professor Olaf Groth of UC Berkeley’s Haas School of Business told ABC News that “if a chip costs about $550,000, then they go for about $1 million or more abroad.” Those margins, he said, may be incentivizing criminals: “Because cargoes are getting bigger. The heists are getting more complex and that will point toward a professionalization of the teams that are hijacking these.” Not every would-be heist crew gets it right, though. A theft in September, reported by Wired, saw two trailers marked with Nvidia and PlusAI logos stolen from outside of a warehouse facility in California. The trailers were recovered by police nearby, presumably after the criminals cut the locks to reveal they were each loaded with 20,000 pounds of sand. PlusAI is a company working on autonomous trucking solutions with Nvidia, and the sand was in the trailers to simulate freight loads.

FAA to finally ditch floppy disks in what it calls "historic" tech upgrade

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-06

While you might be pondering whether to replace a smartphone with one that has a slightly better camera or a second screen, the Federal Aviation Administration is planning a much bigger leap in technology for the country’s air traffic control system. The FAA said yesterday that it will stop using floppy disks as part of a long-awaited upgrade that will give air traffic controllers faster access to data needed for crucial safety alerts. The new Enterprise Information Display System (E-IDS) will be installed in 435 facilities, replacing several information display systems dating to the 1980s. The FAA “is continuing its historic modernization of America’s air traffic control system by replacing outdated floppy disks that contain vital weather and other critical information with a streamlined, 21st-century system,” the agency said. E-IDS will be “deployed to operational control positions at more than 400 facilities across all air traffic control domains,” and “provide operationally essential information for controlling aircraft,” the FAA said. The change has been in the works for over six years. “No more floppy disks or paper strips,” then-Acting FAA Administrator Chris Rocheleau told the House Appropriations Committee last year. The FAA said E-IDS will replace older tools that have “technical end-of-life issues” and address two of the aviation agency’s top safety concerns: distribution of weather alerts to pilots (known as PIREP for Pilot Report); and timely distribution of Notices to Airmen (NOTAM), which include information on abnormal statuses and hazards. “E-IDS gives air traffic controllers the information they need to make timely decisions and keep the flying public safe,” FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford said. “We’re moving away from outdated, manual processes and replacing technology that once relied on floppy disks with a modern system that allows controllers to instantly share critical flight alerts, restricted airspace schedules and pilot reports across the National Airspace System.” It’ll take a year or so to make the upgrades at every facility. The first sites to get E-IDS will be Atlantic City International Airport, Nashville International Airport, Greater Rochester International Airport, and data processing hubs in Atlanta and Salt Lake City. The FAA said it plans to complete the upgrade at 435 sites by the end of 2028. “Good news America: floppy disks are out, 21st century tech is in,” US Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said. Duffy posted a video yesterday about the upgrade in which a newscaster describing the FAA’s outdated technology says, “the FAA systems are essentially running on Windows 95.” Like other Trump appointees, Duffy was quick to give credit to the president, although the plan was shepherded through by officials in both the Biden and Trump administrations. “The greatest nation in the world deserves a state-of-the-art air traffic control system, and under President Trump, we’re delivering just that,” Duffy said. In August 2020, the FAA awarded a contract to Leidos to design and develop E-IDS. Leidos said in a press release at the time that E-IDS is part of the FAA’s Next Generation Air Transportation System (NextGen) modernization project, which encompasses numerous upgrades going back to 2008. The FAA said E-IDS will replace the Information Display System 4 (IDS-4), Controller Equipment Information Display System (ACE-IDS), NAS Information Display System (NIDS), and En Route Information Display System (ERIDS). IDS-4, one of the systems being replaced, runs on MS-DOS and “has been in constant use since the early 1990s as a vital informational resource/tool for air traffic controllers,” according to a 2018 press release issued by Systems Atlanta, a vendor that had an FAA contract to support the system. As PCMag notes, IDS-4 appears to run on desktop PCs with 3.5-inch floppy disk drives. An April 2026 report by the Department of Transportation said, “Controllers have used a system that is driven by floppy disks and an antiquated operation system for critical information such as weather, NOTAMs and airport status.” With E-IDS, those controllers will have “a single platform that pulls that data into one view. Instead of searching for info, they [will] have immediate access to everything they need to make split-second decisions,” the report said. The out-of-date systems were criticized in a September 2026 report by the US Government Accountability Office (GAO). “Legacy display systems rely on obsolete technology (e.g., floppy disks) and interfaces from the 1980s, inefficient data organization, and manual update methods,” the report said. “The systems are cumbersome for controllers to search, retrieve, and display information. Multiple diverse display systems create inefficient activities needed to sustain these systems.” The GAO report said these information display systems are used in Air Route Traffic Control Centers, Terminal Radar Approach Control facilities, and control towers. The systems display textual and graphical information, “such as flight approach charts, combined with Notice to Airmen and weather information. Many Florida homeowners cheered on Nov. 3, 2026, when more than 60% of voters approved a constitutional amendment to significantly reduce property taxes. Under the measure, Amendment 3, a homeowner’s tax break known as a homestead exemption would go from the current US$51,400 to $150,000 in 2027 and increase again to $250,000 in 2028. By shielding more of a home’s assessed value from taxation, this exemption effectively lowers homeowners’ tax bills. New Florida residents who buy a home would also reportedly be eligible for a property tax break, but on a more phased-in basis. But the measure comes with a hefty price tag. The Florida Legislature’s research arm estimated the measure would reduce annual revenue from property taxes by $11.8 billion. This translates to a 30% reduction in revenue for Florida counties and a 13% cut for smaller municipal governments. While homeowners will get some relief, the budget crunch due to the lower tax revenue will affect other Floridians in more adverse ways. As economists who have been following the debates over Amendment 3, we argue that local governments will struggle to fund basic services with less money. And we expect more sweeping economic consequences as well – including less affordable residential housing and a broader shift of the tax burden away from relatively wealthier homeowners to those who are less well off. Much of the political momentum for Amendment 3 came from a rise in the cost of living and frustration over rising property taxes. These now average just over $3,000 annually but can run much higher for Floridians with more expensive homes. In addition to raising the homestead tax exemption, Amendment 3 lowers the cap on annual assessments on rental and commercial properties from a 10% increase to a 5% increase. Since property taxes are based on an asset’s assessed value, this language effectively limits but doesn’t rule out tax hikes for businesses. So landlords, for example, could still see meaningful annual tax increases as their properties go up in value – costs that would likely be passed on to renters. Amendment 3 makes clear that school-related property taxes are exempt from these tax cuts. It also lists some theoretical limits on how local governments should use tax dollars, as a way to ensure continued funding for essential services such as public safety, infrastructure or obligations for employee retirement benefits. But it also states that funds may be used for “expenditures approved” by local and county governments – ambiguous wording that suggests these stipulations can be breached. Florida property taxes are levied and collected by some 640 local government entities. There’s no state income tax, so any reductions in property tax revenue directly affect local and county governments. With tax revenue slashed, businesses, landlords and owners of other non-homestead properties like second homes would almost certainly see their taxes go up. This is a typical pattern: When governments cut property taxes for some groups, taxes shift to other groups. This dynamic is likely to play out with local governments as they try to keep revenue stable: Amendment 3 would force them to hike taxes in other areas to make up for the shortfall. This could include increases in state and local sales taxes, an expansion of the list of goods subject to sales taxes, or a hike in “documentary stamp taxes,” which are required on documents such as deeds, bonds and mortgages. State and local governments could also impose higher fees for specific services, such as solid waste treatment and stormwater utilities. Another option is to hike permits and inspection fees, fuel taxes or other service charges such as vehicle registration fees. Finally, local governments could raise tourism-related taxes, such as on food, beverages and hotels. But this recourse wouldn’t help counties with limited tourism, and such revenues fluctuate in any event. In the end, though, essential services would still almost certainly be affected. Funding for police, fire and emergency medical services is primarily the responsibility of local governments, and, for most counties, they make up the largest line item in their annual expenditures. Reducing property taxes would amount to a direct hit, especially in smaller, less-populated counties. And if local governments do raise sales tax rates to the extent they can, lower-income Floridians will bear a disproportionate burden as they spend much more of their income on consumption. They’re also far more likely to rent than own a home, so any increased rents would take a bigger toll. Less noticed in the debate over Amendment 3 was how it would affect the state’s affordable housing shortage. We argue that the structure of the property tax cut will likely create more incentives for counties and developers to build commercial structures instead of more residential housing. When developers build residential homes, they require a basic level of public services to support residents and the local infrastructure. In fact, property taxes in much of Florida effectively subsidize residential home development: For every dollar that a homeowner pays in property taxes, it costs county governments around $1.20 to provide those services. This gap will only grow when property tax revenues fall. From the standpoint of local and county governments, then, it may make more sense to focus on commercial or industrial construction as a way to keep tax revenues stable. But with fewer homes built at the expense of business construction – from manufacturing to data centers – the supply squeeze will only drive up prices and rents for the workers who are needed for those jobs. Now that Amendment 3 is on the books, Florida’s county and local governments will confront major trade-offs. We believe that state officials will come to realize how much of a change has been made – and how quickly their priorities may have to shift. In academic discourse, use of the correct name is an important means of establishing professional credibility. For example, if within the fields of computer science and information science, a specific term of art, such as artificial intelligence, has gained widespread acceptance, the use of an alternative term—a term, like superintelligence, not widely accepted within the discipline—would seriously undermine the credibility of academic work (and the author’s more general standing within the field). President Donald Trump has notoriously attempted to rechristen the Gulf of Mexico as the “Gulf of America.” So too has he attempted to rename Lake Ontario to “Lake America,” and he is threatening to fashion new MAGA names for an ocean, a major river, and the state of New Mexico. Trump also recently signed an executive order requiring all federal government programs and communications to use the terms superintelligence and S.I. rather than the broadly accepted nomenclature artificial intelligence and A.I. One might shrug all of this off and say, “Well, so what? It’s a free country, and all of us can still use the generally accepted names of places (Gulf of Mexico) and things (A.I.).” Unfortunately, after Oct. 1, 2026, this is no longer the case for academics at state-sponsored colleges and universities in Alabama. H.B. 2 officially bans the use of the phrase Gulf of Mexico by any and all state employees. As a professor at the University of Alabama School of Law, I find this appalling. This bizarre law provides that “all state and local entities and their employees shall be required to … [r]efer to the body of water that borders the coast of Alabama as the ‘Gulf of America,’ ” including “in all newly created maps, documents, educational materials, websites, official communications, and other resources.” Section 3 contains a limited exception to this general rule: “Nothing in this section shall prohibit a public K–12 school or public institution of higher education from using the term ‘Gulf of Mexico’ within a larger course of academic instruction when reasonable for historical purposes.” Imagine, for a moment, a marine biologist at Auburn University preparing to present research at a major global academic conference on the marine mammals of the “Gulf of America”? Or a geologist at the University of Alabama writing about oil deposits in the “Gulf of America” in a scholarly research paper? H.B. 2 plainly renders unlawful the use of the term Gulf of Mexico in any and all professional endeavors, including teaching and scholarship, save for using the generally accepted name within national and international academic discourse solely for “historical purposes.” This is bad enough, of course, but future legislation could compel Alabama’s public college and university professors to abjure using Lake Ontario or artificial intelligence in both their classrooms and academic publications. Moreover, Trump’s “superintelligence” executive order significantly raises the stakes for compelled academic speech and does so on a nationwide basis. The federal government sponsors billions of dollars of academic research annually. Now government-funded research into A.I. will presumably require grant applicants to use the term superintelligence in both their grant applications and subsequent published research. (It bears noting that the leadership of Facebook and X, Mark Zuckerberg and Elon Musk, dumped artificial intelligence in favor of superintelligence in recent quarterly earnings calls with investors.) Efforts to control speech in Alabama’s educational institutions constitute just one part of a larger national effort to standardize speech and, by necessity, thought. Happily, however, the First Amendment not only protects the use of particular words but also extends to a person’s choice of language. Government efforts to compel speech are facially inconsistent with the First Amendment. Cohen v. California, which blocked California’s effort to criminalize the use of the word fuck in public, clearly establishes this rule. Writing for the Cohen majority, Justice John Marshall Harlan explained, “We cannot indulge the facile assumption that one can forbid particular words without also running a substantial risk of suppressing ideas in the process.” The power to control words involves the power to control thought, and “governments might soon seize upon the censorship of particular words as a convenient guise for banning the expression of unpopular views.” Cohen squarely forecloses government efforts to ban the use of particular words—presumably including Gulf of Mexico, Lake Ontario, and artificial intelligence. Under the First Amendment, neither the federal nor state government possesses the constitutional authority to put words in the mouths of citizens. Instead, the First Amendment “is designed and intended to remove governmental restraints from the arena of public discussion, putting the decision as to what views shall be voiced largely into the hands of each of us,” and this holds true “because governmental officials cannot make principled distinctions in this area.” What is true generally for all Americans has special force in the context of education. As Justice Felix Frankfurter explained, in his concurring opinion in Wieman v. Updegraff, “to regard teachers—in our entire educational system, from the primary grades to the university—as the priests of our democracy is therefore not to indulge in hyperbole.” But teachers, at all levels of our educational system, “cannot carry out their noble task if the conditions for the practice of a responsible and critical mind are denied to them.” Accordingly, “they must have the freedom of responsible inquiry, by thought and action, into the meaning of social and economic ideas, into the checkered history of social and economic dogma … to assure which the freedoms of thought, of speech, of inquiry, of worship are guaranteed by the Constitution of the United States against infraction by National or State government.” Forcing computer scientists to use the term superintelligence in federally sponsored research or environmental scientists to use Gulf of America in their teaching and scholarship squarely violates both general free speech principles and the more specific First Amendment protection afforded to academic freedom. Unlike the GOP, which has been in thrall to Trumpism for a decade, and despite Americans’ growing disenchantment with political parties in general, Democrats still behave the way an effective political party should: recalibrating after defeats, adjusting messages as the political environment shifts, backing quality candidates, and tolerating a broad spectrum of ideologies and voices under one umbrella. And isn’t the chief architect of the party’s midterm victories actually the president himself, who spiked prices with tariffs and a war? Yes and yes. No point in sugarcoating it: Losing to Trump not once but twice has been hugely costly, not least for the Democrats’ brand. And both times, the party made major missteps. In 2024, the party did the right thing by pushing Joe Biden out of the race over widespread concerns about his age, but Dems did so too late—and paid for it dearly. Gavin Newsom’s grant of clemency to the 548 people under a death sentence in his state is a singular event in the history of America’s death penalty. No other governor ever has come close to sparing that many lives. Compare Newsom’s move to other governors who, in the last two decades, have cleared out their state’s death rows. In 2003, Illinois Gov. George Ryan commuted 167 death sentences and granted four pardons. Four years later, Jon Corzine commuted the sentences of the eight people on New Jersey’s death row. In 2011, another Illinois governor, Pat Quinn, commuted 15 death sentences, and in 2022, Oregon Gov. Kate Brown commuted 17. The magnitude of Newsom’s action today overshadows them all. But it’s where death penalty sentences are headed. And as I have explained elsewhere, “Since 1977, governors in 24 states have granted capital clemency 327 times. Indeed, in only ten of the states that still retain the death penalty have governors not granted clemency during this timeframe. Three of them (California, Oregon and Pennsylvania) currently have a moratorium on executions, and another four of them have some of the smallest death rows in the country (Utah, South Dakota, Nebraska and Kansas).” Newsom’s clemency reduced the total number of people on death rows across the country by more than a quarter. It comes in the wake of Christa Pike’s failed execution and at a time when capital punishment is very much on the minds of many Americans. Newsom’s reasons for granting universal clemency are compelling. He has described the state’s death penalty system as “unfair, unjust, wasteful, protracted.” As he explained, “Death sentences are unevenly and unfairly applied to people of color, people with mental disabilities, and people who cannot afford costly legal representation.” And the governor noted that “innocent people have been sentenced to death in California.” Those reasons echo concerns expressed by the other governors who have granted mass clemencies to people under death sentences. For example, as Oregon’s Brown explained when she exercised that power, the death penalty is “an irreversible punishment that does not allow for correction; is wasteful of taxpayer dollars; does not make communities safer; and cannot be and never has been administered fairly and equitably.” Illinois’ Quinn commuted all existing death sentences the same day he signed a bill abolishing capital punishment in his state. He said: “Since our experience has shown that there is no way to design a perfect death penalty system, free from the numerous flaws that can lead to wrongful convictions or discriminatory treatment, I have concluded that the proper course of action is to abolish it. With our broken system, we cannot ensure that justice is achieved in every case.” But unlike Brown’s and Quinn’s efforts, more work is needed to complete what Newsom started. Under a California law governing clemency, the California Supreme Court has to approve the commutations of any of the 548 people with multiple felony convictions. This provision was added to the state constitution in 1879, as an alternative to a proposal to forbid the governor from using their clemency power in cases where an offender had committed more than one felony. But it was clear at the time it was adopted that it did not empower “the justices of the court to supplement or supplant the Governor’s executive clemency authority.” In 2018, the California Supreme Court explained that the idea was that “the court could serve as a check on the Governor within the confines of its traditional judicial role, determining whether there exists a legitimate basis on which the Governor could choose to grant clemency to the applicant.” As the court put it, “Article V, Section 8 of the California Constitution does not require the justices of this court to determine whether, in the view of each justice, an applicant deserves clemency, whether based on claimed innocence of the crime, rehabilitation, mercy, or other grounds.” The court’s role is exceedingly limited.  The justices ask only whether the governor’s actions are an “abuse” of his broad constitutional authority, such as corruption or conflict of interest.  The court’s narrow role is consistent with its long-standing view that the governor’s clemency power is “virtually unlimited.” And as an amicus brief filed by California’s death penalty defense agencies observes that “commuting some, but less than all, death sentences would not remedy the injustices the Governor wishes to address; it would make them worse.” The brief argues that since Newsom has made clear that in view of the systemic problems that have long plagued California’s death penalty system, “universal clemency based on systemic factors is more appropriate than grants of clemency based on individual circumstances … the clemency grants he has asked this Court to recommend would buttress those he has already unilaterally issued, to complete his goal.” In addition, the state Supreme Court has regularly approved gubernatorial clemency decisions. In the past few months, it has given the green light to commutations of sentences of life without parole so that they “can be eligible for a parole suitability hearing.” Now, the California Supreme Court has a chance to complete Newsom’s use of the clemency power to spare lives. It should do so expeditiously. When the court approves it, Gov. Newsom will move California, and the United States, one step closer to ridding itself of capital punishment. The interface to the networking library in PyTorch needed a rewrite – it was drowning in technical debt, said PyTorch core developer Tristan Rice, a Meta software engineer specializing in distributed computing, during a live YouTube livestream on Thursday. NCCL is pronounced "Nickel" and stands for the Nvidia Collective Communications Library. It provides PyTorch with a set of inter-GPU communication primitives, allowing GPUs to communicate within a single-node or across multi-node clusters, which is vital for running inference on large neural networks and for other tasks in building and maintaining LLMs. The previous PyTorch backend for NCCL, called ProcessGroupNCCL, could spawn dozens of communicators per process group, leading to intermittent deadlocks and unpredictable performance. The new version of the NCCL backend comes with a strict rule: One process group gets only one communicator during initialization. By default, this rule eliminates crosstalk between multiple communicators and all the complexities that entails. If a program requires parallelism, it will have to dispatch more process groups. The updated library also better handles stragglers by allowing for easier reconfiguration. If a GPU crashes or runs slowly, a third-party orchestrator can exclude the laggard and even assign a replacement worker. A “flight recorder” has also been added to give admins a better idea of what went wrong. Previously, if a cluster of GPUs stalled—perhaps from a timeout or deadlock—the user would just get a generic message. The new flight recorder captures all operations in a series of snapshots that can be inspected after a failure occurs. Rice noted the cleaned-up NCCL backend, also called ProcessGroupNCCL, will give developers a blank slate, making it easier to add new features. The legacy ProcessGroupNCCL is part of the 2.15 package as a rollback, but if the new NCCL backend works as expected, the older version will be removed in the 2.16 release, planned for early next year. Like NumPy, PyTorch supports numerical computing, but it also provides tools for building and training neural networks on CPUs and GPUs. I watched a humanoid robot clean my apartment. It felt like a taste of the future.

MIT Technology Review | 2026-11-06

Visit Original Website
I’m not the sort of person who typically pays for a house cleaner. I’m too frugal, and I kind of enjoy putting on a podcast and going to town on my tub with a Magic Eraser. But when I saw an X post about a new humanoid-robot cleaning service in San Francisco, I immediately signed up for the waitlist. I wanted my apartment to feel like The Jetsons, even if just for an hour. I was also motivated by journalistic interest. In order to ensure an authentic experience, I didn’t tell Tau Robotics, the startup behind the X post, that I was a journalist—though I did use my real name, so it’s possible someone looked me up. Tau’s long-term goal is to make autonomous cleaning robots a reality, but they’re not quite there yet: For now, the robots are piloted remotely by humans, who watch what’s going on via cameras mounted in the robots’ heads and wrists. As the robots clean, they collect data that Tau will use to train up their more independent descendants. Every counter successfully wiped and T-shirt successfully folded will help the models that power the robots learn to do better on the next go-round. Because this data is so valuable to Tau, the company charges less for cleaning than one might expect for such a cutting-edge product. At the time I booked, Tau was offering $30 for an hourlong cleaning with a single robot; now the charge is $60 per weekly cleaning with two robots, or $100 for a one-time visit. But even at the cheaper rate of $30 per hour, Tau’s robots aren’t a great bargain. They are thorough but slow, so if you, like me, are tempted to stand around watching them for their entire visit, it would probably be a better use of your time to just take care of the cleaning yourself. But the robots are physically capable of cleaning, and they’re undeniably cute. Tau has armed each four-and-a-half-foot-tall robot with a 3D-printed “head” with antennae that allow it to communicate with Tau’s headquarters, as well as two 3D-printed claw “hands” (the more humanlike alternatives apparently aren’t durable enough, and claws do surprisingly well for most cleaning tasks). Mine was wearing a Tau Robotics shirt, and grippy socks so that it didn’t scratch hardwood floors. With all the human support they need, the robots are closer to radio-controlled cars than Waymos. Many tasks, such as making the bed and folding laundry, require what’s called kinematic guidance: The human pilot performs the necessary motions, and sensors transmit those movements to the robot, which copies them. These kinematically guided tasks produce especially valuable training data. Tau’s AI models might not have solved laundry folding yet, but learning from the examples of robots that have completed the task in the real world is how they’ll get there. But according to Tau cofounder Cornelia Weinzierl, to whom I came clean as a journalist a few weeks after the cleaning, the robot can already complete some other tasks—such as vacuuming and mopping—without any human help. If Tau succeeds in using all the data it is collecting to train truly autonomous cleaning robots, they could even become San Francisco’s newest tech tourist attraction as Waymos become more commonplace countrywide. The cleaning robot that came to my building last month was already a bit of a spectacle. It arrived in a blue minivan, out of which it was helped by Weinzierl and another Tau employee. That’s when the robot encountered its first obstacle: the eight stairs leading up to the building’s front door. I’d come down to greet Weinzierl and the robot, so I had a front-row seat to its struggles with my steps. It climbed as if it were drunk. Each successful step was as likely as not to be followed by a stumbling backward stagger that erased all its progress—though, impressively, it never entirely lost its footing. When I later discussed my experience with Weinzierl, she explained that the robot sometimes walks strangely because it is operated remotely from Tau’s headquarters, and the short signal transmission delay can affect its coordination. “The amount of work and time that we spend just optimizing, for example, timings and connectivity is crazy,” she said. Inside, the carpeted staircase leading up to my apartment defeated the robot entirely, and Weinzierl had to lug it up the last few steps. This, it turned out, was not typical. As the robot cleaned, it repeatedly stalled out, something Weinzierl attributed to it overheating in the unseasonably hot weather—until she heard from one of her colleagues that the heat had shut down not the robot but the equipment that its human controller was using to watch its progress through my apartment. Without remote assistance, the robot couldn’t clean, and so it, and Weinzierl, departed before my booked hour was up. (Back in September, Weinzierl or another Tau representative remained with the robot throughout its visit, but she says that’s no longer necessary.) You can’t fault Tau on customer service, though. Weinzierl quickly emailed me to apologize, comp the visit, and offer me another on the house (minus whatever price I assign to my data privacy)—and that was all before I told her I was a journalist. I scheduled the next cleaning for about a month later, after a couple of trips out of town, and it went much more smoothly—the robot got up my stairs without much assistance, though still with the drunken stagger. In the hour it was there, it did a creditable job of cleaning my kitchen (though it didn’t brave the stove) and even saved me my most dreaded cleaning task, the toilet. That was its most impressive performance: Three times the head fell off its toilet cleaning wand, and three times it managed to reattach it by poking around with the handle until it made contact at just the right angle. The robot is still rough around the edges, which is to be expected for a product in beta. It repeatedly left my taps dripping, it knocked over a (closed) bottle of olive oil, and it couldn’t manage to right an air purifier that it had toppled. But I easily forgave all those missteps because of how charming it was. As I watched it wipe down my countertops, I felt the same fond gratitude that I feel when a Waymo delivers me safely home after a night out. Still, it’s not all slapstick and whimsy. If Tau Robotics or its competitors succeeds in training robots to clean autonomously and inexpensively, people could lose their livelihoods, and the United States is a long way away from implementing policies to take care of those left unemployed by AI. But Weinzierl maintains that someday turning over cleaning work to robots could make a lot of sense. “Cleaning is not a job that a lot of people really actively want to be doing—there’s high turnover, there’s a high risk of injury,” she said when we spoke after the cleanings. “We need people, and we don’t have them, and no one wants to do it.” She said that the company is also starting to consider someday deploying the robots for other dangerous or physically challenging jobs, such as mold remediation and home health care. Tau Robotics still has a long way to go: The amount of data needed to train robots to do the varied and complicated tasks of apartment cleaning is probably enormous, and the company has completed only a couple hundred cleanings so far. But it’s remarkable that there are now humanoid robots able to clean your toilets and mop your floors, even if they need quite a bit of human help to do so. A new bill could change how the US builds energy infrastructure

MIT Technology Review | 2026-11-05

A sweeping bit of legislation designed to cut red tape could speed up the buildout of energy infrastructure in the US. With the dust barely settled on this week’s momentous midterms, the Senate is set to vote on the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) on November 9. It’s a plan that could finally start an overhaul of the slow, complicated process for getting energy projects approved, as part of something called permitting reform. People on both sides of the political aisle have been pushing for this type of change for some years. The key question is whether this will cut greenhouse-gas emissions from the US energy sector or push them higher still. The federal permitting system is designed to protect the environment against industrial pollution from large projects like power plants, transmission lines, and natural-gas pipelines. Today, permitting is governed by a range of laws, including the National Environmental Policy Act and the Clean Water Act. And if there’s one thing you might know about the current state of permitting in the US it’s that it’s slow and incredibly complicated. So in its 417 pages, BAAJA attempts to tighten things up. The bill has provisions that narrow the sorts of projects that need to apply for federal permits and others that enact deadlines in hopes of speeding up a process that today can take years. (There are also specific provisions about transmission planning, mining, and the rest of the kitchen sink; this thing is dense.) One thing to know about this bill is that it’s largely technology neutral: It opens the doors for the buildout of projects involving both fossil fuels and renewables. So there’s a weird mishmash of legislators and outside groups supporting it. The bill’s four sponsors in the Senate are two Republicans and two Democrats. The White House has backed this permitting reform bill, with its Council of Economic Advisors finding that it would reduce review times and have economic benefits like boosting GDP. Clean energy groups like the American Clean Power Association have largely come out in favor of the bill too, with the group’s analysis finding that it could unlock 350 gigawatts of new clean energy for the grid. Some environmental groups, however, oppose the bill, or parts of it. An assessment from the Natural Resources Defense Council says the parts about transmission could have substantial benefits for the grid, but the narrowing of environmental reviews could be damaging for public and private lands. Many in the climate world have long been calling for permitting reform, arguing that renewables stand to benefit more than fossil fuels. The thinking is that solar and wind power are some of the cheapest sources available today, so if we unlock the ability to build more energy, they are likely to win out economically. A few early analyses have tried to quantify just what BAAJA would mean for emissions in the US. One, from the think tank Rhodium Group, found that in the best-case scenario, the bill would cut economy-wide emissions by 4%, reduce annual energy bills by $78 per household, and add 200 gigawatts of new clean energy by 2040. In the worst case, emissions would stay about the same. Another report, from the nonprofit research group Resources for the Future, found that the bill could cut power-sector emissions 6% by 2041 and save more than $350 billion on system costs by midcentury. But there are some concerns that this permitting bill’s real effect would be to open the floodgates for new natural-gas plants. As data centers have put more pressure on the grid, a wave of such plants have been announced. “You get down to a trade-off between essentially growing the clean electricity industry on the one hand and then, as a compromise, growing the fossil-fuel industry on the other,” says Kevin Kircher, an energy modeler and assistant professor of mechanical engineering at Purdue. To quantify that trade-off, Kircher also modeled the potential emissions effects of the bill and found that if BAAJA had been in place in 2025, energy sector emissions would have been higher than the actual baseline. Health and mortality risks associated with air pollution would also have increased. This bill is scheduled for a Senate vote on November 9, well before the next Congress takes control. It’s not clear what the dramatic victories for Democrats might mean for the legislation, which was expected to pass the Senate with largely Republican support if a few Democrats got on board. Democrats could decide to wait for next year, when they’ll have control of both chambers. But any deal would ultimately need to win Trump’s signature, limiting what a future bill might be able to change. In a world where permitting new projects is easier, we’ll see more clean energy, but we’ll see more natural gas, too. And while prices could get lower, that could push demand for electricity higher, leading to more emissions overall. Simplifying the permitting system and cutting red tape is a worthy goal. But when it comes to emissions, it still really matters what, exactly, you want to build. Huge grid-scale batteries are thriving, but smaller residential systems have lagged. Texas is the fastest-growing data center market in the US and ground zero for Big Tech's largest AI buildouts. But the blue wave that swept the Lone Star State may jeopardize that position. Democrat James Talarico won the US Senate seat against Ken Paxton. Gina Hinojosa won the race for governor against the Republican incumbent, Greg Abbott. At the state level, Democrats will take control of the Texas House for the first time in 24 years. Talarico and Hinojosa made data centers a central issue, discussing them constantly on the campaign trail. Even before the polls closed, insiders in the data center industry noticed that the times were changing. At the industry's DCD conference in Ashburn, Virginia, on Tuesday, panelists lamented that Texas had been the easiest place to build for years—until September. That's when Abbott, perhaps seeing the increasingly bipartisan distaste for these facilities, began changing course himself. Developers said the governor’s office had stopped responding to their emails. On September 21, Abbott directed a key state regulator to halt data center permits. Many panelists at the conference were expecting this to mean that existing projects might be slowed and new ones could be halted altogether. The governor-elect indeed seems eager to do that. Hinojosa supported tax breaks for data centers in 2023 as a way to boost construction jobs, but during her campaign she began pushing for a full-fledged moratorium. That would be a problem for the hyperscalers of Big Tech, which have ambitious plans for AI training facilities in the state. The scale of projects already underway in Texas is massive. The Stargate Frontier data center in Shackelford County is projected to serve 1,820 megawatts—about as much as the nation’s largest data center, SpaceX’s Colossus 2 in Tennessee, and more than twice as much as the largest data center currently operating in Texas. Nexus Hubbard, also under construction and intended mostly to serve Anthropic, has a planned size of 6,400 megawatts and is the second-largest data center being built in the US, behind only Meta’s Hyperion project in Louisiana. The potential for Democrats to order delays to these projects or moratoriums on new ones adds to growing anxiety that the AI buildout could stall. Banks and lenders are already getting pickier about the projects they back. And given the extent to which the data center buildout is underpinning growth in the economy right now, those forces combined could have ripple effects. Voters around the country, though, were unambiguous in the midterms, sending a similar message on everything from state ballot questions to national offices. Voters supported efforts to protect ratepayers from increases in their electricity bills caused by data centers, and they favored some larger bans on new data centers altogether. If we needed any more convincing that Big Tech has fumbled its effort to sell voters on the idea that AI-powered abundance will make health care better, goods cheaper, and scientific breakthroughs more common, this was it. A key question is whether the Texas counties with the largest data center presence were part of the reason these Democrats got elected, or if the anti-data-center vote came from elsewhere. It’s complicated: The counties where much of the data center buildout is happening are around Dallas. Dallas County and others closest to it predictably went blue, but nearby Ellis County, which leads in permitting requests for data centers, went for Greg Abbott and Ken Paxton. Cameron County, near the state’s southern border, is a hot spot for data centers and voted blue in the midterms, which was a shift. But Mitchell County and Scurry County, where a bunch of projects in west Texas are located, went red. This is a big deal, because no single office in the state decides whether a project goes ahead or is halted. Decisions often reflect an interplay between city governments, county officials, and state offices. Local officials who have some say over data center development may resist sweeping restrictions from the state. And Democrats pushing for a moratorium might also face pushback from the construction unions that supported some of their campaigns. So while the blue wave spells a death knell for Texas data centers in theory, election winners may find that following through on their opposition once in office is much more complicated than campaigning on it. It would put TSMC up against Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron as AI demand keeps memory scarce. TSMC is considering making advanced memory chips, Bloomberg reported on Friday, citing The Information. The move would give the world’s largest chipmaker an even bigger share of the AI hardware supply chain. TSMC recently began drafting plans to make 3D DRAM, which stacks memory cells vertically to pack in more capacity, at the request of Nvidia, its biggest customer. It could build new factories or buy an existing plant and convert it, Reuters reported, citing The Information. The work is led by Hsu Kuo-chin, a TSMC vice president who oversees advanced packaging, according to the report. TSMC and Nvidia did not immediately respond to requests for comment. Memory is in short supply as AI data centres soak it up, sending prices soaring. Micron expects the shortage to last into 2028, and Apple raised Mac and iPad prices in June, citing memory costs. Some customers want their own supply: Elon Musk is planning a $55B chip plant to cut his reliance on Micron. TSMC is already moving closer to memory. In October, Nvidia reportedly asked Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron to build the base layer of its custom HBM memory on TSMC’s 3nm process. Amazon’s Alexa for Shopping agent will now take your orders by text

The Next Web | 2026-11-06

Visit Original Website
A text conversation with Alexa for Shopping, which adds lasagna ingredients to an Amazon cart, in an example from Amazon. Amazon is rolling out texting for Alexa for Shopping, its AI shopping agent, starting with invited US customers. It can find products, place orders and track prices by text. Sponsored products can show up in its replies, free to advertisers for now. Amazon is letting shoppers text its AI shopping agent, Alexa for Shopping, which can search for products, compare them and place orders for them, the company said on Friday. The feature is starting with a small group of invited US customers, with millions more to get access in the coming weeks. Customers who opt in get a contact called “Alexa Shopping.” They can text a request, such as needing more dog food, or send a photo of a recipe or an outfit, and the agent texts back with options. It buys with the customer’s approval, or automatically when a price hits a target they set. It can also track price drops, reorder past purchases and check on packages. Amazon says a conversation started by text will carry over to its app, website and Alexa devices. “Now with Alexa for Shopping, your personal shopping agent is just a text away,” said Rajiv Mehta, Amazon’s vice president of conversational shopping. The text thread is also a new place for ads. Sponsored products can appear among the options the agent sends, though Amazon is not charging advertisers yet, and purchases the agent completes on its own carry no ads, Digiday reported. Amazon made more than $68B from advertising in 2025. Amazon launched Alexa for Shopping in May and says more than 400 million customers used it in the past year. In September, it blocked Meta’s Muse agent from shopping its site. Last week, chief executive Andy Jassy said he expects Alexa for Shopping to become the main agent people use to buy from Amazon. Rivals are moving into messaging too. OpenAI added texting to Dot, its personal agent, last month, and DoorDash began taking orders by text in September, GeekWire reported.
