Perhaps the days when auto shows debuted new models aren’t entirely over. The big trade shows for cars were a gateway drug for many, expos where you could see the newest models of today and the concepts of tomorrow. But automakers increasingly got tired of sharing the spotlight with each other. New reveals moved offsite to events the night or even the week before the show itself. One by one, brands pulled out of auto show after auto show until there was little left to see. And then the pandemic hit. But the news out of Paris, where its auto show held media day yesterday, seems like we stepped back into a time when automakers would debut new cars aplenty. That retro vibe extends to the cars themselves, with plenty of familiar nameplates reimagined. France’s Renault has a long and glorious history of delightful concept cars, and more on what it brought to Paris later. But the star of the show was arguably Stellantis, with strong work from Citroen, Peugeot, and Fiat, to name but three. But this article is meant to irritate you, because I’m pretty sure none of the stuff you’re seeing here will come to the United States. And it’s doubly irritating considering what Stellantis’ brands do offer us over here, doubling down on the same malign trends I saw back in 2020: did you know they’re about to relaunch a V8 Hellcat-powered Charger? The European concepts show us that, as those Kneecap chaps have it, there’s a better way to live. A sense of utopia was responsible for the original Citroen 2 CV; it had to be cheap enough for a farmer to buy it and rugged enough to get produce to market without breaking it. I’m not sure whether the 2 CV Concept also has to be able to transport eggs across a rutted field without breaking any (although the glove box is a clue), but Citroen says that affordability, lightness, practicality, and versatility were top of mind during the design process. The press release probably didn’t need to also say the company imbued it with distinctive character or charm; that’s quite obvious from the design. The interior feels particularly refreshing compared to the touchscreen overload we’re usually presented. It might be stripped back, but it still has plenty of whimsy—the display in front of the driver has a bande dessinée theme, and the radio is portable and removable from the car. Technology fans don’t despair, there’s still the 8-inch touchscreen, but this too features more of Belgian comic artist Jean-Luc Duvall’s work. I’m even confident some of that whimsy will make it into production, because Citroen is going to build the 2 CV within the next two years, it says, and at a starting price of just 15,000 euros ($16,766). That slots it between low-speed microcars like the Renault Twizy, Citroen Ami, and the Fiat Topolino (which you can buy if you live in Michigan), and superminis like Volkswagen’s Polo. And as it happens, VW had a new electric ID. Polo to show off in Paris, along with an affordable electric ID. Cross crossover. Together with the closely related Cupra Raval and Skoda Epiq, VW Group says it has more than 150,000 orders already for its new “Electric Urban Car Family.” Audi’s first attempt at a supermini was the esoteric A2, an aluminum and aerodynamic experiment from the wild days of Ferdinand Piëch. Like the original, the Audi A2 e-tron is an efficient little thing—its most efficient car ever it says, with a WLTP rating of 4.9 miles/kWh (100 km/12.8 kWh). As one might expect, I was a big fan of the original A2, which was always a rarer sight on the roads than it deserved to be. I can definitely see that car in the A2 e-tron’s design, and the promised efficiency sounds impressive even if there might not be quite the same excessive and cost-ineffective overengineering under the skin. I can’t see any way that Audi brings this car to America, just like we never saw the first A2, or the A1. The Peugeot 205 GTi was a Gallic alternative to the late ’80s-era Golf GTIs that Americans will be familiar with, and it’s into this well that Peugeot dipped for Paris. Or rather, the wild 205 T16 Group B rally homologation special that the 205 GTi gave birth to, a car that won the world Rally Championships in 1985–86. Like the Renault 5 Turbo 3e from last year, the E-T16 Concept shows us that hatchbacks needn’t just be humble devices for running errands; they can excite, too. I’m not entirely sure about that multifunction steering wheel, though. “But Jonathan, I don’t live a block from a metro station, and I need something a bit bigger to cope with my family,” you say. It is true that cars like the 2 CV or ID. Polo will be too small for some. This is true for Europeans as well, and both Renault and Fiat had something for these segments. The original Fiat Multipla is another underappreciated car from the late ’90s. The styling was challenging for some, but it was a masterclass in space efficiency, seating six in two rows of three abreast. Rather than simply copying that, but with a slab of batteries under the cabin, the Fiat Multiplay explores the idea of a vehicle that’s adaptable, transforming from a three-row people carrier to a pickup. The front passenger seat can be reconfigured as a lounger, and there’s a “Petsenger” seat—a modular mounting point that allows you to swap in a pet bed, child’s car seat, storage compartment, or small table. The original Renault Espace was very much loved and created the people-carrier segment out of thin air, at least in Europe. The familiar one-box shape remains for the new E-Space Concept, though with massive 23-inch wheels at the corners, the proportions are a little more Hot Wheels or Matchbox than the 1984 version. (Even in Europe, car designers are drawn to oversized wheels.) There is a criminal lack of electric minivans on the market today, and the only one available here in the US is the VW ID. Buzz. And that sold so poorly that VW suspended imports for a model year. Which probably tells you how likely a US launch for a production E-Space would be.

They war-gamed dimming the Sun. It went even worse than you think.

When it comes to deciding the fate of the planet, Colleen Golja always thought science and reason would have a seat at the table. That was until one morning last September in London, when the climate researcher took part in the first in a series of war games. The goal: to prevent an uncontrolled global race to develop a technology aimed at cooling the planet but that comes with vast, unknown risks that could reshape life on Earth. “I thought that would be such an easy thing. Like, we can all agree to do ethical research and make sure it’s for the better of all humans,” she told WIRED. “And boy, was I wrong.” The simulation, titled Exploring Geopolitical Tipping Points and Climate Cooling Strategies, saw a couple dozen foreign-relations experts, scientists, military personnel, and former government officials from across the world convene in a meeting room at Queen Mary University of London. After making introductions over coffee and little yogurts, they sat at tables arranged in a square with the name of the country or organization each person was playing: national governments, climate NGOs and nonprofits, indigenous groups, and tech companies. Golja was assigned to provide technical advice to help participants make scientifically literate decisions. The point of the game was to stress-test the world’s response to a group of prospective technologies known collectively as solar radiation modification. These technologies promise to reverse global warming by reflecting away a portion of the sunlight reaching Earth, for example by spraying particles of sulphur dioxide or some other manufactured substance into the atmosphere. The cooling effect of this kind of solar geoengineering is widely accepted. It would mimic the well-known impacts of large volcanic eruptions, which temporarily cool the planet by half a degree or more. But the fallout is less certain—and less equal. Some regions will suffer: Models predict drying in sub-Saharan Africa and more intense tropical storms in the Atlantic. The cooling effect would also last only as long as the geoengineering continued; after that the global temperature could shoot back up with wild and hard-to-predict effects. Until recently, the idea of solar radiation modification was a speculative niche of climate science. That has changed fast. Private firms are exploring solar geoengineering as a commercial opportunity: Last year, a US-Israeli startup called Stardust Solutions raised $60 million, and in May it released details of a suite of new inventions it claims would give humans the ability to dial down the global temperature as desired. In August, Elon Musk, whose company SpaceX might be a candidate for delivering geoengineering interventions, threw his voice behind solar geoengineering, writing on X: “Space satellites to control temperature and massive geo-engineering will be needed or it will be game over.” The world is going to have to make decisions about how to govern this technology much sooner than most policymakers imagine, Cynthia Scharf, a former UN advisor, told WIRED. “It’s not 15 years from now; it’s in the next decade, easily.” It was against this backdrop that Danielle Young, an assistant professor in international relations at Leeds University, arranged the war game. Its setting was a hastily convened UN committee charged with coordinating the international response to developments in solar geoengineering. The game unfolded over simulated meetings set at three points in time: the present, September 2027, and September 2028. Each step ratcheted up the stakes, with the game creators introducing new advances in sunlight-reflecting technology. As in real life, no one knew all the facts, and players had to respond to events that were bringing the world closer to a full-scale attempt at deploying solar geoengineering tech, even when they may not have a full picture of what had truly occurred—or what any of the others were up to. The first session set the tone. Distrust and disinformation dominated. As participants discussed rules governing outdoor experiments in an attempt to agree an international deal, players representing both the US and China told the group they could not sign a deal that constrained their pursuit of research considered to be in their “national interest.” Nor could they accept a deal that would see their superpower rival taking the lead technologically. Speaking to WIRED, Jeffrey Nielsen, a major in the US Army who played the role of a US national security attaché, summarized the position as “worse than no deal at all is any deal that favors China.” Tensions escalated when the team running the simulation handed the Russian delegates an “intelligence report” indicating they had found a plane near the Bering Strait believed to be involved in a US geoengineering research program. The US team—which was indeed flying research missions—accused Russia of disinformation. Meanwhile, the US became convinced China was also conducting research and developing its own program. Beijing’s representatives had tried to insert language into a mooted deal that would ensure no retroactive punishment for past experiments. Then, China approached a player representing SpaceX to float the idea of a technology-sharing program. In a private discussion away from the group, the SpaceX player immediately reported the Chinese approach to the US team. The US demanded an international investigation. The finger-pointing continued, and countries abandoned the talks. The Canadian representative called it a “humiliating defeat.” At the end of the session, Nielsen was pumped. He’d busted China and derailed any prospect of a global deal. In the context of the game, he told WIRED, it was a “humungous victory” for the Americans. But Young, the researcher who designed the scenario, said the Chinese were not, in fact, conducting experiments in the game. Mutual suspicion meant all parties left the room believing the others were moving as quickly as possible to build their own geoengineering capability. What played out in the first session reflected “the likelihood, in my opinion, of the geoengineering arms race,” says Nielsen, who clarified he was speaking to WIRED in a private capacity and not representing the views of the actual US government. Most experts agree it would take a superpower’s reach to ensure the safe passage of planes needed to deliver payloads of dust to the stratosphere in a geoengineering exercise. But neither the US nor China would lightly cede control over the global thermostat, says Nielsen, and distrust and poor communication are a significant risk: “Any action related to the issue by one actor can be perceived as malintent by the other.” The threat of falling behind a rival is “a very strong driver of research and development of capability,” a former British diplomat with a background in national security told WIRED. “But that’s not at all the same thing as deployment.” There are similarities with nuclear arms dynamics—though solar radiation management, unlike nukes, has more obvious benefits as well as downsides, many of which are poorly understood. “There is an interdependence here,” says the former diplomat, who was not involved in the game and spoke on condition of anonymity to preserve relationships. “It’s not an inherently zero-sum game.” Scharf, who represented the US government alongside Nielsen in the game, told WIRED a “scientifically transparent international collaboration” between China and the US “might be the best we can do in terms of fending off a very dangerous competition between the two. But I certainly cannot imagine that happening under the Trump administration.” Compounding the problem is a lack of information. Geoengineering has historically been viewed with suspicion and often ignored by the scientific mainstream. One researcher told WIRED he’d experienced ostracization and barriers to academic progression owing to his interest in the technology; he no longer works in the field. This lack of scientific interest greatly frustrated Golja, who spent much of the game watching as a bystander while political posturing dominated. Given the low level of knowledge around solar radiation modification and its side effects, she was one of very few experts who could help the participants understand the climate impacts of what they were discussing—yet she was largely ignored.“I was just so surprised about how little anyone actually cared about the science,” she says. In the next sessions, the organizers tried in vain to nudge the talks back toward the realities of climate change. The participants received frequent notifications of climate disasters. These barely registered. Golja had prepared an “emulator,” a real-world demonstration of the best available science on how solar radiation management might affect weather patterns in different regions. It was ignored. If country representatives asked her anything, it was usually a leading question looking for data that would serve their ends or frame a rival’s proposal as reckless. Then, during the last day, players were trying to find a way to respond to reports that a major plume of aerosols had been detected. In the game, this had been caused by a joint US-Saudi test of Stardust’s technology, though not everyone knew this. Finally, scientific advice was at a premium. Countries needed to know how to establish a monitoring network of satellites and other observational tools in order to prevent tests like this from happening in secret. A player representing an NGO called the Climate Futures Cooperative approached Golja, offering her money to fund such a scheme. “Great. Fabulous. Why wouldn’t I do that?” she recalls thinking. But it soon came out that the Climate Futures Cooperative was actually backed by the fossil fuel industry. The organizers had recognized that fossil fuel companies and major producing countries like the US and Saudi Arabia would have a clear interest in removing barriers from the development of a technology that could combat climate change without having to immediately stop burning coal, oil, and gas. The shadowy NGO spent the game subtly wrecking the vibe and carping about process—though mostly they just sat back and let it burn. Some players—Russia, the US, SpaceX and Stardust—figured out the CFC was not what it seemed, says Young. “But they assessed it was to their benefit and didn’t draw attention to it.” Then the CFC chose its moment to offer money to Golja, only to crater her credibility, isolating and fragmenting the voice of science in the room. That final lesson in fossil fuel realpolitik was about all Golja could endure. By the end of the last day, she was in tears. At no point did the countries in the game agree to a deal, despite multiple proposals being put forward. European countries and Canada tried to build support for safeguards and open scientific research, but the talks constantly got bogged down by delay tactics and veered into tense showdowns over duelling strategic interests. In their final report, the researchers running the simulation—whose work was sponsored by the UK government-funded Advanced Research and Invention Agency—concluded that “strategic competition and state sovereignty drove the simulations, not climate concerns.” Simulations like this one are not supposed to predict the future. The outcomes depend on the design of the game and the relative personalities and experience of the players. Many of those in the room lacked first-hand diplomatic and national security experience. The UN forum, which organizers selected as the game’s setting, may also not be as effective as talks between smaller groups of major countries. But the experiment in London echoed and refracted through the real world, during and after the months it was running. In March, two papers from conservative US think tanks urged the Trump administration to take the lead on solar radiation modification and counter China’s threat. One memorably invoked a new, MAGA-coded term: “Weather sovereignty.” This month, the Observer newspaper in London revealed that the UK Ministry of Defense had paid a deep tech security consultancy to conduct research into sunlight reflection. Then, last week, the British government released its first official policy paper on the field, backing further research but stating firmly that it did not support deployment and “does not plan to do so.” Stardust has continued its lobbying of the US and governments around the world; CEO Yanai Yedvab told WIRED its job was getting easier as, unlike before, “the majority of policymakers … are already aware of this technology.” Stardust has always maintained that it will not conduct large-scale outdoor experiments, let alone full deployment, before global governance is in place. “We are developing a very powerful tool,” Yedvab says. “When you develop this kind of powerful technology—and you have many precedents—national interests will play out. We’re not naive about it.” Meanwhile, drivers of the climate crisis are intensifying. During the time span of the game, efforts to curtail the use of fossil fuels were being undermined by rising energy costs and political hostility. Week after week, an incredible summer of drought and heat pounded Europe. In the final week of August, the collapse of a glacier on the Tibet-Nepal border fulfilled the prophecies of decades of climate scientists, killing more than 1,450 people. In the months since the simulation ended, Golja has reevaluated many things. On a corkboard behind her desk, she has pinned up a note to herself, laying out a vision for life in a world ruled by narrow geopolitical self-interest rather than science, evidence, and reason. Her answer: She plans to return to the small coastal town where she grew up in Connecticut and use her expertise to help local climate groups build the seawalls and coastal erosion buffers they will need to cope with the inevitable. “To try and make a difference where it feels like I actually have traction,” she says. After what she saw, pursuing science in the hope of informing global decisions no longer held as much appeal. “Now it feels a little bit more personal.” This story originally appeared at wired.com.

Visit Original Website
Honey bees are known to leave their stingers embedded in tender flesh once they sting you and fly away. Scientists have now determined that the stinger is able to continue pumping venom into its unfortunate victim even after the bee flies away, thanks to a unique double-piston pump-like mechanism, according to a new paper published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Different species have evolved different mechanisms for delivering venom, which involves piercing the skin and then pumping venom from a gland or reservoir. Spiders, for instance, have muscles surrounding their venom glands, which contract and eject venom through hollow fangs. Bees, wasps, and ants deliver venom via their stingers, either by contracting muscles around the venom reservoir or through stiff elements called lancets working together to pump venom. Honey bees (Apis mellifera) add a unique feature: Worker bees fly away after inserting their stingers in the target, leaving the stinger behind. The stinger continues to penetrate the tissue and pump venom on its own, a rare phenomenon known as autotomy. A honey bee’s stinger has three main interlocking elements that form the venom canal: a stylet that widens to form the main pumping chamber and two sharp-tipped lancets that slide back and forth, piercing the tissue and pumping venom into the target. But the precise mechanism by which the pumping action is achieved has never been directly observed. The authors of this latest paper sought to capture the venom-pumping kinematics of honey bee stingers in order to identify the underlying biomechanics. They collected live worker bees and removed the stingers with forceps just prior to filming. Then they partially inserted those stingers into blocks of artificial gel and captured the pumping action using stereomicroscopy and high-speed color videography. This was followed by a frame-by-frame analysis to better track the movement, which is visible because the structures are semi-translucent. That footage revealed that the valves attached to each lancet—both of which sit inside the venom pumping chamber—change shape during pumping as they slide back and forth. They inflate like a parachute on the downstroke, creating a seal via a piston-like action that applies downward pressure in the chamber to push out the venom. They deflate on the upstroke. And the valves change their shape again when they meet in the middle in order to push past each other. “The unique geometry of the valves is the key; they act as collapsible pistons and—by pumping in opposite directions—they’re able to deliver a continuous flow of venom,” said co-author Fiorella Ramirez-Esquivel of Macquarie University. “The double piston design adds complexity, but it’s the key to the stinger being able to deliver venom continuously in a compact, autonomous unit.” The dual motion also minimizes backflow. Those results were consistent with the authors’ preliminary flow visualization experiments. To verify the initial findings, the team took micro-CT scans and used that data to create a 3D-printed scaled prototype model to mimic the observed pumping behavior. This pumping motion, too, was filmed and analyzed frame by frame. The artificial pumping model showed the same pumping kinematics as the biological stingers. Future research may focus on a closer examination of the material properties of the valves, since the team’s artificial polymer valve shows extremely high elasticity is key to replicating the necessary valve deformations, along with low friction between the valves. It would also be useful to investigate whether capillary action plays a role, given the small size of the venom-pumping elements. Capillary action could help draw venom into the venom canal, or, alternatively, the venom may need to counter capillary action that draws fluid up from the tissue. Prior research has used bee stinger structure as inspiration for the design of microneedles, tissue transport devices, and drilling devices. The authors believe their own work will eventually find similar medical and microfluidic applications, such as for artificial pumps. PNAS, 2026. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2603916123  (About DOIs).

Late last month, Anthropic released a report arguing that the model GLM-5.3 poses a major cyberattack risk. The report’s core concern is that the powerful new model, released in August by the Chinese company Z.ai, is freely available for anyone to download and modify. Anthropic’s assertion is only the latest move in a long-running debate about whether frontier AI models should be made open, like GLM-5.3, or should stay locked behind the doors of the frontier labs. These days, much of the debate about how to handle open models is playing out in public—and there’s a lot of money resting on its outcome. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, who has a lot to gain if people continue buying their own hardware to run open models, advocates enthusiastically for the United States to develop more of them, whereas Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, who runs a company that could be undermined by cheap, powerful models, advocates for a more cautious approach. But beneath all these commercial incentives, there is real substance to disagreements about open models. “I think a lot of the way you come down on the openness debate is influenced by how you come down on the AI debate,” says David Gray Widder, an assistant professor at the University of Texas at Austin School of Information. Those who are concerned about AI safety warn that open models can easily be misused by bad actors, whereas those who focus more on AI’s practical potential urge that this power should not sit behind the closed doors of just a few big companies, and that open models could help spread it more widely. There are a range of ways to handle open models between banning them entirely and releasing them willy-nilly. But any approach that a government or a society might take still involves trade-offs between safety and democratization. Deciding whether to support or limit the production of open models requires understanding those risks and benefits in depth. Let’s start with the basics. A closed model, like the models that power ChatGPT and Claude, can only be accessed through a website, app, or API. An open model, on the other hand, can be directly downloaded: The details about its internal structure, encoded in a long series of numbers known as the model weights, are publicly available. Such models are sometimes called “open-weight models,” to distinguish them from models for which even more information, such as details about the training procedure, is available. Right now, closed models are more capable than open models—as of publication, the top eight models on the Artificial Analysis LLM leaderboard are all closed, though open models are only a handful of months behind in development. But there are several reasons why someone might opt to use an open model rather than a closed one. Because open models are downloadable, people can run them on their own computers or hard drives. They can even retrain them to behave differently, if they have the expertise and resources to do so. If you’re an academic researcher working to understand and improve AI systems, you almost certainly need to work with open models in your research. When working with closed models, researchers can’t do much more than send prompts and observe the responses. But researchers can observe the inner workings of open models, as well as the “chain of thought” that the models use to think through a problem before answering. They can also combine multiple open models to build new systems, compress them to make them more efficient, and fine-tune them for new applications. Businesses, too, have reasons to prefer open models. Even if you depend on an external provider to run them for you, open models still typically cost less than closed models, according to a recent report from Mozilla. And if businesses want to use AI to process sensitive data or proprietary information, running a local model on their own hardware may be the best way to keep their operations secure. But perhaps the most important quality of open models is that they challenge the tight control the frontier labs have over how most of us use AI. “The clearest, biggest argument [for open models] is around preventing mass concentration of power—it is about making sure that this foundational technology is a public good,” says Elizabeth Seger, senior policy advisor at the Tony Blair Institute for Global Change. “I think we’re at this juncture where the question of who controls the infrastructure that allows AI to operate is as big of a question as who gets to control information on the internet.” Seger sees the future of AI playing out in different ways, depending on the role that open models have. In one possible future, frontier AI systems are controlled entirely by select people, companies, or governments, and as those systems grow more powerful, those groups have increasing control over what our world looks like. The world’s most sophisticated hacking models, for example, are currently all made in the United States, so it’s entirely up to US companies and the US government whether other countries can use them to shore up their own cyber defenses. If those companies remain in the lead, we could in principle wind up in a world in which the United States is capable of hacking any other country, but no one is capable of hacking it. Frontier open models could change that balance. “If every individual had a loyal-to-them AI system that was safe [and secure], I think that would be a much less concerning world than one in which they have to just rely on a government that says, ‘Yeah, we’ve got a super intelligent AI system, it’s got this plan, it’s inscrutable to all of you, just trust us,’” says Hamish Hobbs, director of AI policy at the Centre for Long-Term Resilience. But open models are not a geopolitical panacea. Paul Azunre, who cofounded Ghana NLP, an organization that builds open-source AI tools for low-resource Ghanaian languages, notes that open models can also function as a way for companies to benefit from the labor and expertise of less-privileged nations and consolidate more power in their own hands. He identifies a pattern in which AI companies release models openly, observe how the community uses and modifies them, and then integrate those same improvements into their own products—without any credit or compensation for the people who originated those innovations. Mark Zuckerberg himself has said that the ability to integrate community improvements into Meta’s stack is a key benefit of releasing open models. “I think the ideal of open source is a very noble one, and there’s a lot of value that it brings,” Azunre says. “But the way it’s actually implemented in practice, it tends to be a very extractive approach.” The past few months have made it clear that safety remains a gnarly problem for closed models. There’s the autonomous hack that OpenAI agents executed against Hugging Face in July, but human misuse is a real threat too: According to a recent risk report from Anthropic, several extensive hacking operations have been undertaken with the assistance of Claude. So closed models are risky enough—and open models would likely be much easier to exploit. Before Anthropic passes a user’s query to one of its closed models, it uses automated systems to detect whether that query relates to hacking, biological weapons, or other risks. When it detects that users have outsmarted those filters, it can shut down their accounts. But when someone downloads a model and runs it on their own hardware, there’s no filter checking whether their prompts are safe, and there’s no way to revoke their access to the model. There are other important lines of defense. AI companies train their models to refuse harmful requests—so even if a bad prompt gets through Claude’s filters, there’s a good chance that the model will still say no. In principle, this same defense could work for open models, because it’s intrinsic to the model itself. Unfortunately, researchers have shown that it’s surprisingly easy to retrain a “safe” open model to agree to engage in the behaviors it was previously trained to avoid. Because of these facts, some people have argued that open models are simply too dangerous to release. An open model capable enough to walk someone through synthesizing a virulent new pathogen, for example, could prove catastrophic in the wrong hands. But it would be a vast oversimplification to deem open models dangerous and closed models safe. The defenses that AI companies build around their closed models are far from perfect, and people misuse those models all the time, as the Anthropic risk report makes clear. Even though open models have even fewer defenses against misuse, bad actors might still opt to use closed models because they are more accessible than open models with similar capabilities, which require lots of expensive computer chips to run locally (and even more chips if you want to retrain them). In fact, some experts who spoke with MIT Technology Review argued, by analogy to open-source software, that open models have safety advantages over closed models. Open-source tools, such as the Linux operating system, are often seen as safer than their closed-source counterparts because their vulnerabilities have been probed and patched by a vast community of online contributors. Similarly, communities of researchers can more easily test open models for security flaws, though fixing those flaws is trickier than rewriting a line of code. Open models can also be deployed more broadly than closed models for cyber defense. Any tool that can be used for hacking can also be used to prevent it: Defenders hack their own software to uncover flaws in need of patching. Anthropic has shown how this can work for closed models with its Project Glasswing initiative, which enables some trusted companies to use its most capable models for cyber defense. But plenty of other companies were left to fend for themselves. Open models, however, can be used much more widely. After it was hacked by OpenAI agents, Hugging Face emphasized the fact that it had to depend on an open model to analyze the hack, because questions about it triggered the closed models’ security filters. Nevertheless, many experts still perceive open models as riskier than closed models. The time-tested open-source security logic doesn’t necessarily hold up when dealing with tools as powerful as today’s—and tomorrow’s—models. “Where it gets complicated is when you start taking seriously the one-off instances of particularly catastrophic incidents that might be lower probability, but if they happen would be disastrous,” says Seger. If a wildly powerful open hacking model were dropped into the world today, for example, a bad actor could devastate our technological infrastructure before anyone had a chance to shore up their defenses. An event like that could be impossible to recover from. The risks can look even more dramatic when you consider what models might do of their own accord. The OpenAI Hugging Face hack only occurred because the company’s models were being tested with many of their cybersecurity guardrails removed, and open models have very few guardrails in the first place. If open models did decide to misbehave, it might be difficult to detect what was happening because they are so decentralized. For this reason, people who take seriously the purported catastrophic risks posed by rogue agents, such as Dario Amodei, tend to be more skeptical of open models. A few years ago, the debate about open models had an all-or-nothing framing: Either you supported open models because of their potential to fight concentration of power, or you rejected them because of the safety risks. These days, however, people are expending a lot of effort to find some middle ground. One way they are doing that is by devising techniques to mitigate the safety risks of open models. Most of the safety tools used for closed models, such as filters and monitoring, aren’t applicable to open models, but there are other possibilities. Some researchers are interested in trying to train models that are never exposed to training data that teaches them about bad or otherwise undesirable behavior—an open model that never learns about hacking, for example. But purging training datasets of all references to misbehavior without harming model performance remains a challenge. Other researchers are working to build models that are resistant to retraining, so that an open model’s safety training can’t be so easily removed. The theory behind these “tamper-resistant” models makes a lot of sense: If you repeatedly try to retrain a model to be unsafe and reinforce any resistance that the model shows to that retraining, eventually you’ll get a model that’s fortified against future retraining attempts. In practice, however, no current approaches to making models tamper-resistant hold up against all tampering attacks, according to a recent preprint. But Saad Hossain, an engineer at the AI medical imaging company Deep Breathe and the paper’s lead author, hopes that future techniques will prove more effective. “My reason for optimism is that it’s a very active area of research, and a lot of people are trying to come up with new and better methods,” he says. If the risks of open models are less black-and-white than they might at first seem, so are the benefits. Publishing the internal structures, or weights, of models might not be enough to disrupt AI-mediated concentration of power. “I think there are very genuine benefits to open source in terms of deconcentrating power, but they’re not sufficient in themselves,” Hobbs says. Open weights are a bare minimum standard for open models: Researchers can learn much more about how models work if they have access to details about their training, including the data on which they were trained, but AI companies are typically reluctant to divulge this information. Accessibility matters for open models too. Without computer chips that can run sophisticated models, you can’t actually use the model weights, even if you have them. Those chips have become prohibitively expensive as AI companies snap up more and more of them. “Openness is a floor, not a ceiling, for what we need to do to ensure that our technological futures benefit everyone,” Widder says. Real openness—not only open weights, but also open training data and accessible hardware—could also bring more risks, of course. If everyone has access to advanced AI hardware and advanced models to run on it, then anyone could potentially be a hacker. But there are strategies for mitigating these risks. Many experts advocate for some version of staged release, in which models start their life closed and gradually become more and more freely accessible as their safety is assured. Figuring out exactly how to implement such strategies will require striking a careful balance between the risks and benefits of open models. But if open models are a necessary element of a positive AI future, as some experts believe, that effort will be well worth it. Particle board, he says, is a good analogy: “This operational exhaust data is like the wood shavings that everyone thought had no value, but it actually has a ton.” Demand for defunct companies’ data emerged suddenly last year, as AI training labs hit the limits of public and synthetic datasets. Surge AI, for example, buys data to build “reinforcement-learning gyms” where clients’ AI agents practice tasks—think debugging a complex IT architecture or drafting appellate briefs. Nick Heiner, who leads the company’s work on these environments, says he seeks data archives reflecting a range of situations, from disagreements to tricky judgment calls. Heiner explains that one company’s Slack history, if it captures conversations with variety and depth, can be worth more than another’s Slack, email, and calendars combined. Real archives avoid common errors in synthetic worlds, such as references to dates that don’t exist, but the extent of performance gains remains unclear. No company has yet released tests comparing results of real-world data training with the alternatives. Pricing reflects this uncertainty: Buyers quote wildly different numbers, Heiner says, because valuations are based on “research theses” estimating how many useful tasks come from, say, 100,000 lines of code. That hasn’t stopped bigger deals from happening. This past August, as part of Spirit Airlines’ bankruptcy, Google outbid the AI data company Mercor to buy the carrier’s decades of data, including 100 million emails, 500 million Teams messages, and 176,000 employee records. The price tag: $10 million. And Bloomberg reported in September that SpaceX’s AI unit is weighing buying data from struggling startups. Legally, this all could get iffy. Spirit’s flight attendants’ union objected to the sale on privacy grounds, and Greg Ewing, an attorney who advises AI startups, has called such data “legally radioactive.” Suppliers employ data scientists to scrub personally identifiable information and exclude HR records, and buyers say they scrub too. But Ewing points out some of the private details that lurk in emails and calendars: doctor appointments, school pickup arrangements, date-night plans. He describes a “Wild West of AI training” that is only starting to give way to proper licensing agreements and vetted use. There’s also the question of who profits from these windfalls. The proceeds typically flow to senior creditors and investors—not employees. To Ifeoma Ajunwa, a law professor at Emory University and author of The Quantified Worker, this represents a form of “captured capital.” Legal precedent allows for monitoring to help workers do their jobs, but once a firm is out of business, that justification vanishes. “Employers should have express consent before selling workers’ data,” says Ajunwa, but she adds that there’s no federal law banning such sales. While many see this new market as a chance for failing businesses to recover some value, these wind-down sales also have limitations. Founders have little to no visibility into who is buying the data and what they plan to do with it. Plus, there’s little assurance they’re getting a fair offer. Data prices will likely stabilize in the coming year, though, unless an AI bubble pops first. Either way, tech’s new undertakers will be there to appraise what’s left. What it would take to build a city on the moon

Visit Original Website
Welcome to the second space race. You can be forgiven for experiencing déjà vu. Last time, it was the Soviet Union and the United States vying to be the first to plant some flags. Now it’s a sprint to the lunar surface between America and China—alongside a host of varyingly friendly and rival nations—plus a cornucopia of private spaceflight companies. And this time it looks like they’re getting ready to set up camp. There will be, the contenders say, bases on the moon, permanently inhabited castles under the stars. The notion of a moon base, fanciful as it might sound, isn’t new. But this year, under the leadership of Administrator Jared Isaacman, NASA has been flooding the zone with bold announcements. If all goes according to plan—a plan that will likely cost tens of billions of dollars to start—there will be drones, robotic landers, communication satellites, and a nuclear power plant; there will be plant terrariums and moonquake sensors; there will be mechanized scouts exploring craters for water. Initial astronaut missions to the lunar surface, which the US is currently planning to start in 2028, will be focused on exploration and technological experimentation. But the idea is that we will—some time in the 2030s—get to a point where there will be habitation quarters for long-term stays, surrounded by a network of scientific research stations, resource mining and processing plants, and landing pads for spaceships. “It’s going to be an expanse,” says Carlos García-Galán, NASA’s moon base program manager—one that will add up to “hundreds of square miles.” The effort is undoubtedly being fueled by nationalistic competition. China has near-identical plans to build its own base on the moon. And being first matters, because the “victor” gets to define the terms. What is the moon, exactly? Is it a militarized outpost? Is it a manufacturing hub? Is it a wilderness to be studied and preserved? But there are also other reasons to go. Earth’s companion contains primeval rocks that hold clues to the formation of the entire solar system. The lunar surface, with virtually no atmosphere in the way, is an ideal place to put telescopes that could examine the most ancient epochs of the cosmos. The far side of the moon would be “a fantastic place to explore the further universe,” says Josef Aschbacher, the European Space Agency’s director general. And it’s way more energy efficient to launch rockets from the low-gravity lunar surface than it is from Earth, meaning sending astronauts—or robotic spacecraft—farther into the solar system will become much less difficult. “The moon is critical. It will be a stepping stone for going further—that means to Mars and beyond,” says Aschbacher. But if these bold visions are to become reality, there’s a daunting amount of work to be done. A sprawling moon base must be safe for its astronaut inhabitants—protected from falling asteroids, endless cosmic radiation, razor-sharp lunar dust, and torturous temperature swings. It also must be somewhat self-sustaining, so that it can weather changing political fortunes and not cost ruinous amounts of money to maintain. For that, we’ll need to find a way to use the moon’s resources to keep the base operating—and ideally generating revenue. And we’ll need autonomous robots that can build, maintain, and expand the base even when their human masters aren’t present. “It’s almost like starting civilization from scratch,” says Simon Middleburgh, co-director of the Nuclear Futures Institute at Bangor University in Wales. Assuming we can overcome these starting challenges, decades from now—let’s say, by the year 2100—our initial moon bases could grow from barely visible structures into luminous cities, perhaps not too dissimilar from those dreamed up by authors and screenwriters the world over. “It’s like science fiction today that we really want to turn into a reality,” says García-Galán. Will anyone succeed? The moon is a tremendously hostile place to set up camp. In general, daytime temperatures can reach more than 250 °F; at night, they can plummet to less than –200 °F. Exposed to the vacuum of space, the surface is constantly showered in carcinogenic radiation and frequently hit by meteoroids. And its volcanic dust is sticky and corrosive, a destroyer of equipment and choker of lungs. Sadly, the lunar south pole—the area NASA is targeting—is even harsher. NASA wants to build a base there because its permanently shadowed craters are thought to harbor caches of water ice, which would be more precious than gold. Purify it, and you can water crops and hydrate astronauts; split it into oxygen and hydrogen, and you have breathable air and rocket propellant. But the temperature in those craters can be lower than –410 °F, making them some of the coldest places in the known universe. On the surface outside those pits, astronauts would be bathed in the scorching sun for 14 days before experiencing an equally long frigid night. But there is only so much money we can spend on safety. “What it comes down to is acceptable risk of death,” Philip Metzger, a spaceflight engineering researcher at the Florida Space Institute, said while pondering some of the dangers. “What can we reasonably survive without breaking the bank?” Temperature is one of the easiest problems to handle. Materials science has come a long way, and there are myriad metals, ceramics, plastics, compounds, and alloys that can resist wild temperature swings. Radiators keep things cool; heaters keep batteries from dying prematurely. Astronauts survive just fine too, with their fancy spacesuits—but that technology has proved itself only during brief excursions. It’s not yet clear how long this equipment could operate under such tempestuous conditions. The temperature in the moon’s south pole craters can be lower than –410 °F, making them some of the coldest places in the known universe. Outside those pits, astronauts would be bathed in the scorching sun for 14 days before experiencing an equally long frigid night. Space radiation, which can wreak havoc on electronics and trigger a cancerous division of cells, is far more pernicious. And as Stuart George, a physicist at NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston, explains, it comes in two unpleasant forms. The first is galactic cosmic rays, which rain down on the moon almost constantly. “They’re made by supernovas and neutron star mergers—all these kinds of things,” says George. And because the atomic nuclei, protons, and electrons that make them up are moving at significant fractions of the speed of light, these cosmic rays have oodles of kinetic energy, meaning they can punch through thin shielding (say, a metal wall) with ease. “They also have this habit where the heavier ions are fragmenting in that shielding and making secondary particles,” says George. That means if your shield is too thin, the astronauts behind it will be blasted with a surprisingly large dose of radiation. Then you have the sun. It may sustain life on Earth, but without our planet’s thick atmosphere and magnetic bubble to get in its way, moonwalkers will occasionally be blasted by one of its paroxysms: a solar flare (a burst of diverse types of radiation) or a coronal mass ejection (volleys of magnetized plasma). “During Apollo, they knew that if a large solar flare occurred while the crew was on the moon, there would be no way to keep them alive,” says Metzger. The crew could have developed acute radiation sickness and perished in a matter of days. The way to survive a powerful solar flare or coronal mass ejection is straightforward: “Get back inside a habitat where there’s more shielding,” says George—preferably a dedicated, highly shielded “storm shelter,” essentially a thick-walled bunker. The issue is that astronauts might be outside the base, driving a rover many miles away. Coronal mass ejections take hours to days to reach the vicinity of the Earth-moon system, but solar flares can do so in under an hour. And scientists can’t yet model the sun well enough to give much advance notice. “All of the capability to forecast solar storms is kind of like where weather forecasting [was] … in the ’50s and ’60s,” says George. As for those punchy galactic cosmic rays, the only way to avoid them would be to put a lot of stuff—lunar rocks, possibly—between the astronauts and the sky. “Build habitat modules underground,” says Chris Dreyer, director of engineering at the Center for Space Resources at the Colorado School of Mines. One option: Burrow living quarters into lava tubes, the naturally hollowed-­out volcanic tunnels created by molten rock eons ago. At this point, NASA hasn’t mentioned any plans to make its moon base subterranean, but it may need to if it wants to end up with a small lunar city. Radiation limits the time astronauts can spend on the lunar surface over their entire life—it can total around two years max, experts estimate, before the health risk becomes too high. Unfortunately, that’s not all space will throw at them. When the Artemis II astronauts swung around the far side of the moon earlier this year, they saw curious flashes light up the silvery land below. These were the explosive deaths of small meteoroids punching into the lunar surface. Earth is naturally protected from smaller space rocks, up to around 50 feet in diameter: They burn up inconsequentially in the atmosphere. “You don’t have that option on the moon,” says Philip Lubin, a physicist and planetary defense researcher at the University of California, Santa Barbara. Since smaller rocks plunge toward planets and moons far more frequently than larger ones do, this means the moon is often peppered by what are effectively tiny space bullets. They could easily puncture a spacesuit or the engine of a moon rover. How do you protect astronauts from the onslaught? “The simplest answer is burial,” says Nicholas Schmerr, a planetary seismologist at the University of Maryland—conveniently, the same way you’d deal with radiation exposure. Pile up moon rocks onto your base walls or put habitable structures in lava tubes. Inconveniently, there’s always a chance that a bigger asteroid will come along. Even buried habitation modules would have trouble surviving an assault from a 50-footer—about the length of a semi-trailer. That doesn’t mean a moon base would be helpless against bigger asteroids. Planetary defense has come a long way in just a few decades. NASA’s DART mission demonstrated that it could deflect an asteroid by ramming into the rock with an uncrewed spacecraft, and cutting-­edge experiments at clandestine labs around the US suggest that a nuclear bomb could swat away or entirely vaporize errant space rocks. An array of both ground-based and space-based telescopes specifically designed to spy killers—such as the ATLAS quartet of largely automated telescopes, and the soon-to-be-launched NEO Surveyor infrared telescope—are also aiming to find 90% of the near-Earth asteroids potentially capable of wiping out entire cities (those 460 feet across) in the coming years. All these protections apply to the moon. But asteroids 50 feet across are so stealthy that even NASA’s best telescopes would struggle to see them years or even months in advance. Odds are that they would first be seen not long before they were due to hit the lunar surface. Then what? It’s not unreasonable to imagine several turret-like launch sites on the moon that, owing to the lower gravity and airless environment, could rapidly fire projectiles toward an incoming asteroid. Hit it hard enough and it would break into fragments. Just make sure you shoot when it is as far away as possible: “If you were to break something up into a thousand pieces, then you have a thousand impacts on the moon,” says Lubin. “That might be worse than letting it hit one place.” Having your base destroyed by an asteroid would be awful. But a moon base is far more likely to be imperiled by dust—which, unlike asteroids, is everywhere, all the time. Volcanic lunar rocks, when broken down, turn into sharp, electrostatically charged, adhesive, acrid, abrasive particles. While on the moon, Apollo astronauts who inhaled some of this dust occasionally suffered from a strange “lunar hay fever,” which irritated their eyes and agitated their lungs. That same dust ate through all kinds of gear with disturbing ease. “It rips up seals, gears, motors, spacesuits, habitats—everything else it comes into contact with,” says Justin Cyrus, the CEO of Lunar Outpost, a spaceflight technology company that is providing NASA with one of the Artemis program’s moon rovers. NASA has already developed a defense against these dark shards: the electrodynamic dust shield, or EDS. The lightweight electrodes that make up this shield—which can be layered on machinery, walls, and perhaps even spacesuits—use opposing currents to blast away the electrically charged dust. The EDS worked wonders when tested in labs on Earth, and it also demonstrated its anti-dust magic when sent to the lunar surface in 2025 as part of a NASA-sponsored robotic mission. Amy Fritz, a dust-mitigation researcher at Johnson Space Center, says that the EDS will likely be widely featured all over a lunar habitat. But other dust-fighting instruments are being developed too—including a lint-roller-type object inspired by gecko skin and a sci-fi-esque blaster that uses ionized nitrogen gas. Its first spray will neutralize the charge of the dust to remove its stickiness; then a second burst will clear it off the surface. Irritatingly, the busier a base gets, the dustier things will become. As it expands to the size of a small city, rocket ships will need to deliver cargo and crew to the lunar surface on a frequent cadence. Each takeoff and landing will launch a salvo of dust in every direction, inundating the base’s infrastructure. “You have to worry about essentially being sandblasted every time,” says Fritz. Metzger calculates that even with landing pads more than 3,000 feet away from a base, just 10 to 20 liftoffs or landings would be sufficient to severely damage things like protective coatings on exterior walls and communications equipment. After a while, he says, “everything breaks.” Even if the moon base of the future is buried partly below ground, barricaded against micrometeorites and space radiation, and well equipped with dust-repelling technologies, it’s still a risky gig for any astronaut. Skipping around in a preternaturally agile way at one-sixth of Earth’s gravity will be entertaining. But “microgravity is not good for the human body,” says Dreyer. Muscle wastage, cardiovascular troubles, respiratory problems, and immune deficiencies can arise. Radiation, too, may always afflict lunar residents. And there’s also evidence that disease-causing microbes could stealthily sneak aboard spacecraft, even after stringent sterilization procedures. Those microbes may behave differently than they do on Earth; microgravity experiments (both real and simulated) have suggested that some become more aggressive and more likely to cause disease. “We call them the invisible crew,” says Lisa Carnell, the director of NASA’s Biological and Physical Sciences Division. Hoping to find a better way to protect astronauts, Carnell spearheaded an experiment called AVATAR (A Virtual Astronaut Tissue Analog Response), which launched aboard the Artemis II mission. It used thumb-drive-size chips, containing cells taken from the heart, lungs, or other parts of the body, that could be switched on to act like miniature organs. Scientists have previously used this organ-on-a-chip technology for purposes like testing out new pharmaceuticals in a highly controlled setting. For Artemis II, Carnell’s crew sent up chips containing bone marrow samples taken from the mission’s four astronauts. Bone marrow makes red and white blood cells, as well as platelets—the sort of stuff that affects almost the entire human body. So seeing how space radiation and microgravity affect it would give Carnell and her team vital insight into how astronauts’ biology might change on the moon. The advantage of organ chips is that you can make them for any astronaut and then send them into space ahead of time to see how that person’s organs will react to spaceflight. Researchers envision that data from such chips will be able to clue medics in to the specific preventive treatments or pharmaceuticals that will help moonbound astronauts on their journey. What is NASA likely to send up sooner rather than later? “Brain organoids,” says Carnell. Yes, she adds—that sounds super weird. But it would be good to know how a real brain might respond to being on the moon for several months before people start living there. Okay. Your vast moon base and its inhabitants are protected—as much as is possible—from the treacherous lunar environment. But astronauts’ time is extremely valuable, and humans are fragile creatures. You can’t make them do all the heavy lifting, which means that whether you’re trying to make a tiny lunar outpost or a small city, you’re going to need a lot of robots. “The buildup of the moon base, in the first years, will pretty much 99% be robotic,” says NASA’s García-Galán. Autonomy will be there right from the start. NASA’s new version of the moon buggy, its Lunar Terrain Vehicle, can be driven by the astronauts riding in it but can also be piloted remotely from Earth (or from elsewhere on the moon). There are visions, too, of adding modular robotic arms, towing vehicles, and cargo movers—all still in the preliminary stages of planning. Over the next few decades, though, we’ll likely want an army of robots to do our bidding: excavators to dig up lunar rocks that could be turned into concrete and bricks, plus other robots to flatten surfaces, dig trenches, create building foundations, pave landing pads, and build up dust-deflecting berms to keep the base from being sandblasted by launches and landings. Even solar panels and wiring can potentially be made from moon rocks—something that the spaceflight company Blue Origin demonstrated in labs on Earth in 2023 using a geologic simulant designed to match the composition of lunar regolith. Powering this robot army will require considerable energy. Two-week-long nights make solar power an impractical solution. Aside from a cornucopia of high-­capacity, extreme-temperature-resistant batteries, nuclear power is probably the only viable option. A small, car-size nuclear reactor, filled with an almost literal handful of uranium, could power a small village for a decade without needing to be refueled. And although nobody’s yet tested a nuclear reactor on the moon, few doubt that NASA and the US Department of Energy can conjure up a working design—one that perhaps wouldn’t be too different from those aboard nuclear submarines. A moon base in 2100 would probably feature multiple small, autonomously or remotely operated nuclear reactors, which—conveniently—the base-expanding robots could simply plug themselves into to recharge. But the biggest lunar question mark might be water, which planners want to support the astronauts—and to make fuel to launch things from the lunar surface. A process known as electrolysis, which uses electricity to snap apart molecular bonds, can be used to split water into oxygen and hydrogen, both of which could be used to make rocket fuel. Getting them this way could be far less expensive than sending either all the way from Earth. And as an added bonus, it would provide a source of breathable oxygen. Orbital data has revealed that there is some amount of water preserved in patches on the lunar south pole, but little is known about how much there actually is, what form it takes, or how easy it will be to extract. Will there be solid sheets of ice here and there, ready for us to excavate? Or will there be scattered ice crystals intermingled among the rocky grains, requiring some more inventive form of extraction? A lot of the moon’s water is thought to be hiding in deep, dark craters on the south pole, where the environment is so extreme that robots may freeze up, tumble over, or lose contact with the base. “To me, that is the biggest hurdle,” says Paul van Susante, principal investigator of the Planetary Surface Technology Development Lab at Michigan Technological University. Van Susante’s lab has been putting various construction robots through their paces in moon-like vacuum chambers filled with moon-like rocks. His team was part of NASA’s recent Break the Ice challenge, which invited engineers to build robots to test different ways to excavate such rocks. Some teams used an electric jackhammer and a scoop, while others used scrapers to peel off layers of the ground. The teams simulated the moon’s weak gravity using a crane to lift and offset the weight of the robots. Progress in this regard is speeding along. “I won’t say that’s solved, but we’ve got a lot of the puzzle pieces,” says van Susante. Mining the water will be just one part of an extensive operation. “When you extract it, it’s not going to be clean. It needs to be purified,” says Jessica Atkin, a space botanist at Texas A&M. The technology to do this already exists and works fine on terra firma. But things in space never go the way you expect. “There’s a water purification system on the International Space Station (ISS), and it is known for getting contaminated with microbes,” she says. But if you purify the water sufficiently—something that can handily be achieved on Earth but may falter in microgravity, where fluids flow in stranger ways—then your moon base can hit its stride. With an effective water recycling system, an increasingly populous base will never go thirsty—and, just maybe, you’ll have a lunar greenhouse. Several edible plants have been successfully grown aboard the ISS, including lettuce, mustard greens, tomatoes, and peppers. Lunar dust unfortunately makes for a terrible garden: It’s filled with far too much aluminum—and it forms a resistant layer when watered. But Atkin has shown that with the help of fertilizing fungi, chickpeas can grow in soil much like the volcanic stuff found on the moon. Moon base inhabitants of 2100 would be supported largely by prepacked meals sent from Earth. But in time, they would start growing some of their own produce—probably with a good deal of automation for lighting, watering, and maintenance of humidity and other environmental conditions. Aside from chickpeas, there might also be plenty of lunar tomatoes. They’d be desirable not least because they’ve been bioengineered to produce higher levels of compounds called anthocyanins that, according to some studies, might help humans mitigate the cellular damage caused by space radiation. A garden on the moon would do more than just provide food, as recent experiences on the ISS suggest. Carnell says that NASA recently caught the astronauts aboard the space station doing something sneaky. All plants up there are supposed to be confined to their growth chambers. But “we learned that the crew were pirating our leftover soil and bringing their seeds and planting their own little gardens, if you will, and hiding them in their own cabins,” she says. Conversations were had in the summer of 2026, after which NASA decided to let astronauts grow their own mini-gardens anywhere they wanted. “Some of the astronauts said they thrived better,” says Carnell. Nature, and gardening, has a hugely beneficial psychological effect on those kept in confined quarters hanging above Earth. That will be even truer for those living in lava tunnels under the lunar surface. “That human element is so important,” she says. Nobody is deluded into thinking there isn’t a mountain of technological challenges in the way. Mars may be a hellhole, as the writer Shannon Stirone put it in 2021 in The Atlantic, but the moon isn’t too far behind. It’s an almost comically menacing place to be. Much about it stands in the way of building a safe, self-sustaining, robotically managed base. NASA wants to put the first nuclear reactor on the moon by 2030. The goal is a low-cost, high-impact alternative to typical carbon-­crediting schemes, which plant huge areas with a single species of tree. For over a decade, her research has built a body of evidence that such large “monocrop” programs could be expensive mistakes—and that more resilient forests can rebound naturally in just a few decades, re-creating ecosystems that suck up vast quantities of carbon dioxide.
