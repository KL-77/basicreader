Martin Shkreli—the ex-pharmaceutical executive infamous for illegally hiking the price of a lifesaving anti-parasitic drug by more than 4,000 percent and getting a seven-year prison sentence for the fraudulent scheme he used to do it—has been under a lifetime ban from the pharmaceutical industry since 2022. However, according to the Federal Trade Commission and the attorneys general of seven states, Shkreli has “flagrantly and repeatedly” violated that ban nearly the entire time—allegedly by trading stock in dozens of drug companies and discussing the industry during livestreams on social media. On Tuesday, the FTC and the seven states asked a federal judge to hold Shkreli in contempt for the alleged violations. The states include New York, California, Illinois, North Carolina, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Virginia—the same states that joined the FTC in suing Shkreli in 2020 over the “elaborate anticompetitive scheme” involving the anti-parasitic drug, Daraprim. The scheme raised the price from $17.50 per pill to $750 per pill virtually overnight, while blocking generic competitors from entering the market. In 2022, the federal court recognized the “seriousness” of issuing such a lifetime industry ban, but ruled that “Shkreli’s egregious, deliberate, repetitive, long-running, and ultimately dangerous illegal conduct warrants imposition of an injunction of this scope.” The ban, among many things, “explicitly prohibits” Shkreli from owning stock in pharmaceutical companies and making public statements that could influence a pharmaceutical company’s business, according to the FTC and the states. With the alleged violations, the FTC and the states are now seeking to force Shkreli to divest from all pharmaceutical companies and pay a fine equal to his profits from trading pharmaceutical company stocks. They also want the court to lay out “clear and escalating consequences” if Shkreli violates the ban in the future, including further financial penalties and incarceration. “The extensive record compiled by FTC staff shows that Mr. Shkreli chose to blatantly disregard the order after leaving prison,” FTC Commissioner Mark Meador said in a statement. “Among other actions, he has traded pharmaceutical stocks and actively sought to influence the business and management of multiple pharmaceutical companies through public commentary, in violation of the terms of the injunction. … A lifetime industry ban is only effective if breaches are met with consequences commensurate with the seriousness of the order.” In a response, Shkreli said he was surprised by the FTC’s filing. “I could see totalitarian NY AG Letitia James trying to limit a citizen’s free speech but not from Trump’s FTC,” he wrote on social media. The brief filed by the FTC and the states breaks down Shkreli’s alleged violations in four phases: (1) a 2022 trading competition with another YouTuber; (2) shorting a small biotech firm in 2024, along with trading in other Pharmaceutical Company stocks; (3) a 2025 trading challenge in which he shorted three small biotech firms along with trading in many other Pharmaceutical Company stocks; and (4) a trading challenge begun in June 2026 with a $10,000,000 account in which he is again trading in Pharmaceutical Company stocks. In numerous livestreams on various social media platforms, Shkreli would sometimes show his accounts on screen, the filing said. He discussed pharmaceutical companies at length, evaluating stocks and predicting the outcomes of pivotal clinical trials. In 2025, he told his viewers about his “magic trick,” saying: “It doesn’t matter what trades I make because I can always find a couple of biotech stocks to smoke.” Later in the year, Shkreli advised his viewers to short one pharmaceutical company’s stock, and the company’s stock price quickly dropped 17 percent. The FTC and the states wrote in their filing that the stocks Shkreli discussed in livestreams are “likely the tip of the iceberg.” FTC staff only reviewed a fraction of Shkreli’s social media activity, and Shkreli has made several references to other accounts he has. “I have some other accounts that I’[d] show you, but, like, I know feds are watching, so f[*]ck that,” he said, according to the filing.

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-13

For years, Chinese hardware manufacturers have carved out a market niche with an array of handheld Android gaming devices focused on retro emulation and/or more modern game-streaming capabilities. Now, though, a major game industry trade group is targeting one of those manufacturers, Anbernic, asking the US Trade Representative (USTR) to add the company to its list of “Notorious Markets” that “engage in or facilitate substantial copyright piracy or trademark counterfeiting.” TorrentFreak was among the first to notice the request from the Entertainment Software Association (ESA), which was submitted to the USTR last week. In it, the US trade group—which represents major publishers including Nintendo, Microsoft, PlayStation, Amazon, Epic, EA, Ubisoft, and more—notes that Anbernic devices “are routinely offered or shipped with removable memory cards preloaded with hundreds or thousands of unauthorized copies of copyright-protected video games published by ESA members.” While many online sellers do include an SD card full of ROMs alongside emulation hardware of all types, Anbernic itself does not appear to do the same when selling directly from its website. Regardless, the ESA argues that these ROM-laden Anbernic devices selling through “normal retail channels” help to “normalize hardware-based piracy by delivering illicit catalogues through a polished consumer product.” This, the ESA says, meets the threshold for a “superfake,” an ill-defined designation that applies to “high-quality counterfeit trademark goods … on a commercial scale.” Being included on the USTR’s Notorious Market List wouldn’t bring any additional direct legal consequences for a company like Anbernic. However, the USTR says the list “encourages the responsible authorities to step up efforts to combat piracy and counterfeiting” and notes that the list itself “increase[s] public awareness and help market operators and governments prioritize intellectual property enforcement efforts that protect American businesses and their workers.” Inclusion on the list could also presage a Section 301 investigation that could lead to legal consequences or restrictive import controls on the devices. The ESA’s call-out of Anbernic to the USTR highlights the breakdown of a polite fiction that has long undergirded the distribution of emulators and emulation-focused hardware in the United States. In the not-too-distant past, software and hardware distributors that wanted to appear legitimate took pains to push perfectly legal emulators while completely disavowing any knowledge of the kind of copyrighted ROMs that could get them in legal hot water. If an end-user wanted to later find and load up those kinds of illegal ROMs on their own (instead of running legal homebrew games or backups of their own cartridges), well, that was nobody else’s business, was it. These days, though, it’s trivial to find third-party sellers on Amazon, eBay resellers, annoying TikTok advertisers, or even reputable news sites boldly trumpeting and selling devices that include “thousands of games” from the past, ready to play from the moment you turn them on. To court customers who have never even heard of BitTorrent trackers or Warez sites, these sellers have brought illicit ROM distribution out of the Internet shadows and made it a core part of their business. As major online retailers continue to ignore this blatant copyright infringement on their platforms, it’s no wonder the ESA has felt the need to act. But focusing on Anbernic, which just makes the hardware that powers these emulators, is an odd choice when these centralized e-commerce sites could do a much better job at preventing the wholesale advertising, sale, and distribution of illegal ROMs directly to consumers.

Topgolf’s VMware costs were going to increase by 400 percent under Broadcom. To avoid that cost, the sports entertainment company moved 12,000 virtual machines (VMs) to VergeIO’s VergeOS and has saved over $11 million so far. Before migrating, Topgolf’s VMware connection “ran very deep,” Scott Forehand, Topgolf’s global infrastructure manager, told Ars Technica. Topgolf has over 100 locations worldwide featuring interactive golf games with real-time shot tracking and digital analytics. The company used vSphere and ESXi for decades and started using VxRail, the hyperconverged infrastructure (HCI) platform that VMware co-engineered with Dell, in 2017. Topgolf employees previously participated in numerous VMware customer councils and have been interviewed for books on how Topgolf used vSphere and Horizon (now Omnissa Horizon), per Forehand, who is a leader of the Dallas/Ft. Worth VMware User Group. But Broadcom’s decision to stop selling VMware perpetual licenses and bundle VMware products into fewer, larger, and more expensive SKUs led Topgolf, as well as many other VMware customers, to look elsewhere. With VMware’s strategic changes complicating future planning, Topgolf decided to migrate its on-premises workloads, including those for its venues, offices, labs, and data centers. Twenty Topgolf venues are still running VxRail with the VMware Cloud Foundation software stack. Forehand expects Topgolf to be completely off VMware by the second week of December. He added: Overall, we reduced or eliminated over $11.3 [million] in combined infrastructure costs prior to the adjustments in hardware cost in the market. Comparatively, it’s much more now when you look at the cost of a solution similar to the configuration of the old VxRail solution we installed. The money Topgolf saved by moving to VergeIO isn’t typical because Topgolf has larger needs than many organizations. However, saving money on software licensing and avoiding proprietary HCI appliances and separate tools for data protection, replication, and multi-site management is common for VergeOS users coming from VMware, VergeIO Chief Marketing Officer George Crump told Ars. “Most customers who move from VMware see meaningful reductions in all three,” he said. VergeIO gets about 70 new customers a quarter, “and most of them are coming off VMware,” Crump said. Most of those VMware migrators are facing renewal, are due for a hardware refresh, run many sites, or need multi-tenancy. “Most customers bundle the [migration from VMware to VergeOS] with a hardware refresh or a modernization effort, as Topgolf did with its gaming architecture. Scheduling all of that together is the real work,” Crump said. Since Broadcom’s takeover, VMware competitors have been aggressively courting VMware users. Topgolf ultimately chose VergeOS, a virtualization platform that combines compute, storage, disaster recovery, networking, and AI, because of its tenancy. Topgolf uses VergeOS’s Virtual Data Center offering for multi-tenant segmentation, where each tenant acts like an independent data center. Forehand explained: VMware lacks the ability to segregate clusters into tenants, which has made it a nightmare to manage at scale. Even VMware’s [Software-Defined Data Center] Manager product couldn’t manage the number of vCenter instances we had in our environment. With VergeIO, we have been able to segregate our deployment into tenants by time zone, making upgrades and maintenance much easier. We’ve even been able to leverage tenancy to build nested labs within a single VergeIO cluster, which has saved [us] considerably on the requirement for lab hardware. Topgolf replaced 650 VxRail hosts with about 400 Dell PowerEdge servers in about six months. Per a case study that VergeIO published, Topgolf moved from six-node VxRail clusters with 35TB of storage capacity to three-node VergeOS clusters with 5TB of storage capacity. “Moving to Dell PowerEdge reduced comparative hardware spend by 87 percent,” Forehand said. “Obviously, with the shift in the market and massive increases in compute and storage cost, we have taken a hit to the original numbers, but it’s still exponential.” Topgolf’s software costs, meanwhile, decreased by 77 percent. It also secured a five-year enterprise license agreement (ELA), compared to the three-year ELA that VMware reportedly offered. Topgolf worked with VergeIO employees to develop a solution that accommodated some of its “weird” needs, such as performing machine learning-driven golf ball tracing via Topgolf’s proprietary Toptracer, which runs in the VergeIO cluster, Forehand said. “Toptracer … pushes about 40TB of network data from the cameras in a typical 102-bay venue per day. That’s a lot of bandwidth, and the VergeIO team helped us solve some latency and pathing issues with that traffic to spread the load more effectively across the nodes in the cluster,” he said. VergeOS also appealed to Topgolf because its end-to-end automation didn’t require Topgolf to retool its infrastructure-as-code. VergeOS being “API-first” was key here, Forehand explained, and its employees helped Topgolf develop support for infrastructure-as-code tools Terraform and Ansible early in migration. Topgolf’s biggest challenge in ditching VMware has been coordinating all the projects it had to do alongside the server stack replacement. Forehand explained: On VxRail, Topgolf gaming was powered by the servers. With VergeIO, we simplified the gaming stack and moved everything to the edge game panels in the bay. That meant we had to replace all of the game panels, rewire the columns, replace the server stack, and swap to our new Kubernetes gaming architecture. We also piled a few other projects in there with all of that to eliminate even more technical debt. As Topgolf reduced its use of VMware, it also encountered challenges trying to integrate its new virtualization platform with third-party software, like Rubrik and LogicMonitor, which Topgolf uses to monitor its overall infrastructure. VMware has been a leader in virtualization for decades, resulting in a wide range of enterprise products. This differs from newer competitors, which may not yet offer the same level of compatibility. “Overall, the challenges have been pretty minimal,” Forehand said. “The real challenge has been a hearts-and-minds campaign to educate leadership on the gaps that do exist. With Rubrik, we moved to the VergeIO snapshotting to fill the data protection gap that not being able to use Rubrik left us, but we didn’t have an immediate answer for some of the Rubrik security features, like threat hunting and ransomware protection, so we’ve pivoted to Veeam to fill those gaps for us.” VergeIO’s strategy for addressing product integration challenges includes building the capabilities delivered through third-party offerings into VergeOS and partnering “on the ones that matter most,” Crump said. “Integration is the most common friction point, and it shows up after the migration. … A customer wants to keep using a tool it has already paid for and trained staff on. If that vendor doesn’t support VergeOS yet, the customer has to switch tools or run a workaround,” he said.

Trump commercials paid for by the US government continued airing on TV the day after a judge blocked the ads because they likely violate a US law against government-funded publicity or propaganda. In the most widely aired commercial, Trump railed against his perceived enemies and said he would “liberate America from these villains once and for all.” The campaign also included a commercial praising Trump’s record as president, another in which Trump calls the US the “most exceptional nation ever to exist in human history,” and commercials related to the Iran war and the capture of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro. US District Judge Jennifer Rochon issued a temporary restraining order (TRO) blocking the ads on Sunday, October 11, which took effect at 6:45 pm that night. The order said the government must cancel the ads, “including those placements for the evening of October 11, 2026.” The Trump administration had told the court on October 10 that the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) advertising purchases were set to expire on October 11 and would not be renewed. Despite that, the ads continued airing the night of October 11 and on part of October 12. This was also a week after Trump’s October 5 statement that he would start paying for the ads “myself, and with money I raised for MAGA, Inc.” A US attorney yesterday filed a status report saying that the government is now complying with the order and issued an apology. “As Your Honor is aware, the Court emailed the TRO to the parties yesterday at 6:46 p.m.,” US Attorney Jamie McDonald wrote in a filing. “Counsel for the Government emailed the TRO to agency counsel at 7:58 p.m. Agency counsel did not become aware of Government counsel’s email until this morning. The undersigned apologizes to the Court and the plaintiffs.” The ads aired as part of a $20 million purchase order that US Customs and Border Protection (CBP) made on September 20 with LMD Agency, an ad firm. The DHS told the court that the ad placements through October 11 cost $19 million. In the previous Trump administration filing opposing the motion for a temporary restraining order, one of the government’s arguments was that a court order was unnecessary because the ads would not run past October 11. It said: DHS issued a purchase order to obtain media services from a vendor in support of U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) for placing national media buys. The vendor has currently purchased media buys under the terms of the purchase order through tomorrow, October 11, 2026. DHS has directed the vendor not to make any additional media buys past October 11, and DHS has no plans to make any additional media buys under the purchase order or otherwise use appropriated funds on any similar contracts for the creation or distribution of the media that is the subject of this lawsuit. Accordingly, the alleged offending conduct will end tomorrow, and Plaintiffs therefore will suffer no harm, let alone irreparable harm, absent an injunction. The Washington Post reported yesterday that the ads kept running after the restraining order took effect. Nonprofit advocacy group Common Cause and other groups that sued the government submitted a filing later in the day notifying the court of the government’s noncompliance. The Trump ads continued airing on programs including 60 Minutes, ABC World News Tonight, and America’s Funniest Home Videos. They ran nearly 400 times after the restraining order took effect, including 32 times on Monday, according to AdImpact data cited in the plaintiffs’ court filing. “Plaintiffs do not presently seek contempt sanctions,” the groups said. “But given that the ads have continued to run on October 12—the day after Defendants insisted the ad-buy was no longer effective—Plaintiffs request a status conference to determine Defendants’ compliance.” After the government filed its status report, Rochon scheduled a hearing for Wednesday “to discuss this and next steps in this action.” The lawsuit is in US District Court for the Southern District of New York. The plaintiffs are Common Cause; Robert Hunter, a Democratic candidate for a state legislature seat in Alabama; Service Employees International Union Local 32BJ; and Empire State 32BJ SEIU PAC. Defendants include DHS, the Office of Management and Budget (OMB), and the Executive Office of the President. Rochon found that the plaintiffs are likely to succeed in their claim that the Trump administration violated the 2026 Consolidated Appropriations Act requirement that no funding may be used “for publicity or propaganda purposes within the United States not heretofore authorized by Congress.” “Defendants do not contest that the at-issue advertisements are purely partisan and unconnected to DHS’s official functions,” Rochon wrote. “Thus, the Court finds that Moving Plaintiffs are likely to succeed in showing that the advertisements are impermissible ‘publicity or propaganda’ under Section 718 [of the Consolidated Appropriations Act].” One of the ads “is almost identical to one used during President Trump’s 2024 campaign, other than the fact that its funding disclaimer now identifies the United States government rather than his presidential campaign,” Rochon wrote. “It depicts President Trump walking down a hallway while delivering a series of statements about his political adversaries. Among other things, he promises to ‘demolish the deep state,’ ‘drive out the globalists,’ ‘cast out the communists, Marxists and fascists,’ ‘throw off the sick political class that hates our country,’ and ‘rout the fake news media.’ This advertisement provides no information about DHS’s official functions.” As of October 8, the ad “had aired approximately 9,679 times, generating an estimated 972 million impressions at an estimated cost exceeding $9.09 million,” Rochon wrote. Another $6.3 million was estimated to have been spent on four other government-funded ads challenged in the lawsuit. That’s about $15.4 million as of October 8, and more was spent over the next few days. Rochon found “a clear and substantial likelihood” that the Trump administration violated the Administrative Procedure Act “because Congress did not appropriate funds for DHS to spend on disseminating the challenged advertisements.” Rochon said a restraining order was necessary despite the DHS claim that it had “no plans” to continue running the ads. “Defendants also have not provided reasonable assurances that OMB will not reapportion funds from another source to continue running similar advertisements,” she wrote. The temporary restraining order is in place for only two weeks, but it can be converted into a preliminary injunction that would keep the ads off the air while litigation continues. Trump, the White House, OMB, and DHS face a similar lawsuit filed by the Democratic National Committee in the federal court in the District of Columbia. A hearing on a motion for a temporary restraining order in that case is scheduled for Thursday.

Applied Materials’ $5 billion AI lab aims to cut chip development time in half—and restore Silicon Valley’s edge

Silicon Valley has built some of the world’s most impressive and valuable companies, with names like Apple, Alphabet, and Meta, worth trillions of dollars apiece. When it comes to building the actual facilities where chips, computers, and other game-changing innovations are created, however, America’s tech capital has much less to show. Most chip facilities are now located overseas or out of state, and the most striking thing about Silicon Valley’s landscape of low-slung buildings and office parks is just how unimposing it is. That makes what Applied Materials—the world’s largest chipmaking equipment producer—did on Monday so unusual. The 59-year-old company officially unveiled its new EPIC semiconductor research and development facility, a two-year construction project located at Applied Materials’ Sunnyvale, Calif. campus, just down the road from its headquarters. Quite simply: it’s the biggest thing around. The massive 600,000-square-foot structure of glass, concrete, and steel (two Eiffel Towers’ worth of steel, according to the company), looms over its environment like a spaceship from another world. The idea behind the facility is equally bold. The $5 billion EPIC center was built in partnership with some of the world’s top chip industry players, including Intel, TSMC, Micron, SK Hynix, and Samsung. The companies will each have teams of researchers on-site, working alongside Applied Materials’ engineers and getting early access to Applied Materials’ newest equipment, to develop advances in microprocessors and memory chips. Applied Materials described EPIC as the largest-ever U.S. investment in advanced semiconductor equipment R&D, with total capital investment expected to reach $5 billion when all is said and done. The facility’s so-called clean rooms, in which bunny-suit wearing researchers operate machines that etch microscopic circuits into silicon wafers, will occupy the space of three football fields. Within that space, competing companies—like memory chip companies Micron and SK Hynix—will have their own private test facilities, crammed with advanced chipmaking gear, where they will work directly with dedicated teams from Applied Materials. “Having innovators co-located together, having earlier access to the technologies that enable new AI architectures, is a complete game changer,” said Applied Materials CEO Gary Dickerson in an interview with Fortune at the event. According to the company, the collaboration at EPIC will speed up the time it takes to develop each new generation of chip architectures, cutting the typical “concept to commercialization” timeframe from 10 to 12 years to as little as six years. The ability to understand what’s technically feasible earlier on in the process increases the chance of breakthroughs and hopefully reduces the number of dead ends that researchers go through. Until companies collaborate in the lab, Dickerson said, “they don’t know what they don’t know.” The EPIC center is purely for research, not for commercial production of chips—so it’s not, in itself, likely to bring chip manufacturing back to the U.S. But it represents an important step in keeping Silicon Valley—and Applied Materials—at the forefront of cutting-edge chip technologies, a critical weapon in the race to develop ever more powerful AI models. A reminder of the stakes was on display Monday as CXMT, a memory maker in China, announced a breakthrough in memory chip density. The breakthrough, if substantiated, could allow CXMT to produce more power-efficient memory chips, beating Samsung and SK Hynix to the milestone. The news sent shares of both companies down roughly 7% and 10% respectively in South Korea, according to Bloomberg. The U.S. government has barred the sales of advanced GPU chips and advanced chipmaking equipment to China, in an effort to deprive the country of the computing brawn necessary to create AI models capable of outperforming models made by U.S. companies like OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google. Dickerson repeatedly invoked “winning the AI race” at Monday’s event, though he would not comment specifically on his views about U.S. regulations on sales to China. A blast furnace is the giant furnace that turns iron ore into the iron used to make steel. Rather than replace blast furnaces, Helix wants to clean them up. Its approach promises to slash the steel industry’s carbon emissions while boosting profit margins for iron producers, a rare win-win for a dirty industrial process. Blast furnaces have made iron for centuries, using coke, a processed form of coal, to help turn raw iron ore (iron bound up with oxygen) into metallic iron. The burning coke supplies heat for the reaction but doesn’t take part in it directly. Instead, carbon monoxide produced from the combustion does the work of reducing the iron, meaning stripping away its oxygen atoms. The process releases tremendous amounts of carbon dioxide, or CO2, which is normally dumped straight into the atmosphere. But Helix Carbon has devised a way to turn that waste carbon dioxide into carbon monoxide and recycle it back into the blast furnace. Helix Carbon’s process relies on an electrolyzer, a device that uses electricity and a catalyst to break off an oxygen atom from the carbon dioxide. Electrolyzers aren’t new, but Brown said the startup’s design can handle the entire exhaust stream from the blast furnace. Most electrolyzers are too delicate for raw exhaust like that. They work only on purified, concentrated carbon dioxide, so the exhaust typically has to be processed before they can use it. By injecting carbon monoxide into a blast furnace, iron producers can use less coke. How much, exactly, is still debated, Brown said. Some scientific papers suggest as much as 90% of the coke can be eliminated, while other studies suggest it’s more like 30% to 40%. Helix Carbon is proposing that iron producers start by replacing 33% of their coke. The star of the toe raise is the tibialis anterior, a muscle that runs along the front of your shin. If it’s possible for a dead man to roll over in his grave, Lindsey Graham must be doing just that. One would think that such an element would be an important part of demonstrating the danger of a self-replicating AI botnet, but Anthropic decided it didn’t need to worry about that for this study. “We largely ignore the cyber exploit element of the replication process, since earlier papers CyberGym, ExploitBench, and adaptive worms already show that small models are capable of cyber exploits, especially when given the right tools,” Anthropic explained in the writeup. Late last month, Anthropic released a report arguing that the model GLM-5.3 poses a major cyberattack risk. The report’s core concern is that the powerful new model, released in August by the Chinese company Z.ai, is freely available for anyone to download and modify. Anthropic’s assertion is only the latest move in a long-running debate about whether frontier AI models should be made open, like GLM-5.3, or should stay locked behind the doors of the frontier labs. These days, much of the debate about how to handle open models is playing out in public—and there’s a lot of money resting on its outcome. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, who has a lot to gain if people continue buying their own hardware to run open models, advocates enthusiastically for the United States to develop more of them, whereas Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, who runs a company that could be undermined by cheap, powerful models, advocates for a more cautious approach. But beneath all these commercial incentives, there is real substance to disagreements about open models. “I think a lot of the way you come down on the openness debate is influenced by how you come down on the AI debate,” says David Gray Widder, an assistant professor at the University of Texas at Austin School of Information. Those who are concerned about AI safety warn that open models can easily be misused by bad actors, whereas those who focus more on AI’s practical potential urge that this power should not sit behind the closed doors of just a few big companies, and that open models could help spread it more widely. There are a range of ways to handle open models between banning them entirely and releasing them willy-nilly. But any approach that a government or a society might take still involves trade-offs between safety and democratization. Deciding whether to support or limit the production of open models requires understanding those risks and benefits in depth. Let’s start with the basics. A closed model, like the models that power ChatGPT and Claude, can only be accessed through a website, app, or API. An open model, on the other hand, can be directly downloaded: The details about its internal structure, encoded in a long series of numbers known as the model weights, are publicly available. Such models are sometimes called “open-weight models,” to distinguish them from models for which even more information, such as details about the training procedure, is available. Right now, closed models are more capable than open models—as of publication, the top eight models on the Artificial Analysis LLM leaderboard are all closed, though open models are only a handful of months behind in development. But there are several reasons why someone might opt to use an open model rather than a closed one. Because open models are downloadable, people can run them on their own computers or hard drives. They can even retrain them to behave differently, if they have the expertise and resources to do so. If you’re an academic researcher working to understand and improve AI systems, you almost certainly need to work with open models in your research. When working with closed models, researchers can’t do much more than send prompts and observe the responses. But researchers can observe the inner workings of open models, as well as the “chain of thought” that the models use to think through a problem before answering. They can also combine multiple open models to build new systems, compress them to make them more efficient, and fine-tune them for new applications. Businesses, too, have reasons to prefer open models. Even if you depend on an external provider to run them for you, open models still typically cost less than closed models, according to a recent report from Mozilla. And if businesses want to use AI to process sensitive data or proprietary information, running a local model on their own hardware may be the best way to keep their operations secure. But perhaps the most important quality of open models is that they challenge the tight control the frontier labs have over how most of us use AI. “The clearest, biggest argument [for open models] is around preventing mass concentration of power—it is about making sure that this foundational technology is a public good,” says Elizabeth Seger, senior policy advisor at the Tony Blair Institute for Global Change. “I think we’re at this juncture where the question of who controls the infrastructure that allows AI to operate is as big of a question as who gets to control information on the internet.” Seger sees the future of AI playing out in different ways, depending on the role that open models have. In one possible future, frontier AI systems are controlled entirely by select people, companies, or governments, and as those systems grow more powerful, those groups have increasing control over what our world looks like. The world’s most sophisticated hacking models, for example, are currently all made in the United States, so it’s entirely up to US companies and the US government whether other countries can use them to shore up their own cyber defenses. If those companies remain in the lead, we could in principle wind up in a world in which the United States is capable of hacking any other country, but no one is capable of hacking it. Frontier open models could change that balance. “If every individual had a loyal-to-them AI system that was safe [and secure], I think that would be a much less concerning world than one in which they have to just rely on a government that says, ‘Yeah, we’ve got a super intelligent AI system, it’s got this plan, it’s inscrutable to all of you, just trust us,’” says Hamish Hobbs, director of AI policy at the Centre for Long-Term Resilience. But open models are not a geopolitical panacea. Paul Azunre, who cofounded Ghana NLP, an organization that builds open-source AI tools for low-resource Ghanaian languages, notes that open models can also function as a way for companies to benefit from the labor and expertise of less-privileged nations and consolidate more power in their own hands. He identifies a pattern in which AI companies release models openly, observe how the community uses and modifies them, and then integrate those same improvements into their own products—without any credit or compensation for the people who originated those innovations. Mark Zuckerberg himself has said that the ability to integrate community improvements into Meta’s stack is a key benefit of releasing open models. “I think the ideal of open source is a very noble one, and there’s a lot of value that it brings,” Azunre says. “But the way it’s actually implemented in practice, it tends to be a very extractive approach.” The past few months have made it clear that safety remains a gnarly problem for closed models. There’s the autonomous hack that OpenAI agents executed against Hugging Face in July, but human misuse is a real threat too: According to a recent risk report from Anthropic, several extensive hacking operations have been undertaken with the assistance of Claude. So closed models are risky enough—and open models would likely be much easier to exploit. Before Anthropic passes a user’s query to one of its closed models, it uses automated systems to detect whether that query relates to hacking, biological weapons, or other risks. When it detects that users have outsmarted those filters, it can shut down their accounts. But when someone downloads a model and runs it on their own hardware, there’s no filter checking whether their prompts are safe, and there’s no way to revoke their access to the model. There are other important lines of defense. AI companies train their models to refuse harmful requests—so even if a bad prompt gets through Claude’s filters, there’s a good chance that the model will still say no. In principle, this same defense could work for open models, because it’s intrinsic to the model itself. Unfortunately, researchers have shown that it’s surprisingly easy to retrain a “safe” open model to agree to engage in the behaviors it was previously trained to avoid. Because of these facts, some people have argued that open models are simply too dangerous to release. An open model capable enough to walk someone through synthesizing a virulent new pathogen, for example, could prove catastrophic in the wrong hands. But it would be a vast oversimplification to deem open models dangerous and closed models safe. The defenses that AI companies build around their closed models are far from perfect, and people misuse those models all the time, as the Anthropic risk report makes clear. Even though open models have even fewer defenses against misuse, bad actors might still opt to use closed models because they are more accessible than open models with similar capabilities, which require lots of expensive computer chips to run locally (and even more chips if you want to retrain them). In fact, some experts who spoke with MIT Technology Review argued, by analogy to open-source software, that open models have safety advantages over closed models. Open-source tools, such as the Linux operating system, are often seen as safer than their closed-source counterparts because their vulnerabilities have been probed and patched by a vast community of online contributors. Similarly, communities of researchers can more easily test open models for security flaws, though fixing those flaws is trickier than rewriting a line of code. Open models can also be deployed more broadly than closed models for cyber defense. Any tool that can be used for hacking can also be used to prevent it: Defenders hack their own software to uncover flaws in need of patching. Anthropic has shown how this can work for closed models with its Project Glasswing initiative, which enables some trusted companies to use its most capable models for cyber defense. But plenty of other companies were left to fend for themselves. Open models, however, can be used much more widely. After it was hacked by OpenAI agents, Hugging Face emphasized the fact that it had to depend on an open model to analyze the hack, because questions about it triggered the closed models’ security filters. Nevertheless, many experts still perceive open models as riskier than closed models. The time-tested open-source security logic doesn’t necessarily hold up when dealing with tools as powerful as today’s—and tomorrow’s—models. “Where it gets complicated is when you start taking seriously the one-off instances of particularly catastrophic incidents that might be lower probability, but if they happen would be disastrous,” says Seger. If a wildly powerful open hacking model were dropped into the world today, for example, a bad actor could devastate our technological infrastructure before anyone had a chance to shore up their defenses. An event like that could be impossible to recover from. The risks can look even more dramatic when you consider what models might do of their own accord. The OpenAI Hugging Face hack only occurred because the company’s models were being tested with many of their cybersecurity guardrails removed, and open models have very few guardrails in the first place. If open models did decide to misbehave, it might be difficult to detect what was happening because they are so decentralized. For this reason, people who take seriously the purported catastrophic risks posed by rogue agents, such as Dario Amodei, tend to be more skeptical of open models. A few years ago, the debate about open models had an all-or-nothing framing: Either you supported open models because of their potential to fight concentration of power, or you rejected them because of the safety risks. These days, however, people are expending a lot of effort to find some middle ground. One way they are doing that is by devising techniques to mitigate the safety risks of open models. Most of the safety tools used for closed models, such as filters and monitoring, aren’t applicable to open models, but there are other possibilities. Some researchers are interested in trying to train models that are never exposed to training data that teaches them about bad or otherwise undesirable behavior—an open model that never learns about hacking, for example. But purging training datasets of all references to misbehavior without harming model performance remains a challenge. Other researchers are working to build models that are resistant to retraining, so that an open model’s safety training can’t be so easily removed. The theory behind these “tamper-resistant” models makes a lot of sense: If you repeatedly try to retrain a model to be unsafe and reinforce any resistance that the model shows to that retraining, eventually you’ll get a model that’s fortified against future retraining attempts. In practice, however, no current approaches to making models tamper-resistant hold up against all tampering attacks, according to a recent preprint. But Saad Hossain, an engineer at the AI medical imaging company Deep Breathe and the paper’s lead author, hopes that future techniques will prove more effective. “My reason for optimism is that it’s a very active area of research, and a lot of people are trying to come up with new and better methods,” he says. If the risks of open models are less black-and-white than they might at first seem, so are the benefits. Publishing the internal structures, or weights, of models might not be enough to disrupt AI-mediated concentration of power. “I think there are very genuine benefits to open source in terms of deconcentrating power, but they’re not sufficient in themselves,” Hobbs says. Open weights are a bare minimum standard for open models: Researchers can learn much more about how models work if they have access to details about their training, including the data on which they were trained, but AI companies are typically reluctant to divulge this information. Accessibility matters for open models too. Without computer chips that can run sophisticated models, you can’t actually use the model weights, even if you have them. Those chips have become prohibitively expensive as AI companies snap up more and more of them. “Openness is a floor, not a ceiling, for what we need to do to ensure that our technological futures benefit everyone,” Widder says. Real openness—not only open weights, but also open training data and accessible hardware—could also bring more risks, of course. If everyone has access to advanced AI hardware and advanced models to run on it, then anyone could potentially be a hacker. But there are strategies for mitigating these risks. Many experts advocate for some version of staged release, in which models start their life closed and gradually become more and more freely accessible as their safety is assured. Figuring out exactly how to implement such strategies will require striking a careful balance between the risks and benefits of open models. But if open models are a necessary element of a positive AI future, as some experts believe, that effort will be well worth it. What it would take to build a city on the moon

Welcome to the second space race. You can be forgiven for experiencing déjà vu. Last time, it was the Soviet Union and the United States vying to be the first to plant some flags. Now it’s a sprint to the lunar surface between America and China—alongside a host of varyingly friendly and rival nations—plus a cornucopia of private spaceflight companies. And this time it looks like they’re getting ready to set up camp. There will be, the contenders say, bases on the moon, permanently inhabited castles under the stars. The notion of a moon base, fanciful as it might sound, isn’t new. But this year, under the leadership of Administrator Jared Isaacman, NASA has been flooding the zone with bold announcements. If all goes according to plan—a plan that will likely cost tens of billions of dollars to start—there will be drones, robotic landers, communication satellites, and a nuclear power plant; there will be plant terrariums and moonquake sensors; there will be mechanized scouts exploring craters for water. Initial astronaut missions to the lunar surface, which the US is currently planning to start in 2028, will be focused on exploration and technological experimentation. But the idea is that we will—some time in the 2030s—get to a point where there will be habitation quarters for long-term stays, surrounded by a network of scientific research stations, resource mining and processing plants, and landing pads for spaceships. “It’s going to be an expanse,” says Carlos García-Galán, NASA’s moon base program manager—one that will add up to “hundreds of square miles.” The effort is undoubtedly being fueled by nationalistic competition. China has near-identical plans to build its own base on the moon. And being first matters, because the “victor” gets to define the terms. What is the moon, exactly? Is it a militarized outpost? Is it a manufacturing hub? Is it a wilderness to be studied and preserved? But there are also other reasons to go. Earth’s companion contains primeval rocks that hold clues to the formation of the entire solar system. The lunar surface, with virtually no atmosphere in the way, is an ideal place to put telescopes that could examine the most ancient epochs of the cosmos. The far side of the moon would be “a fantastic place to explore the further universe,” says Josef Aschbacher, the European Space Agency’s director general. And it’s way more energy efficient to launch rockets from the low-gravity lunar surface than it is from Earth, meaning sending astronauts—or robotic spacecraft—farther into the solar system will become much less difficult. “The moon is critical. It will be a stepping stone for going further—that means to Mars and beyond,” says Aschbacher. But if these bold visions are to become reality, there’s a daunting amount of work to be done. A sprawling moon base must be safe for its astronaut inhabitants—protected from falling asteroids, endless cosmic radiation, razor-sharp lunar dust, and torturous temperature swings. It also must be somewhat self-sustaining, so that it can weather changing political fortunes and not cost ruinous amounts of money to maintain. For that, we’ll need to find a way to use the moon’s resources to keep the base operating—and ideally generating revenue. And we’ll need autonomous robots that can build, maintain, and expand the base even when their human masters aren’t present. “It’s almost like starting civilization from scratch,” says Simon Middleburgh, co-director of the Nuclear Futures Institute at Bangor University in Wales. Assuming we can overcome these starting challenges, decades from now—let’s say, by the year 2100—our initial moon bases could grow from barely visible structures into luminous cities, perhaps not too dissimilar from those dreamed up by authors and screenwriters the world over. “It’s like science fiction today that we really want to turn into a reality,” says García-Galán. Will anyone succeed? The moon is a tremendously hostile place to set up camp. In general, daytime temperatures can reach more than 250 °F; at night, they can plummet to less than –200 °F. Exposed to the vacuum of space, the surface is constantly showered in carcinogenic radiation and frequently hit by meteoroids. And its volcanic dust is sticky and corrosive, a destroyer of equipment and choker of lungs. Sadly, the lunar south pole—the area NASA is targeting—is even harsher. NASA wants to build a base there because its permanently shadowed craters are thought to harbor caches of water ice, which would be more precious than gold. Purify it, and you can water crops and hydrate astronauts; split it into oxygen and hydrogen, and you have breathable air and rocket propellant. But the temperature in those craters can be lower than –410 °F, making them some of the coldest places in the known universe. On the surface outside those pits, astronauts would be bathed in the scorching sun for 14 days before experiencing an equally long frigid night. But there is only so much money we can spend on safety. “What it comes down to is acceptable risk of death,” Philip Metzger, a spaceflight engineering researcher at the Florida Space Institute, said while pondering some of the dangers. “What can we reasonably survive without breaking the bank?” Temperature is one of the easiest problems to handle. Materials science has come a long way, and there are myriad metals, ceramics, plastics, compounds, and alloys that can resist wild temperature swings. Radiators keep things cool; heaters keep batteries from dying prematurely. Astronauts survive just fine too, with their fancy spacesuits—but that technology has proved itself only during brief excursions. It’s not yet clear how long this equipment could operate under such tempestuous conditions. The temperature in the moon’s south pole craters can be lower than –410 °F, making them some of the coldest places in the known universe. Outside those pits, astronauts would be bathed in the scorching sun for 14 days before experiencing an equally long frigid night. Space radiation, which can wreak havoc on electronics and trigger a cancerous division of cells, is far more pernicious. And as Stuart George, a physicist at NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston, explains, it comes in two unpleasant forms. The first is galactic cosmic rays, which rain down on the moon almost constantly. “They’re made by supernovas and neutron star mergers—all these kinds of things,” says George. And because the atomic nuclei, protons, and electrons that make them up are moving at significant fractions of the speed of light, these cosmic rays have oodles of kinetic energy, meaning they can punch through thin shielding (say, a metal wall) with ease. “They also have this habit where the heavier ions are fragmenting in that shielding and making secondary particles,” says George. That means if your shield is too thin, the astronauts behind it will be blasted with a surprisingly large dose of radiation. Then you have the sun. It may sustain life on Earth, but without our planet’s thick atmosphere and magnetic bubble to get in its way, moonwalkers will occasionally be blasted by one of its paroxysms: a solar flare (a burst of diverse types of radiation) or a coronal mass ejection (volleys of magnetized plasma). “During Apollo, they knew that if a large solar flare occurred while the crew was on the moon, there would be no way to keep them alive,” says Metzger. The crew could have developed acute radiation sickness and perished in a matter of days. The way to survive a powerful solar flare or coronal mass ejection is straightforward: “Get back inside a habitat where there’s more shielding,” says George—preferably a dedicated, highly shielded “storm shelter,” essentially a thick-walled bunker. The issue is that astronauts might be outside the base, driving a rover many miles away. Coronal mass ejections take hours to days to reach the vicinity of the Earth-moon system, but solar flares can do so in under an hour. And scientists can’t yet model the sun well enough to give much advance notice. “All of the capability to forecast solar storms is kind of like where weather forecasting [was] … in the ’50s and ’60s,” says George. As for those punchy galactic cosmic rays, the only way to avoid them would be to put a lot of stuff—lunar rocks, possibly—between the astronauts and the sky. “Build habitat modules underground,” says Chris Dreyer, director of engineering at the Center for Space Resources at the Colorado School of Mines. One option: Burrow living quarters into lava tubes, the naturally hollowed-­out volcanic tunnels created by molten rock eons ago. At this point, NASA hasn’t mentioned any plans to make its moon base subterranean, but it may need to if it wants to end up with a small lunar city. Radiation limits the time astronauts can spend on the lunar surface over their entire life—it can total around two years max, experts estimate, before the health risk becomes too high. Unfortunately, that’s not all space will throw at them. When the Artemis II astronauts swung around the far side of the moon earlier this year, they saw curious flashes light up the silvery land below. These were the explosive deaths of small meteoroids punching into the lunar surface. Earth is naturally protected from smaller space rocks, up to around 50 feet in diameter: They burn up inconsequentially in the atmosphere. “You don’t have that option on the moon,” says Philip Lubin, a physicist and planetary defense researcher at the University of California, Santa Barbara. Since smaller rocks plunge toward planets and moons far more frequently than larger ones do, this means the moon is often peppered by what are effectively tiny space bullets. They could easily puncture a spacesuit or the engine of a moon rover. How do you protect astronauts from the onslaught? “The simplest answer is burial,” says Nicholas Schmerr, a planetary seismologist at the University of Maryland—conveniently, the same way you’d deal with radiation exposure. Pile up moon rocks onto your base walls or put habitable structures in lava tubes. Inconveniently, there’s always a chance that a bigger asteroid will come along. Even buried habitation modules would have trouble surviving an assault from a 50-footer—about the length of a semi-trailer. That doesn’t mean a moon base would be helpless against bigger asteroids. Planetary defense has come a long way in just a few decades. NASA’s DART mission demonstrated that it could deflect an asteroid by ramming into the rock with an uncrewed spacecraft, and cutting-­edge experiments at clandestine labs around the US suggest that a nuclear bomb could swat away or entirely vaporize errant space rocks. An array of both ground-based and space-based telescopes specifically designed to spy killers—such as the ATLAS quartet of largely automated telescopes, and the soon-to-be-launched NEO Surveyor infrared telescope—are also aiming to find 90% of the near-Earth asteroids potentially capable of wiping out entire cities (those 460 feet across) in the coming years. All these protections apply to the moon. But asteroids 50 feet across are so stealthy that even NASA’s best telescopes would struggle to see them years or even months in advance. Odds are that they would first be seen not long before they were due to hit the lunar surface. Then what? It’s not unreasonable to imagine several turret-like launch sites on the moon that, owing to the lower gravity and airless environment, could rapidly fire projectiles toward an incoming asteroid. Hit it hard enough and it would break into fragments. Just make sure you shoot when it is as far away as possible: “If you were to break something up into a thousand pieces, then you have a thousand impacts on the moon,” says Lubin. “That might be worse than letting it hit one place.” Having your base destroyed by an asteroid would be awful. But a moon base is far more likely to be imperiled by dust—which, unlike asteroids, is everywhere, all the time. Volcanic lunar rocks, when broken down, turn into sharp, electrostatically charged, adhesive, acrid, abrasive particles. While on the moon, Apollo astronauts who inhaled some of this dust occasionally suffered from a strange “lunar hay fever,” which irritated their eyes and agitated their lungs. That same dust ate through all kinds of gear with disturbing ease. “It rips up seals, gears, motors, spacesuits, habitats—everything else it comes into contact with,” says Justin Cyrus, the CEO of Lunar Outpost, a spaceflight technology company that is providing NASA with one of the Artemis program’s moon rovers. NASA has already developed a defense against these dark shards: the electrodynamic dust shield, or EDS. The lightweight electrodes that make up this shield—which can be layered on machinery, walls, and perhaps even spacesuits—use opposing currents to blast away the electrically charged dust. The EDS worked wonders when tested in labs on Earth, and it also demonstrated its anti-dust magic when sent to the lunar surface in 2025 as part of a NASA-sponsored robotic mission. Amy Fritz, a dust-mitigation researcher at Johnson Space Center, says that the EDS will likely be widely featured all over a lunar habitat. But other dust-fighting instruments are being developed too—including a lint-roller-type object inspired by gecko skin and a sci-fi-esque blaster that uses ionized nitrogen gas. Its first spray will neutralize the charge of the dust to remove its stickiness; then a second burst will clear it off the surface. Irritatingly, the busier a base gets, the dustier things will become. As it expands to the size of a small city, rocket ships will need to deliver cargo and crew to the lunar surface on a frequent cadence. Each takeoff and landing will launch a salvo of dust in every direction, inundating the base’s infrastructure. “You have to worry about essentially being sandblasted every time,” says Fritz. Metzger calculates that even with landing pads more than 3,000 feet away from a base, just 10 to 20 liftoffs or landings would be sufficient to severely damage things like protective coatings on exterior walls and communications equipment. After a while, he says, “everything breaks.” Even if the moon base of the future is buried partly below ground, barricaded against micrometeorites and space radiation, and well equipped with dust-repelling technologies, it’s still a risky gig for any astronaut. Skipping around in a preternaturally agile way at one-sixth of Earth’s gravity will be entertaining. But “microgravity is not good for the human body,” says Dreyer. Muscle wastage, cardiovascular troubles, respiratory problems, and immune deficiencies can arise. Radiation, too, may always afflict lunar residents. And there’s also evidence that disease-causing microbes could stealthily sneak aboard spacecraft, even after stringent sterilization procedures. Those microbes may behave differently than they do on Earth; microgravity experiments (both real and simulated) have suggested that some become more aggressive and more likely to cause disease. “We call them the invisible crew,” says Lisa Carnell, the director of NASA’s Biological and Physical Sciences Division. Hoping to find a better way to protect astronauts, Carnell spearheaded an experiment called AVATAR (A Virtual Astronaut Tissue Analog Response), which launched aboard the Artemis II mission. It used thumb-drive-size chips, containing cells taken from the heart, lungs, or other parts of the body, that could be switched on to act like miniature organs. Scientists have previously used this organ-on-a-chip technology for purposes like testing out new pharmaceuticals in a highly controlled setting. For Artemis II, Carnell’s crew sent up chips containing bone marrow samples taken from the mission’s four astronauts. Bone marrow makes red and white blood cells, as well as platelets—the sort of stuff that affects almost the entire human body. So seeing how space radiation and microgravity affect it would give Carnell and her team vital insight into how astronauts’ biology might change on the moon. The advantage of organ chips is that you can make them for any astronaut and then send them into space ahead of time to see how that person’s organs will react to spaceflight. Researchers envision that data from such chips will be able to clue medics in to the specific preventive treatments or pharmaceuticals that will help moonbound astronauts on their journey. What is NASA likely to send up sooner rather than later? “Brain organoids,” says Carnell. Yes, she adds—that sounds super weird. But it would be good to know how a real brain might respond to being on the moon for several months before people start living there. Okay. You can’t make them do all the heavy lifting, which means that whether you’re trying to make a tiny lunar outpost or a small city, you’re going to need a lot of robots. “The buildup of the moon base, in the first years, will pretty much 99% be robotic,” says NASA’s García-Galán. Autonomy will be there right from the start. NASA’s new version of the moon buggy, its Lunar Terrain Vehicle, can be driven by the astronauts riding in it but can also be piloted remotely from Earth (or from elsewhere on the moon). There are visions, too, of adding modular robotic arms, towing vehicles, and cargo movers—all still in the preliminary stages of planning. Over the next few decades, though, we’ll likely want an army of robots to do our bidding: excavators to dig up lunar rocks that could be turned into concrete and bricks, plus other robots to flatten surfaces, dig trenches, create building foundations, pave landing pads, and build up dust-deflecting berms to keep the base from being sandblasted by launches and landings. Even solar panels and wiring can potentially be made from moon rocks—something that the spaceflight company Blue Origin demonstrated in labs on Earth in 2023 using a geologic simulant designed to match the composition of lunar regolith. Powering this robot army will require considerable energy. Two-week-long nights make solar power an impractical solution. Aside from a cornucopia of high-­capacity, extreme-temperature-resistant batteries, nuclear power is probably the only viable option. A small, car-size nuclear reactor, filled with an almost literal handful of uranium, could power a small village for a decade without needing to be refueled. And although nobody’s yet tested a nuclear reactor on the moon, few doubt that NASA and the US Department of Energy can conjure up a working design—one that perhaps wouldn’t be too different from those aboard nuclear submarines. A moon base in 2100 would probably feature multiple small, autonomously or remotely operated nuclear reactors, which—conveniently—the base-expanding robots could simply plug themselves into to recharge. A garden on the moon would do more than just provide food, as recent experiences on the ISS suggest. In a research station in central Panama, you can walk through a century of forest time, watching scrubby plots of adolescent trees give way to cathedral-­like canopies of mature growth. Forest ecologist Daisy Dent, a senior scientist at ETH Zurich, oversees this sequence of plots across the Barro Colorado Nature Monument, some of which date as far back as the 19th century. Not one of the trees she surveys was planted by human hands. She maps, measures, and tracks each trunk that emerges, revealing how waves of tree life rise from bare earth. Tropical forests like these are our planet’s biggest terrestrial carbon sink, accounting for around 40% of carbon stored on land. As part of an international network of researchers, Dent is deciphering the networks of animal and plant interactions that enable some forests to recover after destruction. The goal is a low-cost, high-impact alternative to typical carbon-­crediting schemes, which plant huge areas with a single species of tree. For over a decade, her research has built a body of evidence that such large “monocrop” programs could be expensive mistakes—and that more resilient forests can rebound naturally in just a few decades, re-creating ecosystems that suck up vast quantities of carbon dioxide. Few places are better to demonstrate this than Barro Colorado, a reserve covering the forested peninsulas and islands that formed when construction of the Panama Canal flooded the area in 1914. Under the management of the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute, generations of ecologists have amassed more than a century of records. That means Dent can trace why some plots rebound after clear-cutting for farming and forestry while others falter. Fruit-eating animals—including spider monkeys, bats, and parrots—are responsible for planting more than 70% of trees in tropical forests, and Dent’s dataset can tell which built each patch of trees by depositing seeds in their nutrient-rich droppings. Many of these creatures worked together to plant a diverse 70-year-old patch whose canopy is approaching the stunning structure and richness of an ancient jungle. In contrast, a patch of similar age populated by narrow trunks of a single long-lived species, Gustavia superba, hints that tropical rodents called agoutis are more or less the sole animal architects behind this more homogeneous forest. The data she collects helps write a playbook for when to let ecosystems regenerate and when to give them a helping hand. One 2024 estimate in Nature found that some 215 million hectares of forest worldwide (an area larger than Mexico) could regenerate naturally. “Tropical forests are underpinned by this massive network of interactions,” Dent says—interactions between life-forms like the fungi that help trees take up nutrients, as well as pollinators and seed-distributors. Supporting those relationships is key to growing forests that lock up carbon and help sustain local communities and wildlife. Just over a decade ago, as the landmark Paris Agreement was being codified in 2015, forest recovery seized global attention as a solution to the climate crisis. Global leaders rallied behind plans to regrow a trillion trees, after the ecologist Thomas Crowther highlighted their potential to capture a decade’s worth of global carbon emissions. But the biggest question has lingered: How, exactly, do you regrow two trees for every star in the Milky Way? While startups have proposed seed-spraying drones and nutrient-coated seed bombs, Dent has spent two decades studying what happens when we let forests rebuild themselves. As a PhD student around the turn of the millennium, she researched old-growth forests in northern Borneo but felt hopeless watching entire landscapes felled for palm oil plantations. Rather than despair, she turned to restoration. Then, as a postdoc in 2005, she joined Panama’s Prorena, a joint initiative between the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute and the Yale School of Forestry and Environmental Studies, which aimed at determining how best to establish diverse native forests on clear-cut land. Back then, secondary forests—ones that grow naturally after the original forest was lost—had been “a bit of a niche research area,” Dent says. But as farming and human infrastructure have driven deforestation, resurgent forests now account for some 70% of all tropical canopies. Prorena initially followed the usual playbook of transplanting nursery trees to rangelands, but Dent began to question whether planting was necessary. In 2009, she published an encouraging paper showing that animal populations rebound quickly as forests naturally regrow. In 2010, she began working in Barro Colorado on the plots established by the pioneering American ecologist Julie Denslow. In 1994, Denslow identified plots that had been regenerating naturally for 20 to 100 years, and Dent would add younger ones to continue the sequence. The site provides not just general data on forest regrowth but in-depth demographic information over time, says Robin Chazdon, a professor emeritus of ecology and evolutionary biology at the University of Connecticut who collaborated with Denslow when she established the plots. “It tells you more about the actual processes that are happening in the site,” she says. For example, previous research had shown that fast-growing “pioneer trees,” the first to thrive in the damaged land, are eventually overtaken by larger carbon-storing trees. Dent’s research helped fill in gaps about the mechanics of how this happens—or doesn’t happen—in individual cases. In the Gustavia-dominated patch, for instance, stubborn grasses planted by cattle ranchers smothered most other trees before they could take root. These trees can hang around, crowding out the light, for 150 years, blocking the second wave of large-trunked trees. Dent found herself well placed for the massive influx of interest in reforestation that began in 2015. She’d already become a principal investigator for 2ndFor, an international research network aiming to learn how best to manage secondary forests. Using data gathered from the Barro Colorado plots, the team authored a 2016 paper in Nature showing that a hectare of naturally regenerated secondary forest can capture 11 times as much carbon each year as an old-growth one, with young trees absorbing it quickly as they compete to grow toward the sun. Then, in a 2022 study published by the Royal Society, Dent and colleagues showed that locations such as Barro Colorado are among the best-case scenarios for what can be achieved without planting a single seed. They’re also working to replant tree species that can accelerate growth—what Comita calls assisted natural regeneration. Such sites almost always need some intervention, Comita explains. Many demand hands-on actions, such as enrichment planting. Today, the questions projects like Ponterra are asking are closer to her own research, says Dent. For instance, they want to understand if solitary standing trees, often left by ranchers to shade cows, help sites regenerate faster. Those insights can help Ponterra sell high-quality credits and avoid the embarrassing pitfalls that can lead fresh plantations to literally go up in smoke. While credits can be bought for as little as $5 per metric ton, corporate giants such as Microsoft—one of Ponterra’s clients—have paid more than 10 times as much for high-quality removal. There are plenty of places where a level of natural regeneration can work well. So far, millions of hectares of rainforest are naturally regenerating in Brazil, according to the Brazilian Restoration and Reforestation Observatory. The conversation may be shifting. As droughts hit Latin America, policymakers and carbon creditors might be forced to face the shortcomings of cookie-cutter plantations. “You have much reduced resilience in the system when it comes to extreme events like droughts or floods or extreme heat or pathogens,” Dent says. What makes the refusal useful rather than annoying is what comes with it. A decline from the engine carries the finding, what was read first, the verdict, the reason, the one fact that would change the verdict, and the steps to do it by hand. “A refusal without evidence is a shrug,” Segal says. “A refusal with the evidence is a decision you can defend at nine in the morning. Declined is an answer.” The second break with the category is about trust, and it inverts how the industry has sold automation for twenty years. The convention is a dial. The vendor ships the capability, the customer chooses how much of it to switch on, and the risk of the choice belongs to the customer. Tamnoon’s engine starts with the dial at zero. It connects read-only and changes nothing in the customer’s cloud. It is granted the right to act on a class of finding only after its record on that class holds, and the customer keeps the veto throughout. “You keep the veto,” Segal says. “It earns the keys. And it can lose them. Autonomy isn’t a feature you buy. It’s a track record, and a track record is something you can read.” The unit of that record is the receipt. Every decision the engine makes, closures and refusals alike, becomes one: six fields, readable by an auditor without a translator. When a customer’s engineer overrides the engine, the override goes into the record too, and becomes how the engine judges that environment next time. It gets smarter from the whole fleet and stricter about each customer’s cloud. “The board will never ask you how fast the fix was,” Segal says. “They will ask how you knew it was safe. We built the answer to the second question.” Segal is careful with the phrase, because operations people hear it and think of pods restarting. “Your cloud already restarts itself,” she says. “It has never once closed its own security hole. Healing means the exposure is gone, through your own change process, and someone can defend how.” Her picture of the next five years is specific. Security teams stop being the liability boundary for changes they did not design. The backlog stops being a filing cabinet and becomes a queue with verdicts in it. Engineers see one problem where they used to see 142 alerts, which is the ratio Tamnoon’s cross-scanner model produces on average. The same eight misconfigurations stop coming back every quarter, because what heals once stays healed. And autonomy stops being a checkbox in a vendor’s settings and becomes something granted per class of finding, like a credit limit, on evidence. Three predictions come with it, and she is willing to be wrong about them in public. Refusal becomes a line in every remediation RFP. The receipt replaces the score as the thing buyers compare. And no vendor sells remediation without showing a decline. “In five years nobody will buy remediation from a vendor who can’t show them what it refused,” she says. “The question will sound obvious. Today nobody asks it.” The customers who have run the engine for a full year describe the change in ratios. One went from 6,074 open findings to 2,041 over four quarters, three times fewer, with 93% fewer new alerts arriving by the end. One healthcare company priced a single finding end to end: $8,775 to close it by hand, $775 through the engine. Warner Music Group is a named customer. Tamnoon has raised more than $18 million, with a Series A led by Bright Pixel Capital, and its engine reads findings from seventeen scanners, Wiz and CrowdStrike Falcon Cloud Security among them. Today Tamnoon publishes two things this market does not usually publish. The first is a number. Cloud security has always published how fast attackers move. It has been much quieter about how slowly it heals. The Cloud Remediation Index, out this morning, comes from the same 14.86 million detections behind the company’s report, and it answers two questions in one place: what share of everything ever detected is still open, and how long a critical finding stays open. The attacker’s clock is measured in minutes. This one is measured in months. The second is a record. As of today, Tamnoon keeps a public page of the remediations its engine declined, each with the six fields. “Every company in this industry publishes its wins,” Segal says. “We are publishing the changes we did not make. It is a strange list to be proud of, until you ask which of the two lists a security team should read before it lets a machine into production.” Alongside them, the company has published the six questions it says buyers should put to any remediation vendor, itself included. The first: can it refuse a change, and show one refusal with the evidence attached. “Everyone demos the fix,” Segal says. “Ask them to demo the decline.” It is an unusual way to relaunch a security company, leading with what the product will not do and a number the industry rarely publishes about itself. But the conventional way has had a decade, and half of everything it found is still open. Segal’s bet is that the second decade of cloud security belongs to the machine that has earned the right to touch production, one refusal at a time. The company’s own words for it are eight.
