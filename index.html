Nintendo's biggest ROM chip supplier is bowing out of the business

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-04

Visit Original Website
For years, the AI industry’s insatiable demand for memory and storage has had plenty of knock-on effects on the price and availability of PC components like RAM, SSDs, and GPUs. Now those shortages are also squeezing the supply of ROM chips that power physical Nintendo Switch and Switch 2 game cartridges. Chip manufacturer Macronix announced in its third-quarter earnings report that the company’s production of ROM chips “has reached its end” and that it will begin “gradually decoupling from the game industry,” as reported by Taiwanese news site BigGo Finance. That could be an issue for Nintendo, which analyst David Gibson notes uses Macronix as its “main memory chip maker” for physical game cartridges. That ROM production—which has shrunk 39 percent in the last year and now represents just 5 percent of Macronix’s business—is being crowded out by production of more lucrative, in-demand chips like NOR- and NAND-based memory and eMMCs, the company said. With Macronix’s production capacity stuck at 25,000 12-inch wafers a month—and $1.2 billion in planned new capacity taking a year or more to spin up—less profitable ROM chips are taking up wafer space that can earn more through other types of chips. This doesn’t mean Nintendo will follow Sony into a world without physical game production. On the contrary, the Switch maker said last month that it had no plans to give up on the game cartridges in the near future. But those cartridges could get more expensive to produce as Nintendo is forced to rely on suppliers like Rohm and Lapis Semiconductor for its remaining ROM chips. That could have a knock-on effect on the prices publishers charge for physical Switch cartridges, too. Earlier this year, Nintendo started charging more for some physical games than for the equivalent digital download. And last year, Japanese publisher Inin only committed to a full Switch cartridge release for R-Type Dimensions III after it said “two new smaller cartridge [storage capacity] sizes for Nintendo Switch 2” lowered the cost of producing those carts. On the other hand, Gibson notes that digital software sales represented 61.5 percent of Nintendo’s business in the second calendar quarter of 2026, up slightly from the year before. If that number keeps increasing, lower demand for ROM-based Nintendo cartridges could end up balancing out the costs involved in due time.

Hackers possibly linked to the Kremlin exploited previously patched iOS and Android flaws in an attempt to hack visitors of multiple websites, including one celebrating the 250th anniversary of American independence, researchers said Wednesday. The attackers, which the researchers assessed with “moderate to high confidence” were part of Russia’s Foreign Intelligence Service, targeted other sites, many with links to that country’s ongoing war against Ukraine. The attacks began when iPhone or Android users visited one of potentially hundreds of different sites. JavaScript injected into the traffic then profiled the device. The final payloads, tailored to the type of platform visiting, attempted to steal browser cookies and, in some cases, Wi-Fi passwords and full mobile device data. One of the impersonated sites was america250.org, belonging to a non-partisan group of lawmakers, business executives, and at least one former member of the Trump administration. The attackers registered america250[.]holiday and used it as a reverse proxy to america250.org. The imposter website delivered content from the real one that added malicious JavaScript into it. “We assess that the intrusion set may have deliberately selected this website due to its association with a high-profile national celebration, potentially leveraging the event as a pretext for invitation-themed spear-phishing campaigns,” researchers from security firm Sekoia said. A second website, theowo[.]club, proxied the website Raffles of London OWO, a luxury hotel physically close to some of the UK’s most sensitive government institutions, including the Ministry of Defence and 10 Downing Street, the official residence and office of the British Prime Minister. Open source reporting indicates that Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy stayed there during a visit on October 14. “By doing this watering hole [impersonating] the Raffles London at The OWO, the intrusion set may have sought to target diplomatic delegations and high-profile guests through targeted spear-phishing campaigns,” the researchers said. The attackers also delivered their attacks through compromised websites—including a top Ukrainian gas production company—and a Ukrainian web analytics service that serves thousands of websites. Sekoia said its attribution to the Kremlin is based on the targets’ profiles and similarities to a previous attack attributed to APT29, one of the names the suspected Russian group is tracked under. The hackers relied on three infection vectors: direct JavaScript injection into traffic from legitimate websites, proxy domains such as america250[.]holiday, and the supply-chain compromise of the Ukrainian web analytics service. In each case, the injected JavaScript was the same. During the first stage, the JavaScript profiled the visitor’s browser and device. The attackers then used that information to deliver one of three zero-click exploit chains, which work without requiring any interaction from the target. One of the chains was DarkSword, an iOS exploit kit that Google researchers said customers of PARS Defense, a commercial surveillance vendor in Turkey, and at least two other threat actors have used. Sekoia said the more recent campaign used two other exploit chains that weren’t named. All of the vulnerabilities exploited in the campaign were “n-days,” meaning they had already been patched and attacks worked only on outdated versions of iOS or Android.

Uranium cubes reveal why the German nuclear project failed

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-04

Visit Original Website
During World War II, physicists on the Manhattan Project worried that the Nazi German nuclear program might achieve the first nuclear bomb before the US and its allies. That’s not what transpired, but historians have debated just how close the Germans came to achieving criticality in their last attempt at building a reactor: the B8 pile. Werner Heisenberg, who led that effort, claimed that they were close and had only needed a little more uranium. But according to a paper published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences Nexus, that was not the case: the project simply didn’t have sufficient uranium and heavy water to build a working bomb, given the design they were using. This particular research started in 2013, when University of Maryland physicist Timothy Koeth—a co-author on the new paper—received the gift of a mysterious heavy metal cube from a friend. He instantly recognized it as one of the uranium cubes used by German scientists in their unsuccessful attempt to build a working nuclear reactor. Only 14 such cubes are known to exist today. In 2019, we reported on Koeth’s quest to track down the cube’s origins, as well as several other similar cubes that had somehow found their way across the Atlantic. At the time, Koeth and his partner in the quest, then-graduate student Miriam “Mimi” Hiebert, had tracked down ten such cubes around the US. For instance, the Smithsonian Institute had a German uranium cube in storage. They tracked a third cube to Harvard University, where it was regularly passed around to students in introductory physics classes as a curiosity. Underpinning the Manhattan Project in the US was the fear that German scientists under Adolf Hitler’s Nazi regime would beat the Allies to a nuclear bomb. The Germans had a two-year head start, but according to Koeth, “fierce competition over finite resources, bitter interpersonal rivalries, and ineffectual scientific management” resulted in significant delays in their progress toward achieving a sustained nuclear reaction. German nuclear scientists were separated into three isolated groups based in Berlin (B), Gottow (G), and Leipzig (L). Heisenberg headed the Berlin group, and as the Allied forces advanced in the winter of 1944, Heisenberg moved his team to a cave under a castle in a small town called Haigerloch—now the site of the Atomkeller Museum. That’s where the group built the B8 reactor. It resembled an “ominous chandelier,” per Koeth, because it was composed of 664 uranium cubes strung together with aircraft cable and then submerged in a tank of heavy water shielded by graphite to prevent radiation exposure. As the German scientists were racing against time, Manhattan Project lead Lieutenant General Leslie Groves kicked off a covert mission dubbed “Alsos,” with the express purpose of gathering information and materials related to Germany’s scientific research. When Allied forces closed in, Heisenberg took apart the B8 experiment and buried the uranium cubes in a field, ferreting away key documentation in a latrine. (Pity Samuel Goudsmit, the poor physicist who had to dig those out.) Heisenberg escaped by bicycle, carrying a few cubes in a backpack. As Heisenberg reported, the German scientists’ final experiment failed because the amount of uranium in the cubes was insufficient to trigger a sustained nuclear reaction. But Heisenberg was confident that “a slight increase in its size would have been sufficient to start off the process of energy production.” A model described in a 2009 paper supported that, showing the group would have needed only 50 percent more uranium cubes to make the design work. Koeth and Hiebert uncovered a box of declassified documents about German uranium in the National Archives and found about 400 other uranium cubes from a separate reactor experiment by the Gottow group. They concluded that the combined inventory would have been more than enough to achieve criticality in the B8 reactor, suggesting that Germany’s secretive, isolationist approach hampered their nuclear program because the two groups weren’t sharing information or resources. This latest paper builds on that earlier work but reaches a different conclusion. Koeth, Hiebert, and their co-authors analyzed two of the surviving uranium cubes, along with combing through historical records, technical reports, correspondence, and similar archival sources to learn more about the uranium, heavy water, and graphite blocks used for the B8 reactor experiment. Among their findings: The B8 reactor would have needed between 1,292 and 1,775 uranium cubes to achieve criticality, and twice the heavy water produced by the Norsk Hydro plant in Vemork, Norway—the only source at the time—over the course of the entire war. In short, “Our calculations suggest Heisenberg significantly underestimated the materials required,” the authors wrote. The authors also cited prior work noting key differences in American and German graphite production. The former used petroleum coke. While some of this supply reached Germany, the British naval blockade in January 1940 deprived the Germans of that vital resource. The German nuclear physicists had to use metallurgical coke from the Ruhr, which had twice as many impurities as American coke. (The US scientists had similar issues until they located an unusually low-boron coke in Pennsylvania.) Thus, “no natural uranium, graphite-moderated pile could have gone critical” with the materials available to the German scientists at the time. PNAS Nexus, 2026. After alienating many long-time small- to medium-sized business (SMB) customers, Broadcom’s VMware is trying to appeal to its smaller customers. Today, the virtualization company announced vSphere Express, a lower-end virtualization offering for organizations that require “cost-effective essential virtualization.” vSphere Express comes after many customers reported that VMware was pushing them to VMware Cloud Foundation (VCF), the flagship private cloud bundle that has many features SMBs don’t need and is expensive. Some SMB users have reported that VMware sales representatives previously told them that vSphere Standard, VMware’s prior entry-level virtualization offering, wasn’t available anymore. A VMware spokesperson told The Register that VMware will sell a per-core subscription license at a price that will be “significantly lower” than the VMware vSphere Foundation (VVF) bundle and VCF but wouldn’t provide a price quote. IT distributor TD Synnex lists vSphere Express as costing $9,600 a year for running on up to 128 cores. The price doesn’t get cheaper with fewer cores, which still presents a challenge to the smallest use cases. vSphere Express includes basic capabilities, like vMotion, for “live migration of running virtual machines with improved migration speed and efficiency,” and high availability for the “automated restart of failed virtual machines,” VMware’s announcement said. The product only supports discrete instances, and organizations won’t be able to manage them centrally, Prashanth Shenoy, VP of product marketing for VCF, said, according to The Register. “We started listening to a lot of our lower-end customers who didn’t really want the VVF but wanted the core virtualization capability, and [vSphere Express is] really meant for that market,” Shenoy said, per SDxCentral. Whereas vSphere Standard hasn’t seen a meaningful update in years, vSphere Express will be updated as frequently as VCF and VVF are, The Register reported. Returning focus to a streamlined low-end virtualization product is an about-face for VMware, which has largely focused on enterprise-size customers since Broadcom’s takeover. This focus shift has been evident as the tech giant has reduced the number of channel partners that it works with, bundled its product lineup into a small number of expensive SKUs, and canceled plans to take its biggest clients direct. While VMware has been financially successful under Broadcom, vSphere Express suggests Broadcom may worry about losing business from smaller organizations and, more broadly, that its changes are pushing customers of various sizes to explore their options. In September, while teasing what would become vSphere Express, Paul Turner, VCF’s chief product officer, told The Register that Broadcom issued sales incentives that pushed its sales team to sell VCF. He said that VMware had “too big a focus on VCF.” Nick Watson, chief technologist at UK-based tech advisory firm C4C Group, told TechTarget today: “My reading is that Express exists because customers looked at VCF, did the sums, and started packing. This is Broadcom putting a door back on the building.” Migrating off VMware can be very costly and time-consuming, making matters particularly complicated for SMBs. For organizations that can’t currently juggle a migration alongside other projects and/or have limited resources, vSphere Express can be a much simpler and more easily attainable solution than changing virtualization platforms. Some SMBs, though, including the thousands of firms that have already migrated to VMware rivals and firms whose preferred resellers no longer work with VMware, will be much harder to sway, though. “Broadcom has lost all credibility. No thanks. We have two more locations to migrate off VMware and we’re done with this nightmare. Not playing these games,” a Reddit user said last week in response to details shared about vSphere Express. Speaking with Ars Technica in September, Dean Colpitts, CTO of Canadian managed services provider and former VMware partner Members IT Group, noted that even though migrating to the new low-end virtualization offering may be easy, Broadcom has “already shown both customers and partners they can’t be trusted.”

AMD attributes the growth to demand for its EPYC line of enterprise server CPUs, along with the launch of the Instinct MI450 series GPUs. Sales of AMD Ryzen mobile chips led growth in the Client business, which includes desktop Ryzen processors and posted $2.95 billion in revenue, up 7 percent year over year. Gaming revenue fell sharply for the company, posting a 40 percent drop in revenue at $773 million, which AMD attributes to “lower semi-custom and graphics revenue.” Operating margins in AMD’s data center business expanded from 25 percent to 34 percent, with operating income surging 168 percent to $2.88 billion. Donald Trump’s big, rapid American AI buildout now faces a major slowdown as voters in yesterday’s elections overwhelmingly supported candidates and measures that favored increasing restrictions on AI and data centers nationwide. One resource from a polling firm, called Redbud Consulting, tracked data center initiatives on the ballot and reported that out of 44 measures across 10 states seeking to restrict data centers, 38 passed so far. Those measures included dozens of moratoriums and bans. Analysts suggested those won’t impact data centers already under construction or approved through 2027, instead largely impacting future projects planned for 2028. Only one measure passed allowing a data center to proceed. But even that small win represented pushback on the AI industry—requiring data center developers in a North Dakota city to pay for independent reviews and hold public hearings when expanding or proposing projects. In addition to increasing regulations on data centers, voters also backed anti-AI candidates, electing officials vowing crackdowns in key states building out the most data center capacity in the US. Most notably, Democratic challenger Gina Hinojosa won the Texas governor race that Axios reported would fundamentally scramble the math on Trump’s AI buildout. According to Bloomberg, Hinojosa’s election has developers facing down the “worst-case scenario.” Texas is the epicenter of the AI boom, Axios noted, and was expected to account for more than 41 percent of added data center capacity in 2028. Now, Hinojosa plans to delay projects as long as it takes to pass laws “tackling impacts on water, electricity, air, and property rights.” Approximately “13 gigawatts of data center capacity” is now exposed, Bloomberg reported. Gordon Johnson of GLJ Research warned in a note that Texas projects with “power, permits and steel keep going,” but “those with power but no state environmental permits slip 12 to 18 months,” Bloomberg reported. Some projects may leave the state entirely, perhaps moving to Louisiana or Georgia—or perhaps going nowhere. “She would impose a moratorium until legislation is passed that gives communities a voice, protects household utility bills, and ends the tax incentives for data centers,” Johnson’s note said. “The question now is how long the pause lasts.” As PIMCO head of public policy Libby Cantrill similarly warned, “even if statewide moratoria do not result, we expect to see a lot more sand in the gears of the AI buildout regardless—with more time required to secure permits and other approvals, as well as more concessions that data center developers will have to make.” Texas wasn’t the only big shake-up. Democrats won governor races in the next three biggest states previously wooing data center developers—Arizona, Georgia, and Ohio. Overall, Axios projected that “nearly two-thirds of 2028 data center capacity” could face similar delays as Hinojosa threatens in Texas. Bloomberg estimated that half the data centers in the US are now more vulnerable to scrutiny. For AI firms, that’s a nightmare, since any delays will set back revenue, and IT consulting firm Gartner recently estimated “the AI boom is nearly $5 trillion short of paying for itself.” Likely soon, developers nationwide will face tighter rules limiting tax incentives and requiring stronger protections for residents around who pays for grid infrastructure and water use. Those kinds of regulations will most likely increase developers’ costs at an inconvenient time, Bloomberg’s report said. While many reports suggested that the first so-called “AI election” proved the power of the people to rein in Big Tech, some AI industry insiders downplayed the election results on Wednesday. In a statement, Adam Kovacevich—a former Google executive and the CEO of a tech industry trade group and policy coalition called Chamber of Progress—suggested “the results offer little evidence of a voter mandate to crack down on AI or data centers.” He emphasized that “Democratic gubernatorial candidates who opposed data center bans won at a higher rate than those who supported them,” and admonished, “Let’s not confuse an anti-Trump wave with an anti-tech mandate.” “Voters wanted change on the economy, not a crackdown on innovation,” Kovacevich said, while sharing a resource for those in the tech industry seeking an alternative spin on the results. Additionally, more than half of voters said that “AI and data centers were a crucial or major factor in their vote for Congress.” The mass backlash is not just about utility costs or other data center fears, Montoya-Boyer suggested, but instead stemmed from a call from voters to regulate AI. Election day came “as the biggest AI companies raced to go public, AI systems broke out of the test ‘sandboxes’ meant to contain them, and biased and discriminatory AI kept making life-altering decisions about us,” Montoya-Boyer said. “These results confirm numerous polls and what we civil rights leaders have been hearing from the communities we represent: Innovation is only real when it includes all of us and actually improves our lives,” Montoya-Boyer said. Other analysts told Axios that voters appeared to be “more concerned about efforts to block state AI regulation than they are about data center controversies,” with one noting that across four swing districts, “voters trust Democrats over Republicans to hold AI executives accountable by a margin of 7 to 10 points.” Brad Carson, co-founder of Americans for Responsible Innovation, told Axios that “voters just sent a clear message that they want rules for AI and data centers.” “What we just witnessed was an AI earthquake,” Carson said. Gary Ng Cheuk-yan, a senior economist at a global financial institute called the Natixis Corporate and Investment Bank, told the South China Morning Post that “a slowdown in the US could be a win for China.” Like analysts who projected that Democrats won’t try to block all data center development, Ng suggested that the AI buildout won’t come to a complete halt following the “blue wave” unseating many Republicans. However, a Democrat-controlled Congress will likely “introduce new constraints on US computing capacity,” and that could give China a leg up, Ng suggested. “This would keep [US] AI token prices high, giving China an opportunity to export cost-effective inference services, supported by abundant electricity supplies,” Ng said. At the furthest extreme, US data center opposition could push giants like Amazon or Google to build hyperscalers outside the US, Ng suggested. That outcome represents the opposite of Trump’s vision for 100 percent US-made tech, which experts have warned is an impossible dream. Supply chain shocks have piled up since 2020, following the COVID pandemic, Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine, Trump’s 2025 tariffs and the end of de minimis, and finally the U.S. war with Iran and the closed Strait of Hormuz. Before 2020, Knudsen said resilience meant reacting—waiting for a crisis, then calling for help. “And then we call our banks, our logistics companies, and we say, ‘Hey, can you help us get out of this problem?’” he said at the Fortune Southeast Asia 500 Summit in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam. “Now suddenly you’re looking at your tier two, three, and four suppliers,” he added. “We’ve got to build a supply chain that’s much more robust.” Toll Group, founded in 1888 and currently owned by Japan Post, runs freight forwarding and contract logistics across Asia. In practice, robustness means spare capacity: backup routes by truck and rail alongside ocean and air, and sea-air combinations that fly cargo part of the way and ship it the rest. “Today we’re doing trucking. Tomorrow we’re doing rail. We’re complementing with ocean and air,” Knudsen explained. But robustness has limits. For low-value goods like cement, he noted, building a resilient supply chain is difficult simply because of the cost. Tariffs have also forced Chinese companies to look to new markets and manufacturing hubs. “Is that going to move back? I don’t think so,” Knudsen said. For El-Sayed and other progressives, Medicare for All remains the gold standard, and a single public payer would replace much of the private insurance business that sustains some of America’s largest companies. Dr. Morissa Schwartz is an entrepreneur, author and AI strategist who founded GenZ Publishing and the communications firm Dr. Rissy’s Writing & Marketing. She holds a doctorate in literature and was named to Forbes’ 30 Under 30 list in Media in 2022. I opened my daily to-do list and found 156 items. “Daily” was doing some ambitious work in that sentence. I’m a writer, publisher and entrepreneur. I expect a certain amount of chaos. There are manuscripts, emails, projects, follow-ups and the occasional administrative mystery that arrives disguised as a perfectly ordinary Tuesday. Still, 156 items suggested that my productivity system had developed career ambitions of its own. It was building a department. Soon it would need an org chart. Some of the work was already underway. Some items overlapped. Some were reminders about other reminders. Tasks that belonged together had become separate obligations, each carrying the emotional weight of an unfinished thing. I had asked for help getting work done. I was getting very good at maintaining evidence that work existed. I use AI, and I remain a big believer in what it can do. It helps organize ideas and get thoughts onto a page. Technology that makes expression and work more accessible matters to me. That is why I want to be honest about the less glamorous part: an assistant can generate work faster than it can finish it. Ask it to help with a project, and it may produce a plan. Ask it to improve the plan, and it may produce a checklist. Ask it to make sure nothing gets missed, and the checklist may develop subcommittees. Every item can sound reasonable on its own. Collectively, they can turn help into a second job. The problem starts with an easy confusion: identifying the next step feels like progress. Sometimes it is. A clear plan can get a stuck project moving. But a plan also has a comforting property: it can be created without confronting the hardest part of the work. A draft can sit there looking polished. A calendar can fill up with preparation. A task can be checked off because someone wrote instructions for completing it. Meanwhile, the email still needs sending. The application still needs submitting. The client still needs the finished file. A productivity system earns its keep when those things happen. After the 156-item surprise, I started insisting on a more useful question: What would “done” look like to the person who asked for this? For an article, that could mean a finished draft, if a draft was requested. If the request was to pitch it, “done” includes an actual pitch. If the request was to publish it, a document called FINAL_FINAL_REALLY_FINAL does not quite complete the journey. The distinction sounds obvious. It gets surprisingly slippery once work passes through several tools, checklists and assistants. First, I want a daily list that reflects available capacity. For assistant execution, I’m aiming for roughly 25 meaningful entries on a weekday, while preserving real deadlines and necessary daily operations. The number is a working limit for my system, rather than a productivity prescription for everyone. Its purpose is to force a choice. If everything is due today, the list has stopped helping me decide what to do today. Flexible work can live in a backlog with a reasonable checkpoint. Waiting for someone’s response deserves a waiting status. A genuine deadline deserves space that has not already been occupied by 40 vaguely urgent reminders. A campaign may involve research, outreach, tracking and follow-ups. Those requirements still matter. Putting them under one clear execution item can preserve the work while reducing the number of separate things shouting at me. The details should remain available when they are useful. I don’t need every detail to greet me at the top of the stairs. Third, I want proof attached to completion. If an assistant says an email was sent, I should be able to see it. If it says something was published, I need the live link. If it says a file was delivered, I need the usable file and the delivery record. That proof also helps prevent duplicate work. A system that remembers what actually happened can stop proposing the same action with slightly different punctuation. “Unable to complete” gives me another mystery to manage. “The form requires an answer only you can provide” gives me a decision. “This browser session cannot access the account” identifies an access problem. The next question is whether anything else can move forward. One missing answer should not freeze every unrelated part of a project. Finally, I want the system to count outcomes. How many pitches actually went out? Which replies need an answer? What has been accepted? Which deliverables reached the client? What remains unfinished? I care about those numbers because they describe the work I was trying to accomplish. A magnificent list of possible actions describes the system’s imagination. These changes require judgment. Some days have more real deadlines. Some tasks take minutes; others need uninterrupted hours. A short list can still be unrealistic, and a long checklist can be useful inside a complicated project. The goal is a system that helps me see the difference. AI can be extraordinarily helpful here. It can reconcile records, find overlaps, preserve context and handle routine steps. But it needs instructions that reward finishing, checking and communicating honestly about what remains. It also needs permission to stop manufacturing another plan when the next useful action is already clear. I still want an assistant. I still want technology to make work easier and possibilities bigger. I would just like my to-do list to stop recruiting. A New York appeals court on Wednesday threw out a lawsuit over the rollout of Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s pied-à-terre tax, ruling that the homeowners who sued had not adequately shown they were harmed. The Appellate Division, Second Department, reversed a Sept. 29 judgment by Staten Island Supreme Court Justice Wayne Ozzi that had granted the homeowners injunctive and declaratory relief. The four-judge panel was unanimous. It denied the petition and dismissed the case a week after hearing arguments. Seven homeowners, represented by former First Deputy Mayor Randy Mastro, sued the city in August. They challenged the Department of Finance’s publication of a supplemental roll of about 900,000 properties and the roughly 17,000 notices it mailed to owners who might owe the surcharge, arguing the city made owners prove they live in their homes instead of checking its own records first. The court did not rule on that argument. It found the homeowners lacked standing, or the legal right to sue, because they showed no concrete injury. Appearing on the roll or receiving a notice wasn’t enough, the judges wrote. The notice, court documents read, “serves an informational purpose,” like a debt collection letter, and the state legislature “must have anticipated that the list of notice recipients would not be exact or flawless,” or it would not have created an exemption process. The court also said the rollout confusion was too “generalized, tenuous, and ephemeral” to count as an injury. It said the homeowners offered no evidence that anyone had misused the information or that they faced stalking or fraud, and it noted that the city’s annual assessment rolls already list each owner’s name, assessed value and market value. The court also said that the cost of applying for an exemption was not enough cause for injury, as all taxpayers spend time and money complying with tax rules, and the homeowners had not documented or quantified their costs. Two of the seven petitioners, Rachel O’Brien and Carmine Morano, never alleged they received a notice. The other five were exempted after the suit was filed. The panel sided with the homeowners on two points: It rejected the city’s argument that exempting the petitioners made the case moot, and its argument that the tax law limits challenges to appeals before the city’s Tax Commission. It also declined to decide whether DOF broke its own rule by not relying on 2024 income-tax data when it chose who got notices. The ruling does not settle the tax’s other legal fights. Gothamist reported the surcharge faces three separate challenges. The city has also changed course since the notices went out. After the state sent early 2025 income-tax data on Aug. 12, DOF cleared 1,210 owners and said it would send new letters to about 10,800 others. Gothamist reported that DOF has now granted more than 8,800 exemption requests. Comptroller Mark Levine estimated earlier this year the tax could raise up to $380 million from about 11,200 properties. Mamdani has said it will raise $500 million a year. Conventional wisdom holds that foreign policy doesn’t affect the outcome of American elections, but it cratered President Donald Trump’s party in this one, at least indirectly. Trump will probably resist the effort, claiming his powers, under Article 2 of the Constitution, as the armed forces’ commander in chief. He also threatened to veto bills that he considers “insane,” a term he applies to anything that challenges his prerogatives and priorities. But a few caveats are worth noting. First, Democrats are projected to win at least 250 of the 435 seats in the House—just 40 seats short of the two-thirds needed to override a presidential veto. And given the widespread unpopularity of Trump’s war, tariffs, and other unilateral aggressions, it may not be so difficult to rally 40 Republicans to join an override campaign. A huge consequence of the Democrats’ victory—especially its scale and margin, even in states that had previously been seen as Republican sure things—is the evaporation of Trump’s biggest reservoir of power: the fear that kept the entire GOP in his thrall. It is worth noting that the Vietnam War was ended in 1973 by a congressional amendment that cut off funding for any combat activities in the region. It’s also worth noting that the Senate at first narrowly defeated the measure but then passed it overwhelmingly a year later, after the 1972 elections—which widened the Democrats’ majority by two seats—and the deepening revulsion toward President Richard Nixon amid the Watergate scandal. The language of the bill, sponsored in the Senate by Republican Clifford Case and Democrat Frank Church, was very specific: None of the funds herein appropriated under this Act may be expended to support directly or indirectly combat activities in or over Cambodia, Laos, North Vietnam and South Vietnam or off the shores of Cambodia, Laos, North Vietnam and South Vietnam by United States forces, and after August 15, 1973, no other funds heretofore appropriate under any other act may be expended for such purpose. Nearly identical language could be drafted this January, with a good chance of success, substituting the names of Iran and various other Middle Eastern countries.
