ULA's Vulcan rocket is set to return to flight on Friday the 13th

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-28

Visit Original Website
United Launch Alliance announced Wednesday it plans to return its Vulcan rocket to service on November 13, nine months after a solid rocket booster anomaly threw the company’s 2026 launch schedule into oblivion. The return-to-flight mission will launch 40 satellites for the Amazon Leo broadband constellation, the first of 38 Vulcan flights Amazon has booked to deploy the bulk of its network. The mission will lift off from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, Florida, and ULA said the launch date is pending range approval. The launch is critical for ULA after its new flagship rocket suffered nozzle failures on its solid rocket boosters on two of its first four missions, most recently during a flight in February. In both cases, the rocket overcame the nozzle failure and completed its mission, but officials at the US Space Force, ULA’s primary customer, lost confidence in Vulcan. Space Force officials will closely scrutinize Vulcan’s return-to-flight mission. The Pentagon has entrusted ULA’s Vulcan rocket for roughly half of its major launches through the early 2030s. The Space Force certified the Vulcan rocket for national security launches in 2025, then flew two missions on Vulcan before its grounding after the February booster incident. In a statement, United Launch Alliance said engineers “made great strides” in understanding the booster anomaly that occurred in February. Engineers “have identified several potential contributors, which have been mitigated through design and process improvements to the nozzle,” ULA said. “To support our customers, this upcoming launch will utilize the improved nozzle design, and additional testing and analysis will continue in parallel with the mission,” ULA said. “By flying the improved design, ULA gains valuable flight data that validates these improvements and is the most beneficial path in support of all our customers.” ULA and Northrop Grumman, the booster manufacturer, have not publicly identified the root cause of the nozzle malfunction in February. Investigators blamed the nozzle problem’s first occurrence in 2024 on a manufacturing defect in an insulator meant to protect the nozzle’s metallic structure from the rocket’s superheated exhaust. Neither company responded to questions from Ars on Wednesday. The Space Force is counting on United Launch Alliance, a 50-50 joint venture between Boeing and Lockheed Martin, to deliver into orbit a backlog of the military’s communications, navigation, missile warning, and surveillance satellites. Amazon Leo, a potential competitor to SpaceX’s Starlink network, also has an inventory of broadband satellites in storage waiting for rides on the Vulcan rocket. With ULA’s delays, the Space Force has moved six missions off the Vulcan rocket to expedite their launch on SpaceX Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy rockets. ULA received six future missions originally assigned to SpaceX, the military’s other certified launch provider. The swaps included three GPS satellites that moved off ULA’s Vulcan rocket for launches last year and earlier this year on SpaceX Falcon 9s. Space Systems Command, which oversees military launch acquisition and procurement, also swapped one ULA launch for the Space Development Agency with another on SpaceX. A launch carrying the Air Force Research Laboratory’s Oracle-M lunar mission will now fly on SpaceX’s Falcon Heavy rocket instead of ULA’s Vulcan, which will receive a future SpaceX-assigned launch in compensation. Space Systems Command completed a similar swap to move a large strategic missile warning satellite from Vulcan to Falcon Heavy. So far the swaps have had no effect on the number of missions the Space Force has awarded to ULA and SpaceX. The trades are separate from the Space Force’s assignment of unallocated future missions to launch providers, which may already be hamstrung by SpaceX’s plans to retire the Falcon rocket family. “These swaps are executed in coordination with and with the support of both launch service providers,” a Space Systems Command spokesperson said. “We are grateful for the flexibility shown by our launch service providers during these swaps to help us ensure we can deliver capabilities to the warfighter when and where they are needed.” Brig. Gen. Eric Zarybnisky, the chief rocket buyer at Space Systems Command, said the military wants to see data from commercial flights on the Vulcan rocket before clearing ULA to resume national security launches. At least two Amazon Leo missions are on the Vulcan launch schedule before it flies another national security mission, perhaps early next year. “Those flights will have a significant number of solids,” Zarybnisky said. “It’ll depend a lot on what we see, not only on the engineering and tests that we’re doing on the ground, but also what we see from the flight data from the commercial missions they fly.” The Vulcan launch next month will be the first to fly with a full complement of six solid rocket boosters. The flight will also debut an extended payload fairing to accommodate Amazon’s satellite stack and a shortened Centaur upper stage optimized for low-Earth orbit missions.

As US ties fray, Europe plans first undersea cable link to Greenland

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-28

Visit Original Website
The European Union plans to create the first subsea Internet cable directly connecting Europe to Greenland. The Pan-Arctic Cable project could also extend to Canada and eventually link with Japan and South Korea, notably bypassing the United States. The Pan-Arctic Cable is described as a European Union flagship project in a European Commission joint communication published on October 20, 2026. The policy document describes a new European strategy for the Arctic that supports “a sovereign, secure and resilient submarine cable connection linking Greenland directly to the EU,” while also suggesting that “like-minded partners like Canada” could provide a stepping stone for future cable expansion toward Asia. “It is interesting that this project is even being considered, because five years ago it would have seemed like not a particularly commercially attractive proposition,” Elisabeth Braw, a senior fellow at the Atlantic Council who leads the Threats to the Global Maritime Order Initiative, told Ars. “But the fact that it’s being considered is very much on trend and a sign of our times.” The project has gained official support after repeated Trump administration attempts to claim Greenland were finally settled through a trilateral security agreement between the United States, Greenland and Denmark in September 2026. The agreement preserves Greenland’s autonomous territory status within the Kingdom of Denmark while expanding US military access. But relations have grown increasingly strained between the United States and its allies in Europe and Canada since President Trump initiated trade wars with multiple countries and has taken an increasingly pro-Russia stance on the war in Ukraine. Europe has also been eyeing new Internet cable routes to Asia because more than 90 percent of communications between Europe and Asia run through undersea cables in the Red Sea, according to TeleGeography. Maintaining such cables has become riskier since open warfare resumed between the Yemeni government and Houthi faction this year, with Houthi forces having fired missiles and drones at commercial ships in the Bab el-Mandeb Strait chokepoint and the broader Red Sea. The Pan-Arctic Cable took shape when European companies and research organizations joined forces with a Greenland telecom operator to sign an agreement on developing the project in 2025, according to Submarine Cable Networks. Since that time, the project has received European Union backing through the Connecting Europe Facility Digital program. The European Union has already invested $119 million (107 million euros) to “advance Greenland’s secure and sovereign connectivity” along with funding the Pan-Arctic Cable plan, according to the new European Commission policy document on Europe’s Arctic strategy. But the goal of eventually extending the undersea cable to Japan and South Korea by way of Canada faces considerable challenges, including requiring more European icebreaker ships to support the installation and maintenance of the cable in icy Arctic waters. Multiple pan-Arctic projects have been proposed in the past and some are still in development. One earlier project known as the Arctic Connect subsea cable aimed to connect Europe and Asia using the Northern Sea Route running along Russia’s Arctic coastline—but that was eventually halted in May 2021 before the full-scale Russian invasion of Ukraine led to growing Russian isolation from Europe. Still, the Pan-Arctic Cable is not the only subsea cable project that could strengthen Europe’s ties to friendlier nations. In September 2026, the Norwegian company Bulk Infrastructure Group AS began looking for a Canadian partner to take a majority ownership stake in a new transatlantic cable directly linking Europe to Canada, according to Bloomberg News. That proposed project has been named the Leif Erikson system. In previous decades, “geopolitics played virtually no role when companies considered where to put their cables and what countries to connect,” said Braw, whose book Undersea War explores why the world has become more dangerous for the Internet’s undersea backbone. But the focus on maximizing data connectivity and Internet traffic has given way to more complex planning that also accounts for international relations and the growing risk of adversaries carrying out surveillance or sabotage of cables. “We are going to see more cables that connect friendly countries, and then you have to figure out how you can connect friendly countries without going through unfriendly countries’ exclusive economic zones or territorial waters,” Braw told Ars. Growing international tensions have coincided with suspicious incidents of Russian- and Chinese-linked commercial ships severing European undersea cables in the Baltic Sea by dragging their anchors for long periods of time. In spring 2026, NATO countries’ forces also confronted Russian submarines that were practicing how to disable undersea cables in Arctic waters near Norway, Reuters reported. Europe’s new Arctic strategy document addresses such security threats by stating that the European Union will establish an Arctic Cable Hub to “monitor, protect and support the repair of submarine cables in the North Atlantic-Arctic.” It also says the European Union will invest in “smart technologies on cables that act as early warning systems for potential threats.” Such risks for undersea infrastructure are not limited to the Baltic Sea or Atlantic Ocean. In the Pacific Ocean, Chinese-linked commercial ships have also severed undersea cables near Taiwan. Analysts have documented Chinese research vessels spending unusual amounts of time near Pacific undersea cables and warned of the rising risks for US-linked subsea cable projects that are often owned by Big Tech companies such as Google and Meta. Meanwhile, Iran has threatened to charge fees to subsea cable operators whose cables run through the Strait of Hormuz chokepoint. That action comes as part of a broader regional war in the Middle East that began when the United States and Israel attacked Iran in February 2026—and Iran has drawn up plans to potentially attack undersea cables if hostilities ramp up once again.

Peptide peddlers cheer as RFK Jr. unilaterally grants access, overruling FDA

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-28

Visit Original Website
Anti-vaccine Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy has unilaterally passed a rule that will allow specialty pharmacies to sell six specific peptide drugs, overruling scientists at the Food and Drug Administration. The peptides are popular among members of Kennedy’s Make America Health Again movement and many prominent MAHA figures and Kennedy allies sell them. Kennedy said he uses some of the peptides and called himself a “big fan.” But FDA scientists continue to say there is insufficient evidence to show if they’re effective and they are potentially dangerous. In 2023, the safety concerns led the FDA to remove the six peptides from a list of drugs that can be made by specialty pharmacies called compounding pharmacies—which make customized formulations of drugs for patients, or, in cases such as this, specialty drugs in bulk. Kennedy’s new rule sidesteps the need to formally undo that FDA action. The rule is titled “Enforcement Discretion Policy Regarding 503A Compounding Using Certain Peptide Bulk Drug Substances” and was made available Wednesday on the Federal Register, with the final publication data set for October 30. The rule does not change the regulatory status of the peptide; instead, it states the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) will no longer take enforcement action against compounding pharmacies for making the peptides, essentially giving them a green light to freely produce and sell them. Kennedy had quietly proposed this rule earlier this month. Along with the notice in the Federal Register, HHS also posted a more detailed explanation of the policy today that includes more of Kennedy’s rationale for easing enforcement of the six select peptides, which are: BPC-157, Epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, Semax, and TB-500. One reason cited for all the peptides was that an FDA advisory panel voted in July to add these six peptides back to the list of drugs compound pharmacies can make. However, prior to the vote, Kennedy stacked that 12-member committee with eight new members. At least six of Kennedy’s additions have ties to peptide-related businesses and clinics, raising serious conflict-of-interest concerns. Another explanation for allowing the peptides is that the safety concerns are mainly hypothetical or “inferential” and not based on observed harm in humans. But this is mainly because there are few to no studies involving humans that show either benefits or harms from the peptides. Still, Kennedy argues in the policy that making the peptides more available through compounding pharmacies will improve safety because it will keep people from buying them on the gray or black market or buying formulas not intended for patient use. “The Secretary of HHS has determined that this limited enforcement-discretion policy will discourage the continued, widespread use of certain research-use-only peptides supplied by foreign manufacturers that pose a safety risk to US patients,” the HHS policy document states. Calling the compounded peptides “a more controlled alternative” to illegal versions of the peptides, the document says that “the Secretary has determined that the policy advances patient choice and freedom while appropriately protecting patient safety.” Peptide sellers online were quick to celebrate the news. Brigham Buhler, who sells peptides in a wellness clinic he runs, cheered the news on X, thanking Kennedy, President Trump, Calley Means, and Joe Rogan. “Medical freedom just took a major step forward!” he wrote. Zachary Shurtleff, a marketing executive for a wellness-focused compounding pharmacy, also celebrated, writing, ” Peptides are BACK like they never shoulda left!” Lee Rosebush, chair of the American Academy of Peptide Medicine, a group formed last year, released a statement thanking Kennedy and the Trump administration for “taking a forward-thinking, evidence-based approach to the regulation of peptides.” Critics have pushed back, noting that there are already reports of harms from peptides. In a statement released Tuesday, before the new rule was released, the Center for Science in the Public Interest laid out several reasons why making unproven peptides more accessible is dangerous. For one, reports of harms will “continue to mount,” CSPI said. And if any of the peptides actually showed promise of efficacy, reputable pharmaceutical companies will have less incentive to follow up with thorough clinical trials if the peptide is already widely available. Further, allowing access to peptides based on marketing demand could open the doors for other unapproved products, creating an incentive for illegal sales. Fred Mills, chief policy officer of Americans for Safe and Effective Medicines (ASEM), said in a statement Wednesday that the new rule “does not resolve problems with untested products, safety risks, questionable ingredients, or misleading claims. In fact, we believe this will worsen what we have already seen—the harms from mass compounding hitting communities hard due to a lack of oversight to rein in bad actors. We need to follow the science, and the reality is that FDA itself has continued to sound the alarm on lackluster data on these peptides. Safety and efficacy aren’t a nice-to-have; they are required.”

Trump admin sues Utah to save Kalshi from the state's total ban on gambling

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-28

The Trump administration is trying to stop Utah from enforcing its gambling ban against Kalshi, making Utah the 10th state sued by the US government agency that regulates prediction markets. Utah, which prohibits gambling, sued Kalshi in a state court last week. Utah’s lawsuit seeks an order prohibiting Kalshi from offering bets on sports, elections, politics, and other events. “Kalshi runs an online casino in Utah and calls it a prediction market. That violates Utah law,” Utah Attorney General Derek Brown, a Republican, said last week. “Not only do our state laws ban gambling but Utah is one of only two states where gambling is also prohibited under the state constitution. For 130 years, Utah has been clear: gambling has no place here, and we will enforce our laws.” Utah already defeated Kalshi in federal court after the prediction market filed a preemptive lawsuit that sought to prevent the state from enforcing its gambling laws. Now that Utah is moving ahead in its attempt to shut down Kalshi, it must defend against a lawsuit filed by the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC). The CFTC “has filed a lawsuit against Utah to block the state’s efforts to shut down CFTC-registered contract markets using state laws,” the commission said yesterday. “The lawsuit follows Utah’s decision to file civil enforcement actions in state court against CFTC-regulated markets and other businesses. Utah’s effort to restrict the functioning of CFTC-registered exchanges obstructs Congress’s decision to federally preempt state law.” If Utah can shut down Kalshi, that could spell trouble for Kalshi’s chief prediction-market rival, Polymarket, and for sports betting sites like DraftKings and FanDuel. DraftKings and FanDuel advertise availability in all US states via a mix of products, including prediction-market platforms in states like Utah. Both are registered with the CFTC. The Utah attorney general’s office declined to comment today on the CFTC lawsuit. The CFTC previously filed lawsuits against Arizona, Connecticut, Illinois, Kentucky, Minnesota, New Mexico, New York, Rhode Island, and Wisconsin. The federal agency also filed briefs supporting Kalshi in appeals court cases. “Utah seeks to shut down prediction markets across a wide variety of markets,” CFTC Chairman Michael Selig said. “Event contracts do not just serve hedging and investment purposes—they also provide the public with information about the outcome of future events. Congress entrusted the CFTC to be the federal regulator with sole responsibility to oversee these markets. As I’ve said before, the CFTC has the expertise and responsibility to defend its exclusive jurisdiction over commodity derivatives, and that’s exactly what we’ll do.” The US Commodity Exchange Act (CEA) gives the CFTC authority to regulate swaps. The key legal questions being grappled with by courts throughout the US are whether event contracts offered by prediction markets qualify as swaps, and whether the CFTC jurisdiction over swaps preempts states from applying their gambling laws. The CFTC is suing Utah in the same US district court that previously ruled in Utah’s favor. Kalshi sued Utah in February in an attempt to stop the state from bringing an enforcement action against the prediction market. In August, US District Judge Robert Shelby granted Utah’s motion for summary judgment and denied Kalshi’s motion for a preliminary injunction. Shelby wrote, “[T]he court concludes the federal law relied upon by Kalshi does not preempt Utah’s ability to enforce its anti-gambling laws.” Shelby determined the text of US law makes it clear that state regulation is not forbidden. US law states that CFTC jurisdiction does not “supersede or limit the jurisdiction conferred on courts of the United States or any State,” and “explicitly provides for State regulation of its gaming laws in all but a few specified cases,” he wrote. “Congress is aware that some States permit gambling while others do not, and the CEA explicitly provides for State jurisdiction,” Shelby wrote. Kalshi is now appealing its loss to the US Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit but was unable to obtain an injunction preventing enforcement of the state gambling ban while that litigation continues. On September 8, Tenth Circuit judges denied Kalshi’s emergency motion for an injunction pending appeal. Utah filed its lawsuit seeking a shutdown of Kalshi about six weeks later. The CFTC lawsuit against Utah makes arguments similar to the ones already rejected by the district court, although some other courts have ruled otherwise. “Utah is not the first State that has attempted to invade the Commission’s exclusive jurisdiction over swaps,” the CFTC lawsuit said. “Already, several federal courts—including the Third Circuit—have swiftly responded by issuing or affirming temporary restraining orders and preliminary injunctions barring the States from enforcing their gambling laws against CFTC-regulated exchanges that offer sports-related or other event contracts.” The Third Circuit appeals court ruled that New Jersey cannot regulate sports bets on prediction markets. But Kalshi lost similar cases in the Sixth Circuit and Ninth Circuit in rulings that went in favor of Ohio, Tennessee, and Nevada. Because circuit appeals courts are divided on the issue, it is likely that the Supreme Court will take up the case and determine whether states can apply their gambling laws to prediction markets. New Jersey petitioned the Supreme Court to resolve the circuit split, and 39 other states and the District of Columbia filed a brief supporting New Jersey’s petition.

The last, best hope for space tourism just rolled onto a runway in Arizona

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-28

The private space tourism era looked so very promising five years ago. Blue Origin and Virgin Galactic had begun flying customers—real people with zero space experience—on suborbital missions. And at about the same time, private citizens were going to orbit for days at a time aboard SpaceX’s Crew Dragon. But that was then. Consider the state of play today: So we are now 10 months, and counting, without a space tourism mission in the United States. At the time of Unity‘s final flight, Virgin Galactic said it was moving on to the second generation of its suborbital spacecraft. It planned to bring two of these Delta-class ships into service by the year 2026. Of course, it is now 2026, and there are no Delta-class ships in service. But apparently we are getting closer to that goal. On Wednesday, the first Delta-class ship received a name: Apeiron. According to Virgin Galactic’s news release, the name is drawn from the ancient Greek concept of the boundless, which is an interesting choice for a vehicle that will forever be bound by Earth’s gravity. It was chosen through a public vote. “We’re proud that the lead ship in Virgin Galactic’s Spaceship fleet will carry a name chosen by the people who share our excitement for what comes next,” said Michael Colglazier, chief executive officer of Virgin Galactic. But more significantly, the company said Apeiron would be moved from its Spaceship Factory in Mesa, Arizona, to Spaceport America in New Mexico later on Wednesday. Apeiron won’t be making a free flight anytime soon. After being carried to New Mexico by the VMS Eve, aircraft engineers and technicians there will complete work on the vehicle’s avionics equipment and flight software. Following additional ground tests, Apeiron will be mated again to Eve for captive carry tests, followed by a drop test and ultimately a powered test flight above 80 km. Presently, the company is saying it now expects Apeiron’s first commercial spaceflight to take place “early” in the second quarter of 2027. As with every other projected date in aerospace, this should be taken with a grain of salt. But it seems plausible that Apeiron may begin flying paying customers on suborbital space tourism missions over the next 12 to 18 months. There is considerable skepticism about the commercial viability of Virgin Galactic’s space tourism ambitions. Success will require its vehicles to fly nearly flawlessly and with unprecedented durability to bring hundreds of people to space each year briefly. As this reality has dawned on investors, the markets have not been kind to the company in recent years. In 2021, at the height of space tourism euphoria, the company’s stock briefly reached a stratospheric valuation of $1,118 a share. More recently it has been trading in the vicinity $2.50 a share. Whatever you might think of Virgin Galactic, however, it seems clear that any hopes of expanding the aperture of space tourism in the next decade will be flying beneath its wings.

Touchscreen MacBook Pro hands-on: OLED looks great on a Mac

LOS ANGELES—For about five minutes on an extremely crowded showroom floor, I got to take pictures of the new MacBook Pro and test out some of its new features. First impressions: It’s not as much of a departure from the previous design as I expected, but the outstanding OLED display steals the show regardless. The first thing I noticed was that it’s lighter. My daily driver is a 14-inch MacBook Pro (M1 Max), and it’s a bit weighty for a 14-incher. Picking up the new model, I could immediately tell it was lighter. It’s not like it’s in a different general weight class, but it’s a noticeable difference. The next thing I noticed was the Dynamic Island. Given the larger screen real estate of a laptop, it’s much less dominant on the screen than it is on an iPhone. I do worry about what will happen with fullscreen games, which I wasn’t able to test today. The notch MacBook Pro has an option to maximize apps or games to just below the notch, so it doesn’t cut into the image. I’m curious to test in the full review whether that remains the case with this little cutout. And lastly, the Apple logo on the back glows again! Some find this tacky, some find it essential. I just think it’s a little bit nostalgic. Of course, you might make the case that the most notable thing about this laptop is not its weight or the Dynamic Island. For years, people have debated whether Apple should introduce a touchscreen laptop. Steve Jobs was famously against it. A few years ago—there were reports that under Tim Cook and now John Ternus—Apple decided to move ahead with the idea. I played with it a bit, and it works just about exactly as you expect for a laptop touchscreen. A few of the things I did with the touchscreen during my brief time with the laptop: All of that is nice to have, I guess, but there’s not much you can do with it that you couldn’t already do with trackpad gestures, at least at the OS level; some creative apps, like finger painting on the screen, seemed to offer a bit more. When the credible reports emerged that the MacBook Pro would get a touchscreen, I imagined a convertible with Apple Pencil support—something you could use as a large iPad or drawing tablet, essentially. That’s not what this is; it only takes the usual laptop shape, and I was told it does not support the Apple Pencil. It’s just a MacBook Pro, in its normal form factor, but you can use your fingers on the screen to do things like drag files around, swipe between desktop spaces, or pinch-to-zoom on maps or images. But while it’s not about the touchscreen capability for me, I was impressed by the OLED screen itself—and I’m quite the display snob, if I’m being honest. The peak brightness, absolute blacks, well-balanced color, and dynamic range were genuinely remarkable. It’s an enormous upgrade over the MiniLED screen in the previous MacBook Pro. It took far too long for OLED to come to the MacBook Pro, but unsurprisingly, Apple spared no expense and cut no immediately obvious corners on quality. I was also happy to see that the cutout near the top of the screen that has replaced the camera notch is what Apple calls a Dynamic Island; it can resize to show live activities and other information on the fly. That’s one of my favorite features on the iPhone, as I’ve been impressed with Apple’s ability to turn something that should be an albatross into an appealing feature. Based on my time with the device and today’s announcement, the screen seems to be the main draw (though it’s nice that it noticeably weighs less, too), but the price is the real barrier. A 16-inch model with an M5 Max, 64 GB of RAM, and 2 TB of storage is currently priced at $5,799. Sure, that’s a high-end spec, but that’s some serious cash. Much of that is due to the storage and memory; upgrading from 64GB to 128GB adds a whopping $1,600 to that. Sure, most people don’t need anywhere near that much RAM—but some might for future-proofing for local AI workflows. I’m looking forward to spending more time with the new MacBook Pro, but for right now, it seems to follow in the lineup’s existing tradition: impressively high-end components like an outstanding OLED panel, but a very steep price to match. No surprises here! Though after well over a decade of speculation and back-and-forth, you’d be forgiven for being surprised that the touchscreen thing actually finally happened.

Microsoft’s cloud business is humming even as spending to build out its AI business remains high. The software giant’s first-quarter earnings report Wednesday showed ample demand for its AI services, as Microsoft races to put more computing capacity online and as other parts of its business, such as legacy software and gaming, lag. The Redmond, Wash.-based company said first-quarter revenue totaled $92.3 billion, up 19% year-over-year and above what Wall Street analysts expected. Its profit was $36.6 billion, also edging out analyst predictions. Its Azure cloud business grew by 48%, ahead of analyst estimates and a welcome sign for investors. The company’s shares were trading up around 2.5% in after-hours trading. Microsoft investors have been eager to see Azure continue to grow at a high clip and for the company to prove that its AI bets, such as its Copilot assistant, are generating solid revenue. Both are important metrics because they show whether Microsoft, and the AI industry in general, is sustaining demand, especially as tech giants spend gobs of cash on building data centers. On Wednesday, Microsoft Chief Financial Officer Amy Hood said in a call with analysts that calendar-year spending on capital expenditures would reach about $170 billion, with the company expecting CapEx to exceed $50 billion during the current quarter. Microsoft said that its first-quarter CapEx reached $45.2 billion, higher than the previous quarter but less than what analysts predicted. Hood said the figure reflected some delays the company had seen in expected third-party capacity deliveries. “We still have demand that exceeds supply,” Hood said. Hood also said total revenue is expected to be between $96.0 billion and $97.1 billion during the current quarter, above what analysts expected. Operating margins are expected to be down slightly year-over-year. Microsoft is one of several so-called hyperscalers spending big to meet AI compute demands, with Amazon, Microsoft, Google parent Alphabet, and Meta Platforms collectively expected to devote more than $700 billion to capital expenditures in 2026, mostly for data centers. Microsoft’s Wednesday earnings comes in the middle of a run of major tech earnings reports. Meta said last week that its capital expenditures this year would surge to as much as $145 billion, and that its quarterly free cash flow, or the cash it has after expenses, had turned negative, sending its shares down. Alphabet on Wednesday reported gains in its cloud division but a weaker performance in advertising, and Amazon is scheduled to post on Thursday. Hood said that she expects Microsoft to remain free cash flow positive during its 2027 fiscal year. Microsoft didn’t provide an update on Copilot sales specifically, but Chief Executive Satya Nadella said the number of Copilot customers actively consuming credits through usage-based billing grew more than 75% during the quarter. In its previous quarterly report, Microsoft said Copilot had 30 million paid seats, up from 20 million the quarter before. Investors have been anxious about the Copilot business after it had a slow start. Microsoft, for its part, has reimagined Copilot, recently turning the service into a “super app” that will span coding, chat, and autonomous functions. It also this month unveiled new features for its Windows operating system that let users work with AI agents, along with a plan to integrate seamlessly with Copilot. Microsoft has benefited from the AI boom primarily because of its cloud computing service, which helps AI companies train their models and make them available through Azure, but the company has also strained to get computing capacity online and to balance the needs of its partners with those of its own proprietary AI models. Earlier this week, AI company Anthropic released the prospectus for its upcoming initial public offering, which provided insight into just how much Microsoft and other hyperscalers are benefiting from the AI lab’s needs. Anthropic has committed to spending at least $44.9 billion on Microsoft compute between this November and May 2033, regardless of actual usage. That is part of Anthropic’s commitment of at least $389.9 billion for compute spending to companies including Amazon, Alphabet, and SpaceXAI. Microsoft’s Wednesday report arrives during a time of great change at the 51-year-old company. This week, it offered buyouts to roughly 60,000 employees, or about 27% of its workforce, as it continues a company-wide pivot into AI that has pressured it to cut costs. Eligible employees must accept by the first week of December, though Microsoft expects fewer than 10% of employees will take the offer. In July, Microsoft cut about 4,800 jobs, following more than 15,000 layoffs in 2025. Many of the recent cuts have been centered in its struggling Xbox unit, which has dragged on overall financial performance. On Wednesday, Microsoft said Xbox content and services revenue decreased by 2%, partially offset by growth in first-party content, while its hardware revenue fell by 14%. Microsoft’s Windows OEM and Devices revenue decreased by 22%, weighing down its overall Devices and Consumer segment. For the current quarter, the company projects Xbox content revenue to grow in the mid-to-high single digits, driven by launches of first- and third-party content. The UK government commissioned an independent review in November 2025 into why so many young people are becoming detached from work and education. The review is led by Alan Milburn, a former UK health secretary. Its interim report, published in August 2026, argues that the problem is about more than the economic cost of young people being out of work. The report describes some young people spending much of their time at home, often using personal devices and looking for work online. They may be searching for jobs while having fewer opportunities to meet people face to face, build relationships and develop confidence through participating in everyday life. The UK government has proposed new technical and vocational routes into work in England. These may help young people gain practical qualifications, but education has a wider role to play than that. It can also give young people opportunities to develop the confidence and interpersonal skills that can later help them participate in working and community life. Digital technology is woven into everyday life, and the Covid-19 pandemic disrupted face-to-face education and social interaction for millions of children and young people. Online learning and relationships can be valuable, of course, but they do not always provide the same opportunities to practise interacting with people in person. Schools, colleges and universities do more than teach academic subjects. Group projects help young people learn to contribute to a shared task and consider other people’s ideas. Presentations offer practice in communicating with an audience. Sport involves cooperation and negotiation, while clubs and performances create opportunities to meet people, build friendships and become part of a community. These experiences can help young people develop capabilities that are difficult to measure through conventional academic tests. They also give them opportunities to learn by doing. Things like working through disagreements, sharing responsibility and discovering how to contribute to a group. Such opportunities are not equally available to everyone, and schools cannot address every cause of social isolation or youth unemployment. But we should not assume that all young people will develop these capabilities automatically. If education is increasingly focused on academic content and formal qualifications, then educators need to consider how interpersonal skills are developed alongside them. The Welsh government introduced the curriculum for Wales in schools from 2022. It offers one example of a broader approach. Its four purposes include helping young people become capable learners, creative contributors, informed citizens and confident individuals. These aims recognise that education can develop qualities beyond academic knowledge. Educators could build on this approach by creating deliberate opportunities for young people to practise communication, collaboration and participation, and by recognising their progress. Rather than leaving these capabilities to develop as an accidental byproduct of school life, they could become an explicit part of what education is designed to achieve. This does not mean replacing academic learning with so-called ‘soft skills’, or making schools responsible for every aspect of young people’s wellbeing. Academic and technical knowledge remain essential. The point is that education should also recognise the value of learning to work with others, navigate different perspectives and contribute to shared activities. For young people who feel disconnected from education, work or their local communities, group activities may offer opportunities to build confidence and connections. They cannot guarantee employment, nor can they replace suitable jobs, training or support. But they can form part of a broader effort to help young people participate in society, rather than preparing them solely to pass exams. For a 14-year-old thinking about the future, advice should therefore go beyond choosing the right subjects or finding the fastest route into a job. Young people also need opportunities to learn how to work with others, communicate their ideas and take part in the world around them. Update your preferences in Account Settings Have you ever had a disturbing thought pop into your head out of nowhere—something you'd never consider actually doing? Intrusive thoughts are unwanted thoughts, images, or urges that can feel upsetting, confusing, or wildly out of character. They're more common in people with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) but can happen to anyone, and they can involve everything from violence and sex to religion, relationships, and the fear of having done something wrong. Many people are able to brush off the occasional intrusive thought and move on quickly. People with OCD, on the other hand, can get stuck trying to figure out what the thought means—and whether they might act on it. “We stop and we treat that thought as super important and meaningful,” says Alissa Jerud, a clinical psychologist and clinical assistant professor of psychiatry at the University of Pennsylvania. Intrusive thoughts can feel especially disturbing because they often involve someone or something the person cherishes. “We tend to have intrusive thoughts about the things we care most about,” Jerud says. Some of Jerud’s patients have kept their intrusive thoughts to themselves for 20 years before telling her. They might be thinking: “Why am I having these thoughts? What does it say about me? Maybe I’m a monster,” she says. A calm, compassionate response can make it easier to keep talking. “It can help them feel like there’s hope,” Jerud says. “Maybe I don’t have to beat myself up for having these thoughts in the first place.” Here’s what to say if a loved one tells you they’re having intrusive thoughts. Start by thanking your friend for trusting you. Skip responses like “How could you think that?” or “Don’t say that,” which can reinforce the shame they’re already feeling. It’s helpful, too, to make it clear that what they told you won’t affect your relationship: “This doesn’t change how I think about you,” suggests Madeline Thompson Smith, an Atlanta-based licensed professional counselor who specializes in OCD. Then acknowledge how upsetting the thoughts sound: “That sounds really scary to have stuck in your head.” Pay attention to how you sound and look, too. A warm invitation to explain more can feel like judgment if you’re staring intensely or using a sharp tone. If you look startled, acknowledge it instead of hoping they didn’t notice. Kimberly Leonte, a psychologist in Boston who specializes in OCD, suggests wording it like this: “I guess I wasn’t expecting that, but I want to hear more.” When someone you love is distressed by an intrusive thought, reassuring them can feel like the kindest thing to do. But if they keep coming back with the same question, answering it again and again can become part of the OCD cycle. Say your partner keeps asking whether an unwanted thought means they’re dangerous. You reassure them, they feel better, and you both get back to your evening. Until another question arrives: But what if you’re wrong? What if they didn’t explain the thought accurately enough? Next thing you know, they’re asking for reassurance again. A single reassuring response isn’t necessarily a problem, Thompson Smith says. The concern is when seeking reassurance becomes a compulsion—a behavior someone feels driven to repeat to relieve their distress. Each answer briefly helps them feel better, but it leaves them needing another answer when the doubt returns. Instead of debating what the thought means, acknowledge how they’re feeling and express confidence in their ability to get through the discomfort. Thompson Smith suggests: “I know this feels really uncomfortable right now. I’m not going to help you go down the rabbit hole, but I’m happy to sit with you while it’s hard.” If reassurance has become a routine between you, talk about changing it together, ideally with guidance from their therapist. Abruptly refusing to answer can leave your loved one wondering what happened. “It’s tough if the person’s used to getting reassurance, and you just stop giving it and they don’t know why,” Leonte says. Instead, discuss how you’ll respond the next time they ask—and make sure you both understand why you’re trying something different. Meanwhile, aim to avoid suggestions like “Just stop thinking about it” or “Replace it with a happy thought.” Besides being considerably easier said than done, trying to force an intrusive thought away can keep someone preoccupied with whether it’s gone yet. Find out what your loved one wants from the conversation. They might be seeking support in taking a step toward getting mental-health help, or they may simply want you to know what they’ve been going through. “Do you want me to just listen, or do you want help figuring out what to do next?” Thompson Smith suggests asking. If this is the first time they’ve said their intrusive thoughts out loud, they might not be ready to discuss next steps. If they’re already in therapy, ask how you can support what they’re learning: “What would your therapist recommend trying right now?” Leonte suggests asking. If they’re interested in learning more about intrusive thoughts but feel overwhelmed, you could offer to read about them together. Sometimes, they simply want someone to listen. Your loved one might be telling you because “they just don’t want to be alone in this, and you can help them hold it,” Thompson Smith says. Or they may need to know “you’re not gonna judge me or people aren’t gonna run away if I start trying to be open and get help.” If unwanted thoughts are causing significant distress or interfering with daily life, offer to help your loved one find a therapist. If you react in a way you regret, apologize and let them know you’re still there for them. “No one’s perfect,” she says. “The goal isn’t to be perfect, it’s just to be supportive.”

First, a Saturn 5 rocket lifted off from the Kennedy Space Center (KSC) in Florida carrying three astronauts, an Apollo spacecraft, and a lunar module. Then they got to orbit and the astronauts flew both ships. Then they came home. That straightforward mission was accomplished 57 years ago, during the flight of Apollo 9. The next time NASA attempts something of the kind—during the planned flight of Artemis III in the summer of 2027—things will be a lot more complicated. First an uncrewed Blue Origin New Glenn rocket will launch from KSC carrying the company’s Blue Moon lander into orbit. Then NASA will launch its prosaically named Space Launch System (SLS) moon rocket, carrying the four Artemis III astronauts aboard an Orion spacecraft. Then the crew will rendezvous with the Blue Moon, dock with it and enter it, checking out its systems before returning to the Orion. Then SpaceX will launch a Starship rocket carrying its own Starship lander, which will also be uncrewed. The astronauts will rendezvous and dock with that ship too, but not enter it since it will not yet be equipped with a life support system. Then they will separate and the crew will spend a few more days running tests on the Orion. All of that work will take up to 17 days from first liftoff to splashdown. And only if everything goes just right with that mission can NASA achieve its goal of landing astronauts on the lunar surface during the 2028 flight of Artemis IV—and in the process, win a new space race with China. On Oct. 23, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman—along with Associate Administrator Amit Kshatriya and Artemis Program Manager Jeremy Parsons—conducted a marathon two-hour press conference at KSC, detailing plans for Artemis III and IV, conceding that the hurdles are considerable, but insisting the goals are within reach. The current NASA, with its tight funding and long timelines, may not be the old NASA that used its generous budgets and garage full of rockets to pop off nine crewed Apollo lunar missions just from 1968 to 1972, but it will be no less successful. “In that remarkably short span of time, the ambitions of generations once confined to the pages of science fiction became humanity's greatest achievement, and for the first time in billions of years, life born on Earth would set foot on another world,” said Isaacman. “Now, in this incredibly demanding and dangerous business of putting astronauts [back] on the moon, NASA must own the near impossible…That is how we give Artemis IV the greatest possible chance of safely and successfully returning Americans to the lunar surface in 2028.” The extreme complexity of Artemis III is owing in part to a pair of decisions NASA made in 2021 and 2023 and in part to the simple limits of known rocket technology. If the engineering physics were perfect—and engineering physics are never perfect—a lunar crew could use just one spacecraft to get to the moon, land, blast back off, and return home. But a spacecraft that could do all of those jobs and remain robust enough to reenter the Earth’s atmosphere would simply be too heavy for a lunar landing and subsequent liftoff. Instead, as in the Apollo days, the job will be divided between two ships—one lightweight one to perform the landing and liftoff and one orbiting mothership to bring the crew home when the lunar exploration is done. It was a simple ship with a low center of gravity, ensuring a stable foothold on the lunar surface. Starship is a silo-like cylinder with a tapered nose cone, measuring 165 ft. tall, calling to mind less an actual spacecraft than the 1953 illustrated book about Tintin’s adventures on the moon. Apollo astronauts used a nine-rung ladder to get down to the lunar surface. Artemis astronauts aboard Starship will use an elevator. Living digital twins are helping industrial plants run more like living systems, and setting the stage for industries that work in sync. Most people associate industrial environments with cold steel and clockwork machinery. Functionally, though, a plant or factory is more like a living thing: It has a pulse, even a metabolism. It goes through wear and tear as it ages, and signs of deteriorating health often show up as barely perceptible irregularities. Digital twin technology has long aimed to mirror that dynamism and model anomalies that may be symptomatic of larger problems. But earlier implementations were a few beats behind physical reality; they were incapable of real-time analysis and unable to proactively address any issues detected. They focused more on simulation, observation and what-if scenario planning, rather than closed-loop execution. Rather than serving as a digital mirror of reality that can spot anomalies or suggest optimizations, a living digital twin combines a dynamic, high-fidelity digital representation of complex operations with the capacity to intervene in the physical world through operations and control systems. AI is at the center of this change. Rob McGreevy, chief product officer at AVEVA, likens newer, AI-infused digital twins to organisms that grow and evolve. These “living” versions are adaptive, evergreen models of a plant’s assets and processes. Because they keep pace with live conditions and connect systems that once ran in isolation, they provide a previously missing layer of visibility and orchestration that better reflects the organic rhythms of the facility itself. Now, agentic AI is also enabling industrial systems to move beyond diagnostics or suggestions and act of their own accord. As the technology progresses toward greater autonomy, it’s starting to develop the reflexes for a kind of industrial homeostasis, in which small, constant adjustments keep a plant or factory healthy and humming. All that said, human judgment and operator oversight still play an instrumental role by defining what healthy looks like to begin with—and flagging when AI systems are falling out of alignment. Living digital twins provide the means for human engineers to monitor what AI is doing and ensure execution is on track. Living digital twins link engineering and design information with real-time operational data through a digital thread, or a connected record of an asset that follows it through its lifecycle. A couple of notable technological shifts have helped string this thread together: Sensors (which have gotten more affordable in recent years) now stream telemetry from IoT devices and industrial control equipment, and software platforms bring that information into user-friendly interfaces. Predictive and physics-based models can also provide AI-enriched insights into how a machine or process might behave. The lenses through which operators view digital twins have evolved in turn. “You can look at the digital twin through the lens of sustainability: What are my emissions?” says McGreevy. “Or, through the lens of maintenance: Is this asset performing in a healthy way? Transformers are a crucial but nearly invisible part of our grid. The devices help efficiently move power across long distances, but they’re complicated and in short supply. And given that their basic design hasn’t changed much for about a century, they could use an upgrade. As the world races to meet rising electricity demand from EVs, data centers, and more, researchers and companies are looking to modernize transformers. New, solid-state versions could help relieve some of the supply concerns and make sure the grid can handle the growing pressure. Conventional transformers generally rely on huge amounts of specialized steel alloys and miles of copper wire to do their work of transferring electricity between circuits. Solid-state models, in comparison, combine magnetic components with high-power semiconductors, making them more compact and allowing for more control over the flow of electricity. These devices are still in development, and can’t replace all traditional transformers today, especially the largest ones that work at the high voltages necessary for long-distance transmission. But a new generation of transformer technology could be a crucial part of our future grid. Moving electricity through the grid isn’t perfectly efficient: As electricity flows through power lines, some gets lost as heat. How much varies—the higher the voltage, the lower the current, and the less electricity gets lost. So a key function for the grid is stepping voltage up and down. Consider, for example, your local solar plant. For that electricity to efficiently travel to your home hundreds of miles away, it needs to reach a higher voltage than what’s generated by the solar plant. Enter transformers. In its path from the power plant to your outlets, electricity might go through one transformer at the solar farm and another at a substation nearby, stepping up the voltage twice to reach a high enough level to travel on high-voltage transmission lines. Solar panels also produce direct current (DC) electricity, which needs to be converted to alternating current (AC) via an inverter before it flows into a transformer. Closer to its destination, the voltage will be stepped down again, probably at least twice: once at another substation and again at a smaller transformer in your neighborhood. If you’re in the US, electricity from a solar plant might be converted to AC by an inverter at around 480 volts, get stepped up to 115,000 volts for the transmission lines, and finally get stepped back down to 120 volts and 240 volts for your home. Conventional transformers do this work using what essentially amount to loops of wire. The core of a transformer is typically made of a special type of steel called grain-oriented electrical steel. Coils of copper wire sit on either end of that core. The number of loops in each coil determines the voltage on either end of the transformer: If there are more loops on the secondary coil than the primary, the voltage steps up, and if there are fewer, it steps down. When incoming electricity passes through loops on the upstream end of the transformer, that forms a magnetic field in the core. That field induces a voltage in the loops on the other side of the device, which is what allows the energy to continue flowing at a new voltage. This process is called magnetic induction. Transformers form the backbone of electricity transmission and distribution, says Srdjan Lukic, a professor of electrical and computer engineering at North Carolina State University. In addition to doing the job of stepping up and down voltage, transformers also often provide what’s called galvanic isolation, meaning there’s no direct electrical path flowing through them. That can help limit disturbances from moving through the grid, improving safety. The problem is that today’s transformers are big and bulky, and they’re also difficult and expensive to replace, Lukic says. Solid-state transformers essentially add so-called power electronics—semiconductors that convert and control electricity to the typical blueprint of today’s transformers. While a conventional transformer operates at the frequency of the part of the grid it’s connected to, a solid-state transformer actually controls the frequency of the current. Typically, a solid-state transformer first converts incoming AC power from the grid to DC power, and then to a higher-frequency AC power using switches made of a semiconductor like silicon carbide. From there, a magnetic component changes the voltage. Because the advanced semiconductors allow transformers to exert so much control over the frequency and stability of the current, the magnetic core can be much smaller than in a conventional transformer. Some come in at roughly one-tenth the size but have the same capacity, Lukic says. Finally, additional power electronics convert the electricity from high-frequency AC to its required form—generally back to DC power and finally to lower-frequency AC. Today, stepping voltage up and down is many transformers’ only job. But solid-state transformers can do much more. They absorb the jobs of devices like rectifiers and inverters (which convert AC to DC power and vice versa), shrinking the total footprint of the electrical equipment and potentially cutting the amount of power lost in the system. In August, the New York Power Authority and the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) tested a one-megawatt solid-state transformer under realistic utility conditions at EPRI’s test site. The device was built in Lukic’s lab, and it was the first such test for a transformer of that capacity, he says. The prototype was roughly 98% efficient—meaning it lost only 2% of the energy fed in. An equivalent conventional transformer and rectifier, by comparison, would be about 96% efficient together, Lukic says. A difference of 2% might seem small, but it is significant, since it means the solid-state transformer could cut power losses from conventional equipment in half, Lukic says. One major goal of the project was to help utilities gain more confidence in these new components, Lukic says. That’s crucial, since these devices could be used on the power grid in the future. In the near term, solid-state transformers could be used within data centers to provide electricity for server racks. The prototype is still a relatively low-voltage system, operating at about 15,000 volts. Industrial facilities like larger data centers often require higher-voltage connections, and transmission lines typically operate at hundreds of thousands of volts. Adapting solid-state transformers to these higher voltages will require further engineering development. But the test was a successful demonstration that solid-state transformers can work under real conditions, Lukic says. Companies developing solid-state transformers are largely focused on data centers right now, Lukic says. There’s an influx of money to support developing the new technology, and the voltage requirements aren’t as high as they will be for larger transformers that will be needed for long-distance transmission. In February, Heron Power raised $140 million to commercialize its solid-state transformer, which the company calls the Heron Link. The technology could be used in data centers, solar power arrays, and grid batteries, says Drew Baglino, the founder and CEO. The company is starting to test its technology at larger scales now, Baglino says. Ten megawatt-hours’ worth of batteries at Heron’s headquarters in Scotts Valley, California, will soon undergo testing with Heron transformers. The company is also planning a pilot with a nearby solar farm, Baglino says. One challenge for solid-state transformers is that their power electronics have more failure modes than conventional transformers. In a traditional device, a failure means that the entire device will typically be taken offline, resulting in power interruptions. But Heron designs with that in mind, Baglino says: The company uses a modular design, with many small devices that work together. If one fails, the rest of the system keeps working until the affected bit can be replaced, which doesn’t require shutting the whole thing down. Heron Power is currently building a $100 million factory in California to make advanced transformers. The facility should begin production in 2027 and reach its full production rate of 40 gigawatts’ worth of transformers per year in 2029. In recent years, companies that make grid equipment have put $1.7 billion into the US supply chain for power transformers, which are used in high-voltage transmission, says Benjamin Boucher, a supply chain analyst at Wood Mackenzie. There’s been an additional $1.3 billion invested for distribution transformers, the smaller devices that help transform power for homes and businesses, he adds. But even this investment isn’t keeping pace with the rapid growth of the grid. Wait times for large conventional transformers—the kind needed to connect new power plants, long-distance transmission, and high-demand sites like data centers—are stretching as long as five years. “Transformers have become one of the biggest bottlenecks for utilities, generation developers, and even data centers,” Boucher says. The big challenge is that it’s difficult to get new transformer manufacturing up and running, because it’s a quite technical process that requires a lot of specialized labor. So even facilities announced years ago are still years from opening, Boucher says. Solid-state transformers could help ease some of that pressure. But high-voltage devices needed for power plant connections and transmission lines are the holy grail for solid-state transformers, Lukic says. Existing solid-state technology can’t be used for those applications yet, so the industry will need to develop new materials and designs to support high voltages and handle electricity safely in a compact device. “We’re facing a big risk related to grid infrastructure,” Boucher says. “Without the transformers, you don’t really have an electrical grid.” Huge grid-scale batteries are thriving, but smaller residential systems have lagged. CXMT’s booth at the IC China semiconductor expo in November 2025 China’s biggest memory chipmaker, CXMT, plans to start making HBM3E, an advanced memory used in AI chips, even though only 20% to 30% of the chips it makes are usable. Two people familiar with the matter told Reuters, which reported the details on Wednesday, 28 October. TNW has not independently verified the report. CXMT has sent HBM3E samples to several Chinese AI chip designers, the people said. It expects to begin limited production within the next two months, one of them said. The Information first reported CXMT’s HBM3E plans; the yield figures and timeline are new from Reuters. High-bandwidth memory (HBM) stacks memory chips on top of each other so they can pass data quickly to AI processors. It is a key part of the chips that train and run large language models. Yield is the share of chips from a production line that meet specifications. CXMT’s is about 20% to 30%, the people told Reuters. SK Hynix, the market leader, gets around 70%. Low yields make production expensive, because the company must throw away or rework many chips. In testing, CXMT’s HBM3E performed closer to SK Hynix’s older HBM3 than to its HBM3E, one of the people said. CXMT has sent customers two versions: a standard design and an update meant to improve yields. Its chip will likely cost more than comparable foreign products, one of the people said. Chinese AI chipmakers cannot get enough advanced memory to raise output, the people said. The US tightened export controls on some advanced HBM products bound for China in December 2024. Chinese firms have since sought the memory through unofficial channels, where it can cost several times more, according to the people. Last month, Huawei, Cambricon, MetaX and Iluvatar CoreX raised prices for their AI chips by 20% to 50% as memory costs rose, Reuters reported earlier. Cambricon’s latest sales missed expectations as chip supply tightened. SK Hynix held 50% of global HBM revenue in the second quarter, according to Counterpoint Research. Samsung had 33% and Micron 18%. CXMT held 10% of global DRAM revenue, making it the fourth-largest supplier of conventional memory, Counterpoint said. CXMT said in September that it had caught up with Samsung and Micron in conventional memory. It also swung to a profit of nearly $20bn this year as memory prices soared. In HBM, its rivals have moved on. SK Hynix said on Wednesday that HBM4 had beaten its target yield and would pass HBM3E in shipments and revenue in the fourth quarter. A Discord Server Web Page for Dead by Daylight, one of the servers in the pilot Messages posted in Discord’s largest public communities will start appearing in Google and other search engines from mid-December. Discord announced the change in a blog post on Tuesday, 27 October. It covers more than 63,000 Discoverable Servers. It is on by default for adults in most countries, though not in Europe. Discoverable Servers are the communities that anyone can join without an invite. They need at least 1,000 members and must be at least eight weeks old. They must also meet Discord’s activity and safety rules. Their admins chose to list them in Discord’s Server Discovery. The changes leave private servers, direct messages and group chats untouched. “This has led to valuable information being locked in Discord conversations, inaccessible to the wider internet. We want to fix this,” wrote Kelsey Duncombe-Smith, a group product manager at Discord. From 16 November, every Discoverable Server gets a Server Web Page, a public page that describes the community. If admins have not made one, Discord publishes a default page. The pages may also show automated summaries of conversations in public channels. From mid-December, some messages from public channels can appear on those pages and in web search. They appear in the posters’ own words. Discord will not show usernames or avatars next to them. Discord won’t include messages sent before mid-December. It will label the channels that can appear on the web. Discord has already tested the messages with game communities including NBA 2K, Rust and Dead by Daylight. During that pilot, all the published posts came from admins and moderators. Users can turn their messages off in User Settings, under Data and Privacy. One switch covers every Discoverable Server they are in. Messages sent while the setting was on stay on the web unless the user deletes them. Users cannot opt out of the channel summaries, which only admins can switch off. Admins can choose which channels take part, or opt them all out, in Server Settings under Web Presence. If they change nothing, public channels stay in. A server that does not want a web page at all has to stop being Discoverable. The message setting is off by default in the EU, the UK, Canada, Brazil, Australia and South Korea. Discord put that down to “legal restrictions”. Users there have to opt in. Discord excludes teen accounts entirely. Discord sorts users by age with checks it rolled out last month. The plan drew criticism from users over privacy, as Lauren Forristal reported for TechCrunch. Some argued that posting in a public server does not mean they want their messages found across the internet. Others asked whether AI companies could train models on the messages. Reddit users have raised the same worry about AI scraping, TechCrunch noted. Discord says the goal is to bring back what forums offered: answers anyone can find. Amazon Leo is buying satellite operator Globalstar The US Federal Communications Commission has approved Amazon Leo’s $11.5bn purchase of Globalstar. Globalstar runs the satellite service behind Apple’s iPhone emergency features. On Tuesday, 27 October, the FCC’s Space Bureau cleared the transfer of about 25 megahertz of Globalstar’s mobile-satellite spectrum. The regulator announced it in a press release. That approval ends the FCC’s review of the deal. Amazon agreed to buy Globalstar in April. The deal gives Amazon Leo Globalstar’s two dozen satellites and its spectrum, as David Shepardson reported for Reuters. “By approving this deal, the FCC has empowered Amazon Leo to deliver fast, next-gen satellite broadband services, increase competition, and help communities stay connected all across the country,” said FCC Chair Brendan Carr. The spectrum is the core of the deal for Amazon’s direct-to-device plans. Direct-to-device service lets phones and other gadgets connect straight to satellites, without a mobile mast. Amazon plans to use the spectrum to add capacity and reach places that ground networks don’t cover, the FCC said. The regulator found the deal is likely to benefit the public. It said the deal would improve coverage in unserved and underserved areas and strengthen emergency communications. It found no material harm to the public interest. Last week, the FCC also let Globalstar serve the US with its C-3 System. That is a planned network of 48 satellites. Amazon Leo may build and launch that system once the deal closes, the FCC said. In July, Amazon also asked the FCC to approve up to 5,105 direct-to-device satellites. Globalstar powers Emergency SOS and Find My via satellite on iPhone and Apple Watch. Amazon has signed an agreement with Apple to keep providing those services, Reuters reported. Apple invested about $1.5bn in Globalstar in 2024, which gave it a 20% stake. Apple made up about 63% of Globalstar’s revenue last year. Amazon plans to buy Apple’s stake, the FCC said, according to Reuters. The approval “brings us a step closer to bringing new connectivity capabilities to customers,” Amazon said in a statement quoted by Reuters. Amazon Leo has launched nearly 400 satellites since April 2025, Reuters reported. It plans thousands in low Earth orbit by 2029. SpaceX’s Starlink has more than 11,000. Amazon has committed more than $10bn to Leo. It plans to start commercial service by the end of 2026, the FCC said, according to Reuters. The company said this month that it had built its thousandth satellite. The FCC has already approved a 15,000-satellite direct-to-device network for SpaceX. It also cleared a 248-satellite system for AST SpaceMobile. On Thursday, 29 October, it votes on a proposed auction of more spectrum for direct-to-device services.
