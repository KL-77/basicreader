NASA updates lander plans for Moon landings, finally solves long-festering Orion issue

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-23

Visit Original Website
During a lengthy news conference on Friday, NASA provided a detailed overview of how it plans to return astronauts to the lunar surface as soon as 2028 as part of its Artemis Program. Although much of the information behind these plans has been previously reported at Ars Technica and elsewhere, this marked the first time NASA has laid them out in detail and acknowledged some substantial changes in the architecture. Most notably, for SpaceX’s Starship vehicle, the revised plan calls for the Orion spacecraft to dock with the Starship lunar lander in high-Earth orbit before transiting to the Moon. And for Blue Origin, the mission architecture includes four launches of the New Glenn rocket, but no spacecraft-to-spacecraft refuelings. Additionally, the Blue Origin plans allows for the possibility of its Blue Moon lander rendezvousing with Orion in low-Lunar orbit. During the presentation on Friday, NASA officials said they continue to anticipate launching the Artemis III mission—a demonstration involving docking with both Blue Moon and Starship near Earth—by “summer” 2027. And they are still tracking toward a 2028 launch of Artemis IV, which would involve an actual landing on the lunar surface. “This is an achievable path we are on right now,” NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said. “We have the mandate, we have the resources, and the support from our stakeholders to be able to execute, and that’s what we intend to do.” It is commendable that Isaacman and his leadership have taken a hard look every element of the Artemis Program and sought to make changes where they can help the lunar lander companies execute and accelerate their plans. But can they hold to those dates? The biggest ask SpaceX engineers had in meetings with NASA in early 2026 was removing the requirement to dock with Orion in a near-rectilinear halo orbit around the Moon. This required more energy and had other difficulties associated with it. When NASA canceled the Lunar Gateway earlier this year, it solved this problem for SpaceX. Now SpaceX will fuel a Starship lunar lander in low-Earth orbit before docking with Orion in high-Earth orbit. The crew will fly to the Moon inside Orion before two of four astronauts transfer into Starship for a landing on the Moon. A key question surrounding SpaceX’s architecture is the number of refuelings required to top off a Starship for a lunar mission. On Thursday a NASA Safety Panel reported that the current number of tanker launches is “about 22.” SpaceX founder Elon Musk said that estimate was wrong, and that the actual number will be six to eight. During a question-and-answer session on Friday afternoon, NASA officials declined to put a precise number on the refueling launches required. A lot of uncertainty remains in the lift capacity of Starship, as well as in how much cryogenic methane and liquid oxygen will boil off in orbit and how efficient the process of transferring propellants from one ship to another will be. However, NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said this of the estimate of 6 to 8 launches proffered by Musk: it was possible, but represented the “ultimate potential end state” of Starship. In response to NASA’s call for a faster approach to reaching the Moon, Blue Origin has modified its plans such that it would no longer require refueling between vehicles for initial missions. Under the concept of operations released Friday, a New Glenn rocket (this could be an expendable version of the smaller 7×2 variant of New Glenn, or a reusable 9×4 rocket) would launch three “transfer stages” to low-Earth orbit. These transfer stages are modeled after the Blue Moon Mark 1 lander, but have some significant modifications. Then New Glenn would launch the Blue Moon Mark 2 lander into orbit, where it would rendezvous with the transfer stages that will push the stack to the Moon. This is not new information, as Ars first reported this architecture in March. However, NASA did reveal a potentially significant change on Friday. The chart shared by NASA showed Orion docking with the Blue Moon lander in an elliptical lunar orbit, approximately 8,500 km above the Moon’s surface. However NASA is also working to have the option of docking with Orion in low-Lunar orbit. According to Jeremy Parsons, the manager of the Artemis Program for NASA, such an orbit a few hundred km above the Moon would significantly reduce the propellant needed by Blue Moon to bring astronauts down to the lunar surface and back up. This mass could be used elsewhere on the vehicle. The lower orbit would also offer more, and faster opportunities for astronauts to get off the lunar surface if needed. In short, it makes some aspects of a lunar landing significantly easier for Blue Origin. As part of this, NASA announced Friday that it is working with United Launch Alliance to develop an “extended” version of the Centaur V upper stage. In concert with the core stage of the Space Launch System, this upper stage would allow Orion to reach low-lunar orbit. This is a big deal. It has long been a point of embarrassment for NASA that Orion, with its low-performing Service Module, does not have the delta-V capability to launch from Earth, reach low-lunar orbit, and return to Earth for splashdown in the Pacific Ocean. But no one had seemed prepared to fix this problem before. “Getting Orion to low-Lunar orbit means solving propulsion and spacecraft integration problems that were left unaddressed for far too long,” Isaacman said Friday. Instead, NASA created workarounds like the Lunar Gateway, which was put in a near-rectilinear halo orbit that Orion could safely reach. The burden of performance to reach the lunar surface was pushed on to the lunar landers and the companies developing them. Given the urgency of the situation, NASA has been trying to help lunar lander companies in any way they can, Isaacman said. “We’re listening to the data, and looking at what’s happening around us,” he said. “What progress are our landing partners making? Where are they struggling? What can we do to be helpful? Their lives get easier if we can get Orion closer to the Moon, substantially. We could choose to continue on our track and say, maybe they’ll show up and meet our timelines. Or we can say well let’s start turning those knobs to help.”

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth issued a memo warning military personnel against using nonpublic information to make bets on prediction markets, saying the wagers could give important clues to enemy countries. “Do Not Compromise Military Operations for Personal Financial Gain,” said the subject line of an October 20 memo published yesterday by CBS News. Hegseth’s memo was sent to senior Pentagon leadership, Combatant Command leaders, and Defense Department field activity directors. Our enemies watch movements in these markets. When an American Service member, intelligence professional, or other Department civilian engages in a financial transaction on a military outcome, they create a signal. That signal can be aggregated, correlated, and exploited by hostile foreign intelligence services to anticipate our movements, targets, and timing. The lives of our Service members often depend on the enemy not knowing what is coming. The men and women who carry our flag into harm’s way deserve a Force that protects them—not one that exposes them to the enemy for personal gain. In April, a US Army soldier was arrested and charged with using confidential information to make $410,000 in Polymarket bets on the timing of the military’s capture of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro. An August report by the Anti-Corruption Data Collective said the group found 152 suspicious wallets that placed longshot bets on military and defense markets and won a combined $8 million. Hegseth’s memo said the prohibition applies to “all financial platforms,” including traditional betting services and prediction markets such as Polymarket and Kalshi. Defense Department personnel may not make any financial transaction informed by nonpublic information, regardless of whether that information is classified or unclassified, Hegseth wrote. “All Department of War personnel are prohibited from engaging in financial transactions using nonpublic information, either directly or indirectly through a third party… Violations may result in administrative, disciplinary, and, where appropriate, criminal action under the Uniform Code of Military Justice and/or applicable federal law,” Hegseth wrote. The memo said betting on military matters related to a person’s job can warp one’s judgment. “Beyond operational security, financial stakes can warp judgment,” he wrote. “A logistician with money riding on a strike date has an interest in that date. An analyst with a position on an outcome has an interest in that outcome.” Kalshi and Polymarket both prohibit insider trading. But criminal charges of the sort described by Hegseth are sure to be a bigger deterrent than being banned from the Kalshi or Polymarket platforms. This was not the first warning issued internally by the Trump administration, The Associated Press wrote yesterday. “A group of new accounts on Polymarket made highly specific, well-timed bets on whether the US and Iran would reach a ceasefire just before President Donald Trump announced it in April, resulting in hundreds of thousands of dollars in profits for the new customers,” the AP wrote. “The White House warned staff against using private information to trade on prediction markets the same day the report came out.” In another incident, a Trump teleprompter aide reportedly made $100,000 on Kalshi by betting on what Trump would say in prepared remarks. Prediction markets have flourished under the Trump administration’s light-touch approach to regulation. The Trump administration is also trying to stop state governments from regulating the markets, arguing that state laws are preempted by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission’s exclusive jurisdiction. Courts have reached different conclusions, and state governments want the Supreme Court to uphold their right to impose restrictions on prediction-market gambling.

Updated Google privacy policy says your Gmail might be used for advertising

Using Google’s products and services has always been fundamentally precarious—doing so gives the world’s largest advertising company a lot of juicy personal data. Google has long said it won’t use the sensitive data from products like Gmail, Drive, and Photos for ad personalization, but a change to the company’s privacy policy appears to dismantle that firewall. On October 16, Google issued an update to its privacy policy, spotted by Android Authority. The ad personalization section used to include a clear limit on data from certain apps. “We don’t show you personalized ads based on your content from Drive, Gmail, or Photos,” the document said. Now, Google has updated it with the following language. This equivocation opens the door to using data from apps like Gmail and Photos in ads when it’s shared with other Google services, and it just so happens Google has a mechanism for doing that, with Personal Intelligence. The company released Personal Intelligence in early 2026, allowing users to hand data from multiple apps to Google AI products. The idea is that the added context from your existing data will lead to better AI answers, but it’s also proving to be a privacy minefield. Personal Intelligence connects your data to the Gemini app but also to features like AI Mode search. While the Gemini app does not currently have ads, you may see sponsored content in AI search tools. Google’s changes to the privacy policy appear to acknowledge that data shared with AI search via Personal Intelligence could, therefore, contribute to ad personalization. According to Google, this clarification still doesn’t mean ads are “based directly” on your personal data. Here’s the company’s full statement: “Connecting your apps is entirely opt-in. Ads will continue to be relevant to things like your query, the context of the response and your interests. Ads will not be based directly on your Gmail inbox or Photos library, and as always, sensitive information will not be used to tailor ads.” Google’s statement is essentially a roundabout way of confirming that Workspace and Photos data may inform ads. If you interact with AI Mode while Personal Intelligence is enabled, there’s a chance that summaries and insights from your data will inform the answers you get. Because that same data is used for ad targeting, it’s accurate to say that your personal data is an element of ad targeting in AI search. The privacy policy now makes that clear. A Google spokesperson tells Ars that its privacy clarification does not indicate a change in how it handles your data with respect to its advertising partners. It’s not scanning your entire inbox to enhance your advertising profile, and it’s not giving any of this data to third parties. But if you pipe personal data into an AI search tool, the ads it shows you could be based on that information. The core of the problem is that Google wants its products and services to talk to each other. Google sees that wealth of data as a key strength of its AI efforts. Some apps like Gmail are still given protected status in Google’s data handling, but there are now more avenues for that data to get out. The more data you share across Google’s ecosystem, the greater the risk to your privacy. We’ve seen a similar exception in the way Google uses your AI interactions for model training. While Google says it doesn’t use personal data for AI training, it does use your inputs and the model outputs for that purpose. With Gemini integrated with so many services and connected to them via Personal Intelligence, those outputs may very well contain elements of your personal information. Google has said it tries to filter that content from the user activity controls, therefore keeping it out of training sets. However, it doesn’t promise it will always be successful. Google is correct that Personal Intelligence is opt-in, but the company uses various prompts and pop-ups to entice users to turn it on without fully explaining the consequences. You may have turned it on without realizing. If you want to ensure ads never use your personal data in this way, you’ll need to make sure Personal Intelligence is turned off. The easiest way is to access the Gemini settings for Personal Intelligence and disable the connected apps. These settings are also a few levels deep in your main Google account settings under Data & privacy > Personalization settings > Manage Personalized Recommendations in Search Services > Connected Content Apps. From there, you can disable connections to Workspace and Google Photos.

Leaders in the US Space Force would like to have a new spaceport up and running in four years, adding a third heavy-lift launch site to relieve pressure from bases in Florida and California that have shot rockets into orbit for nearly 70 years. The obvious problem with that ambition is 2030 is four years away, and Pentagon officials have not determined where the new spaceport should go or how to acquire land for it. Air Force Secretary Troy Meink first mentioned the service’s search for a third launch base in May. A congressionally mandated study of the Space Force’s launch infrastructure identified the need for “another site that’s capable of heavy and super heavy launch capability,” Meink said in a House Armed Services Committee hearing. The Trump administration released a space transportation policy in August that endorsed the construction of more launch and reentry sites for commercial and military missions. This week, the Space Force general responsible for procuring launch services for the military laid out an “aspirational” timeline for a new launch site. “We need this capability by 2030, but I’m also a pragmatist,” said Lt. Gen. Philip Garrant, head of Space Systems Command. “That is not an inconsequential demand to build a spaceport when you factor in orbital regimes, safety, access to commodities, the Americans that live near it. In many ways, it’s much like an airport, but even louder. So there’s a lot of consideration.” But there’s no firm plan yet for when the military might get moving on actually setting up a new launch base. “It’s going to take a strong partnership with local and state governments,” Garrant said. “As far as decision timelines, we don’t have anything yet.” There were 175 orbital launch attempts last year from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station and Kennedy Space Center in Florida and Vandenberg Space Force Base in California. Space Force officials project the country’s established spaceports will need to support 700 launches per year by 2036. A third launch site would offload some of the launch demand from limited real estate at Cape Canaveral and Vandenberg. It would also provide what Space Force leaders call “geographic resiliency” to mitigate the risk of a natural disaster or military attack knocking the Cape or Vandenberg offline. The federal government already owns a third spaceport at NASA’s Wallops Flight Facility in Virginia, used for suborbital rocket tests and small- and medium-class satellite launches. Small rockets can also launch from Pacific Spaceport Complex, owned by the state of Alaska. There are also commercial options that might be available for a new military spaceport. Ars asked Garrant if the Space Force might consider leasing property from a privately owned launch base, such as the expansive spaceport SpaceX plans to build in southern Louisiana. “We’ve discussed that we need to consider all of those opportunities: public-private partnership, new federal installation, state land, leveraging commercial land—all of those are possibilities. They all have pros and cons. We haven’t made that decision yet, but we’re not discounting any of those options.”

As reported by Music Business Worldwide, Spotify appears to be stepping up enforcement of existing policies regarding Family and Duo subscription plans by requiring users to scan a QR code in person on the plan manager’s device in order to maintain access to it. Those who fail to scan the address verification QR code within seven days will see their account demoted to the free tier and be barred from joining any other Duo or Family plan for 12 months. Spotify’s support documentation verifies the policy after users on Reddit began posting reports of receiving the QR prompt. The enforcement stems from Spotify’s requirement that family plan members reside at the same address—a seemingly reasonable policy in concept. But what if you have a child away in college, a spouse who works on the road, or any number of other circumstances that might see families separated by physical distance? The policy could extend beyond friend groups looking to save a buck, affecting actual families who thought they were using the program as intended. Goldman Sachs analysts, in their October 15 Music in the Air: 10 Years On report, made available by HITS Daily Double, estimate that “a meaningful share of family plan users are not actual household members, but rather friends sharing a plan to reduce costs.” The report goes on to add, “We see additional monetization upside from tightening household verification on multi-user plans. Most family plans require members to reside at the same address, but enforcement has been minimal in practice.” If the pressure on the accounts policy from Wall Street sounds familiar, it should. Streaming services have often turned a blind eye to account sharing during their growth periods, only to walk back the leniency once viewers are hooked. Netflix tweeted in March of 2017 that “Love is sharing a password” before launching its own enforcement policies in May of 2023. Later that year, Netflix raised prices up to 17 percent, and by April of 2024, the company saw the policy pay off with 9.3 million added subscriptions and a 54 percent bump in operating income. Netflix isn’t alone in the practice, as competitors like Max (now HBO again) and Disney+/Hulu eventually followed suit with their own policy enforcement. Goldman’s report argues that while family plans are great for getting subscribers onto the platform, “the per-user monetization of these plans materially lags individual plan pricing. So if you go to load up your favorite playlist on a shared account, you might soon be getting a warning that it’s time to check in with your plan manager. If that’s physically impossible to do within seven days, you might need to look into an alternative streaming service—or start digging through drawers until you find that old iPod.

Crows can evaluate visual info twice as fast as mammals

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-23

Visit Original Website
Much of what we know about how a brain makes decisions comes from studying monkeys watching dots. In a task known as a random-dot-motion task, a cloud of flickering specks drifts weakly in one direction while the subject tries to determine whether—and in which direction— it’s moving. The task has shown that in the primate neocortex, neurons gather visual evidence over time, with their firing rates climbing until they cross a threshold, when the animal commits to a decision. But avian brains don’t have a neocortex, so it wasn’t obvious how they made similar decisions. “This was the big question,” said Andreas Nieder, a neurobiologist at the University of Tübingen. “How [would] a differently organized avian brain encode decisions?” To find out, Nieder and his colleague Philipp Schmidbauer put two carrion crows through the same task the monkeys faced, recording their brain activity along the way. The crows solved the task the same way primates do, using the same mathematical logic despite having a very different brain structure. And they did it two times faster. Nieder and Schmidbauer put two hand-raised crows in front of a touch-sensitive monitor showing a cloud of white dots on black, with some fraction of them drifting coherently left or right or up or down. The birds’ job was to report the overall direction by pecking one of the targets that flanked the display. Correct answers earned them a mealworm. “We intentionally wanted to have exactly the same random-dot motion task,” Nieder said. “The only difference was that the crows had to peck to indicate their decision, whereas monkeys were indicating by making an eye movement.” The researchers varied how many dots moved together, ranging from 81 percent of the dots coherently moving together, which is trivially easy to determine, down to 3 percent, which is barely distinguishable from noise. They also included a zero percent condition, in which there was no correct answer. Both birds scored better than chance, even when only three percent of the dots were moving. While the crows worked at the task, the team recorded from 248 neurons in the NCL (nidopallium caudolaterale)—a region at the top of the avian processing hierarchy that is widely regarded as the functional counterpart of the primate prefrontal cortex, despite being built differently. Birds and mammals parted evolutionary ways more than 360 million years ago, and bird brains evolved a different architecture. The mammalian cortex is arranged as stacked sheets of cells, while the equivalent tissue in a bird is organized into dense clusters, with no layering at all. This alternative architecture, Nieder’s study found, proved faster. Measured from the moment that the dots appeared to the moment the bird’s head started turning to peck at the button, the crows averaged 235 milliseconds. In closely matched versions of the task, monkeys took roughly 410, 490, and 600 milliseconds in three studies. The obvious question was whether the birds simply sacrificed accuracy for speed, but it turned out they did not. “This would be a simple explanation, that they are very fast at the cost of precision. But that’s not what’s happening,” Nieder said. Crows were as good as monkeys or humans in the same task. “We’ve never forced the crows to be as quick as possible,” Nieder explained. “We just gave them a self-paced decision. And even in this situation, they were twice as fast compared to any monkey or human that I’m aware of.” One possible reason for the crows’ speed was that their’ brains were working on a fixed deadline, and the data initially seemed to support that. In primates, difficulty has a large effect on how long a decision takes. In the crows, reaction times ran from 220 milliseconds at the easiest task to just 254 at the hardest, a difference of only 34 milliseconds. But Nieder doesn’t think this means an avian brain is just wired to produce answers on a timer. “If you zoom in, there is a statistical increase in the reaction time when the coherence is going down,” Nieder said. “They show the same effect as mammals. It’s just much less pronounced, because everything happens much faster.” There are no shortcuts, he explained, as the crows had to look at the direction of dots’ movement and process this information—they didn’t have any other cues. “That’s why I think they do accumulate evidence. They have to,” Nieder said. The recordings of neuronal activity show the evidence accumulation in action. Just under a quarter of the neurons sampled, 58 of 248, fired differently depending on which direction the bird would eventually choose, and these neurons started registering that decision incredibly early—about 70 milliseconds after the dots appeared and roughly 170 milliseconds before the bird’s head moved. “The neurons were accumulating sensory evidence, which is very reminiscent of what monkey neurons would do in the neocortex,” Nieder said. This kind of signal in the brain is called a decision variable, a running tally of evidence that can climb toward a commitment. “It turns out that what we find in the crow brain looks basically the same,” Nieder said. “And that’s interesting considering their brains have evolved very distinctly, as if evolution has found the same physiological mechanism twice, independently.” So far, the team can’t attribute the crows’ speed to any one particularly fast process. Nieder’s leading explanation is that nearly every biological process runs faster in birds than in mammals. “We cannot really point our finger to one specific process that is totally faster. It is probably a cascade of different processes that are faster,” Nieder explained. This cascade, he said, begins with vision, since avian visual systems are exquisitely movement-sensitive. It continues through the decision itself and extends to the motor response. Nieder argues there are likely two physiological aspects that make this possible. The first is simply size. Flight imposes strict weight limits, so birds pack enormous numbers of very small neurons into very small brains. Their axons, the long cable-like fibers that transmit electrical and chemical signals between cells, are shorter. “Long lines take time. Short lines speed everything up,” Nieder said. The second factor is temperature. Humans’ body temperature is about 36.6° Celsius, while birds go up to 41, or even 42°. This higher body temperature speeds up all chemical processes in birds’ bodies. “Every ion channel would open and close faster. Every transmitter would move through the synaptic cleft faster,” Nieder explained. “Evolution really took their bodies to the limit. This is life in the fast lane, not only in the brain, but also respiration, the cardiovascular system—everything is going toward the limit in birds.” But calling crows (or birds in general) decision-making speed demons comes with a few caveats. First, the study included only two birds, a rather small sample. “On the one hand, that’s a valid constraint,” Nieder said. “On the other hand, when you look at monkey studies, that’s the standard number of animals that we have for these very complex types of studies, combining behavior and electrophysiology.” Also, the decision-making time of 235 milliseconds in crows and above 400 milliseconds in primates is not an absolute limit. These times apply to the random dot-motion task, which inherently requires waiting to see which way things are drifting. Finally, it might be hard to learn whether the results extend beyond corvids or crows specifically. “The problem with many other birds is that they are not as smart as corvids and crows and you need a smart bird in order to train it to do exactly this task,” Nieder said. So far, he admitted, we can’t be sure whether excellent decision-making skills of crows in his lab are due to birds’ brains being speedy in general or to crows simply being smarter than other avians. “Among birds, the corvids are renowned to be extremely intelligent, so maybe there’s something special about them,” Nieder said. One of us led the Environmental Protection Agency under President Biden. The other chaired the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission under President Trump. We disagree on plenty, but not on this: the status quo is failing America. Data centers are being developed faster than the rules meant to govern them can evolve. Communities are pushing back on rising power bills, air pollution, strained water supplies and constant noise. Grid operators are warning about reliability. Governors and county boards are improvising, from moratoriums to new permitting rules, because Washington has no framework, even as the industry asks for one. Without one, the country risks stalling tools central to its competitiveness, or building them at the expense of the communities next door. Elected officials and communities in both parties are raising the alarm. A century ago, the airplane was years ahead of the rules governing it. Pilots followed rail lines and roads a few hundred feet off the ground and in low-light, they landed by bonfire. The United States invented the airplane but had no national aviation policy, and Europe was pulling ahead. In 1925, President Calvin Coolidge convened a board to study aviation for national defense. The findings were blunt: aviation was essential to the country’s defense, civil aviation served a national purpose, and the public would not trust it until the federal government set standards for the aircraft and the people flying them. Congress answered with the Air Commerce Act of 1926, which put the Commerce Department in charge of commercial flight. By the late 1930s, the country had four major airlines and well over a million air passengers a year. When aviation outgrew the Commerce Department, Congress consolidated oversight into a single independent agency in 1938 and then again in 1958 under the FAA, which moved quickly to strengthen air traffic management and safety rules. As the industry kept growing, Congress added safety, noise and emissions standards to protect the communities living beneath the flight paths. Each framework was designed to keep the country competitive and secure while protecting the public, and the industry grew up inside them. Data centers are at the bonfire stage, but we don’t have decades to get it right. In May, the North American Electric Reliability Corporation issued a Level 3 alert, its most serious, warning that sudden swings in data center demand threaten the stability of the grid. Last month, Pennsylvania’s Public Utility Commission reported that the largest grid in the country will miss its reliability standard starting next year. Governors are responding the only way the current system allows. Some, for example, are imposing moratoriums of up to a year on new hyperscale facilities, while others like Virginia removed future large data center projects from its fast-track permitting process, allowing developers to develop community benefits agreements, protect grid reliability, and meet standards on water, pollution, noise control and local hiring. The “blanket response of no” or forge ahead unbridled are more of a short term political response. The “maybe, but here’s what you have to do” is the beginning of a responsible framework. Data centers house the computing power that underpins American competitiveness, our national security and, increasingly, the everyday digital lives. They will be built, but the questions are how, where and who will benefit? Our responsibility is to set the terms. AI has quickly become a matter of national security and global competitiveness. The country that leads in artificial intelligence will help write the rules of the global economy for generations, and the U.S. will need all of it: cloud computing, the everyday AI tools Americans currently use, and the most advanced systems that can propel society to its highest levels or possibly alter it as we currently know it. Simply put, to stay economically competitive, America needs computing power that matches or beats its adversaries’. The debate over the most advanced AI systems will keep playing out in national security circles. Our focus is more immediate: the infrastructure behind the ordinary AI tools that a quarter of American adults now use daily. So we must charge forward, but not with a one size fits all approach. Facilities differ in cooling technology, load profile, footprint and demand for water. Community needs differ across the nation and they should have a say in how they participate. The fit should be a deliberate choice, not an imposition. Here is what that choice looks like done well. With a pragmatic and fair approach, data center infrastructure can operate from a combination of baseload fuels, battery storage, and renewables, while prioritizing community needs and impacts. Communities should not shoulder the costs of water infrastructure, interconnection and transmission upgrades. As a matter of fact, under the right conditions, data centers can leave the grid stronger than they found it while simultaneously providing significant co-benefits to the community, if we hold them to that standard. Industry has made this harder than it needed to be, and it’s a story we’ve seen before. After the fracking boom, some pipeline developers raced for market share and cut corners on siting, restoration and appearance. Projects that communities had originally welcomed were suddenly met with fierce opposition, and companies had to improve. Today, data centers are on the same path. In Northern Virginia, much of the noise neighbors complain about comes not from the buildings but from the gas generators running behind them. Much of these are solvable problems, if the right people are engaged to ensure that the community is involved from the onset. The appropriate parties should negotiate a community benefit agreement, transparently , that puts value back into the community and makes lasting changes that fit its unique needs, such as replacing aging water and electricity infrastructure, lowering costs for affected residents, and building a trades curriculum at the local high school. These are just examples, but residents across generations can point to exactly what the data center gave them: students graduating ready for jobs in their local facilities, and infrastructure built to sustain. We’re seeing this model take shape in Virginia, which would require local approval of any data center using more than 25 megawatts of power, regardless of prior approvals, and would ban nondisclosure agreements that keep communities in the dark. It’s also a model that has outlasted a change in party. Governor Spanberger, a Democrat, is building on the groundwork laid by her Republican predecessor, Glenn Youngkin, rather than tearing it up. Under this approach, a developer with a sound project has nothing to lose, and the community decides which projects fit best and how they should benefit residents. The right facility suits its site. A closed-loop plant that recirculates its cooling water, set on a remediated industrial parcel behind an enforceable setback and a noise standard, makes an unobtrusive neighbor. Such facilities successfully operate today, prioritizing community leadership, transparency, compensation and quiet all at once. Ultimately, it is in the best interest of the companies themselves to put people and communities first, allowing everyone to benefit from this new technological frontier. None of this enforces itself, and today no one is positioned to enforce all of it. The federal government currently regulates a snack cake more rigorously than it regulates some of the infrastructure that now underpin the economy. Data centers are permitted through a patchwork built for a different era: pieces at EPA, FERC, the Army Corps, state utility commissions and county zoning boards, with no one responsible for the whole. The patchwork isn’t working. Infrastructure on this scale needs a dedicated body that can keep pace with the technology and draw on the expertise already spread across existing agencies. To be clear, its job would be AI infrastructure, not AI itself. It would hold developers, hyperscalers, and utilities each accountable for their part and require projects to answer to the communities that host them. With one entity in charge, no community slips through the cracks. One of us is a smaller government Republican who is cautious about the call for a new bureaucratic entity to serve as the point for coordination, while the other is a Democrat who agrees but is clear eyed about the muscle required to compete globally and at home. Together, we propose putting oversight of AI and digital infrastructure, the infrastructure and the power behind it, under one roof. Data centers will not follow aviation’s path exactly, but the shape of this moment is familiar. For the security of our country, the strength of our economy, and the trust of our people, it’s time for a national guardrail with a community touch. With just days to go before November’s midterms, the Department of Justice ended this week by launching its most desperate and unprofessional assaults on the American election system yet. On Thursday, the DOJ filed an absurd lawsuit seeking to block a declaration of a winner in Alaska’s U.S. Senate race until the courts relitigate the eligibility of one of its candidates. This comes the same day as an audacious DOJ effort to preemptively push a California election case to higher courts before lower courts have even had a chance to hold a hearing. The executive branch’s continued election interference has not been deterred by going zero for 26 in cases seeking unredacted state voter lists and losing badly at the Supreme Court in a case that would have shut down mail balloting this election. From Alaska to California and who knows where else, DOJ has been filing cases to interfere with how states count and certify votes. The agency’s latest moves—as factually unsupported as they are legally vacuous—demonstrate that for all the fear about DOJ coming in with guns blazing to try to overturn the results of the elections, it is most likely to be shooting blanks. The main reason that DOJ has been losing election-related lawsuits is that the Constitution does not give the president or the executive branch a role to play in running elections. That matter is left to the states, subject to congressional legislation that can alter the rules in federal elections. So when President Donald Trump issued a March 2025 executive order soon after coming back into office that would have changed the federal voter registration form (mandated by Congress) to require it be accompanied by documentary proof of citizenship like a birth certificate, a federal court shut it down. It ruled that, “Put simply, our Constitution does not allow the President to impose unilateral changes to federal election procedures.” And when the USPS followed Trump’s second executive order by announcing a rule that would have made the agency a federal gatekeeper over state mail ballots, the Supreme Court ruled that USPS was likely to lose on the merits because its muscling into state election processes exceeds its power. DOJ’s latest moves in California and Alaska are even weaker than DOJ’s earlier attempts to assert power over elections. In California, DOJ demanded that California and Los Angeles County go through a 20,000-person list of what it termed “likely” or “suspected” noncitizens (it vacillated on what to call them), take their mail-in ballots out of the pile, and segregate them so they won’t be counted. It first provided the information on a thumb drive with the password on a Post-it note. After California officials complained that using such a drive would breach security protocols—would you put a Trump-issued thumb drive into your PC?—they provided a spreadsheet. The spreadsheet appears to be wildly inaccurate; more than half from a sample of 200 looked at by the county were people not registered, and many were U.S.-born—a great indicator they are citizens. This is no surprise. As I recently recounted in Slate: When the Department of Homeland Security made unsupported claims that states were allowing thousands of noncitizens to remain on their voting rolls, state and local election administrators demanded evidence. After Nevada got the data and investigated, the DHS list of 16,000 potentially ineligible Nevada voters fell to zero. Even weaker than DOJ’s factual claims are its legal arguments. The sole basis that DOJ relied upon in seeking this extraordinary relief from California is a federal statute that makes it a crime for a noncitizen to vote in an election. That statute gives the federal government zero right to tell California what it can or cannot do in counting ballots, and indeed, another federal statute, the National Voter Registration Act of 1993, prohibits states from systematically removing ineligible voters in the 90 days before an election. That’s to prevent the inadvertent disenfranchisement of eligible voters. Undeterred by the legal and factual weakness of the case, DOJ asked a federal district court to issue a temporary restraining order to make Los Angeles County segregate out those ballots. The district court set a hearing on the motion for this upcoming Tuesday. DOJ then “requested” that the court rule by noon on Thursday. TROs are not appealable, and the judge has not even ruled yet, so there is no order to appeal from. It does not give DOJ the power to determine ballot access or block certification of a federal election. To say these are press releases masquerading as lawsuits does not convey how weak these cases legally are. Over the past few months, an upstart political polling firm kept delivering good news for Democrats—and especially those on the left. Across more than a dozen surveys, it found progressives leading in key primary races and showed other Democrats in striking distance of winning traditionally Republican states. Except, it turns out, the surveys were fake. The deception, perpetrated by an organization called Wedgewood Polls, doubles as a cautionary tale about how people who follow politics should think about polls—not just fake polls, but also ones that are on the level. A data-journalism outlet called FiftyPlusOne contacted Wedgewood Polls this week with some troubling allegations: The firm, which claimed to have started conducting polls over the summer and didn’t list any employees on its website, appeared to have manipulated survey data to produce fake results. The details of the deception are a little in the weeds, but Wedgewood ultimately came clean. In a public statement on Tuesday, the firm described itself as a “fictional entity” that had deliberately published “fabricated numbers.” It then deleted its social media accounts and website. Yikes. But why would someone want to fake poll results? Wedgewood claims it did so as part of “an experimental project to demonstrate the vulnerabilities, lack of oversight, and overall low barrier to entry in the modern political polling industry.” Yet Wedgewood also lied to media outlets that did ask probing questions about its methods, so that explanation is hard to take at face value. Money is one potential motive. Wedgewood appears to have charged clients to conduct surveys on their behalf, a standard practice for aboveboard polling firms that could in this case amount to fraud. We still aren’t 100 percent clear on who the people behind Wedgewood are, but they may have wanted to make money by manipulating betting markets on the races they claimed to survey. Wedgewood claims its deception “was never intended to sway an election or permanently mislead the public,” but it may also have wanted to make certain Democrats look more electable—either to boost progressives or get Democrats to spend valuable resources in red states. Did Wedgewood’s surveys affect those races? Polls can influence who voters, donors, and party committees decide to support, and there’s some evidence that Wedgewood’s did. Mallory McMorrow, a Michigan Democrat whose U.S. Senate campaign flopped, says a Wedgewood survey showing her support cratering helped throttle donations and increased calls for her to drop out. “It killed us,” she wrote this week. On the other side of that coin is Francesca Hong, a democratic socialist who nearly won the Democratic primary for Wisconsin governor. Before her narrow loss, Hong referenced a since-deleted Wedgewood poll in a fundraising pitch to argue that her campaign had “momentum.” And as Politico reports, Democrats stepped up funding to Scott Colom, who’s running in deep-red Mississippi, after a Wedgewood poll last week showed him within 4 points of Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith. OK, but how much impact can one fake polling firm really have? Actually, Wedgewood’s deception is part of a pattern. A group called Median Strategies also confessed to fabricating survey data earlier this year, similarly describing itself as a “social experiment examining how purported polling information could enter and spread through the political information ecosystem.” And on Thursday, FiftyPlusOne uncovered a third political polling firm that may have cooked some of its data. That firm, SoCal Strategies, has since deleted its website and social media accounts. So does this mean political polls are just made up?? That’s going a step too far. It’s true that political polling has fallen on hard times. Americans are increasingly less likely to answer surveyors’ cold calls, making high-quality polls more expensive. Major polling misses in recent elections have also caused some universities, survey firms, and media outlets to abandon political polling entirely. But does that mean most polls are fake? Emphatically no. Legitimate pollsters strive to be transparent about who’s conducting them, who’s funding them, and the methods they use. They’re also among the people most upset about what Wedgewood did. “People like the idiots behind Wedgewood, and, to a lesser extent, Median, make it harder for the rest of us who are absolutely busting our butts to do good public opinion research,” the polling expert Natalie Jackson wrote on Substack today. But if that’s true, does this Wedgewood incident really matter? What’s the bigger picture? Unless you’re some kind of oracle, election outcomes are unknowable in advance. In that sea of uncertainty, it’s natural to grab onto any shred of data that seems to offer clues, and polls have become an outsize part of how many Americans think about politics. It’s also becoming more common for campaigns to blast out poll results—sometimes of questionable provenance—on social media or in fundraising emails. Some of that is understandable. When it comes to predicting elections, polls remain much better than person-on-the-street interviews, lawn signs, or crowd sizes at rallies. But polls have always been imperfect, their accuracy subject to reaching a representative sample, assumptions about who will actually vote, and mathematical laws of probability. None of that justifies Wedgewood’s deception, and it’s a frustrating reality that the stakes of American politics have gotten higher even as the availability of high-quality polling data has declined. But the next time you see a polling result that seems too good to be true, just remember: As the cliché goes, the only poll that really matters is on Election Day. Designing an AI serving harness built to last

The next competitive advantage in enterprise AI has less to do with which model you choose than with what you build around it. That’s an important shift. For the past several years, much of the AI conversation has centered on model capability: bigger models, better reasoning, larger context windows, higher benchmark scores. Those advances have been extraordinary. But as AI moves from experimentation to production, the question facing enterprise leaders has changed—it’s no longer, “what can the model do?”—it’s now, “Can we deliver that intelligence reliably, economically, and at scale?” Increasingly, the answer depends on the serving harness—the engineering system surrounding the model, including the architecture, memory management, scheduling, and optimization decisions that determine how efficiently model intelligence becomes a production capability. For technical executives, that changes where AI investment should go—and what they should demand from the architecture supporting it. There is no universally “best” model or AI architecture. There is only the right solution for the business problem. A customer-facing assistant optimized for fast responses has fundamentally different requirements from a document-processing pipeline that can run overnight. An internal knowledge application and a high-volume customer service platform may use the same foundation model but require very different serving architectures. The workload defines the constraints. The constraints should determine the architecture. That sounds simple, but it runs counter to the way many organizations have approached early AI deployments. During a proof of concept, model accuracy naturally dominates the discussion. Production introduces constraints that are much easier to ignore when only a handful of users are involved: latency, concurrency, memory pressure, GPU utilization, infrastructure cost, and unpredictable traffic. A model that produces marginally better answers is not necessarily the better production choice if it doubles inference costs or cannot meet the application's latency requirements. Not every workload needs a Ferrari. Sometimes the smarter engineering decision is the model that delivers exactly the performance the business needs without paying for capability it doesn't. The objective should be to deploy the right intelligence at the right cost for the workload. That makes serving architecture more than an engineering concern. It becomes an investment decision: where to spend on compute, where to optimize, and where additional model capability simply isn't worth the cost. Once AI reaches production, seemingly isolated engineering decisions begin affecting one another. Serving architecture influences memory utilization. Memory management affects concurrency. Concurrency shapes batching and scheduling, and scheduling affects GPU utilization and latency. Only then can techniques such as quantization, speculative decoding, or optimized attention mechanisms deliver their full value. This interdependence matters because organizations can spend considerably more on infrastructure without solving the constraint limiting performance. GPU memory is a good example. During inference, the model's weights remain fixed, but the key-value (KV) cache grows with active requests and context length. As concurrency increases, a system can run short of usable GPU memory even when compute capacity is still available. Adding more compute without understanding that bottleneck may simply make an inefficient system more expensive. The lesson for technology leaders is broader than memory management: Measure before you optimize. Identify the dominant constraint. Address it. Measure the result. Then determine if another bottleneck emerged. Repeat. Don't think of optimization techniques as Pokémon cards: The goal isn't to collect them all. The goal is to apply the technique that removes the highest-impact constraint while preserving the application's business requirements. For technology leaders, that demands a different governance question. Instead of asking which AI capabilities the organization can deploy, ask which constraints stand between a proven capability and sustainable business value. Perhaps the most important reason to invest in the serving harness is that today's leading model will probably not be tomorrow’s. Models will improve. Hardware will evolve. New optimization techniques will emerge. The architecture surrounding them should make those changes easier to absorb, not force the enterprise to rebuild its AI platform every time technology advances. That makes adaptability an increasingly important outcome of AI strategy. Organizations that build serving systems around workload requirements, clear service-level objectives, and measurable production economics gain the ability to evaluate new models and infrastructure against what the business actually needs. A model change can become an evaluation and configuration decision rather than an architectural reset. It also changed Marketplace rules. The pledge comes as Oracle cuts costs to fund its AI data centres. Oracle will spend $1B over the next four years on the companies that build, sell and install its software, the company said on Thursday. It made the pledge at its AI World conference in Las Vegas. The aim is to help partners cash in on demand for AI. The money will go into Oracle’s partner programme. It covers training and certification, test and demo setups, funding to move customers onto Oracle’s cloud, and help selling through Oracle Marketplace, its online store for business software. Partners include consulting firms, software makers, cloud providers and resellers. They build their own apps and AI agents on Oracle’s databases and cloud, or help customers set up and run Oracle software. In July, Oracle also put Google’s Gemini models into the tool its business-app customers use to build AI agents. “Our partners extend our sales reach, expand our delivery capacity, and bring their own innovation and investment,” said Mark Hura, president of Oracle Global Field Operations. Oracle also changed how its Marketplace works. Customers can now put more of their prepaid Oracle cloud credits toward partners’ products, and software makers can agree prices directly with buyers. Marketplace buying is also opening up in India and Japan. The pledge is small next to Oracle’s AI spending. The company spent about $50B on capital projects in its last financial year and shed about 21,000 jobs as it moved money into cloud and AI data centres. In September, it set aside $700M more for job cuts it has yet to make. In July, S&P cut Oracle’s credit rating to one notch above junk, citing the cash it is burning.
