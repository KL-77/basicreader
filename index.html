A 39-year-old unvaccinated woman in Franklin County, Pennsylvania, has become the sixth person to die of the highly infectious virus amid a state outbreak that has stricken over a thousand people since it began in April. The woman died on Saturday, October 10. She had several underlying health conditions and had recently tested positive for measles, according to an advisory posted on social media by Franklin County Coroner Jordan Conner. The advisory noted that the woman’s death meets the definition of a measles death set by the state’s health department. In an emailed statement to The Washington Post, the coroner’s office said the primary cause of death was listed as “probable acute cardiac event due to possible complications of the measles.” The previous five deaths amid the state’s measles outbreak included a newborn, a 6-week-old, an 18-year-old, a 20-year-old, and a 40-year-old. All the deceased were unvaccinated; the two infants were too young to have been vaccinated. As of the latest measles update from Pennsylvania’s health department on Friday, October 9, there have been 1,136 cases in the state since the start of the year—1,124 of which are from the current outbreak. Overall, 218 people have been hospitalized. The outbreak, which now spans 41 of the state’s 67 counties, shows no signs of slowing. There had been 157 new cases reported just in the previous week. A spokesperson for the state health department told the Post that it is “aware” of the new death and is reviewing the case. It will provide an update once the review is complete. Measles, declared eliminated in the US in 2000, is roaring back amid misinformation and slipping vaccination rates. The nationwide measles case count for the year has hit 4,080, the highest total since 1991. Pennsylvania’s outbreak is also the largest on record since that year. The US is widely expected to lose its measles elimination status when experts from the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) meet in November to review US measles data. Under anti-vaccine Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has only included two of the six deaths so far in its reporting. There were three measles deaths reported in the US in 2025. Prior to those deaths, there had not been a measles death reported in the country since 2015.

Just over 10 years after its initial release, space exploration and survival crafting game No Man’s Sky is making its way to the iPhone and iPad, Apple and developer Hello Games announced today. It will run on devices as far back as the iPhone 11 and the third-generation iPad Air. No Man’s Sky might not have the most cutting-edge AAA graphics (though they’re still strong, and they’ve come a long way since launch via updates), but the game’s uncommon method of generating galaxies of incomprehensible scale on device consistently for all players from a single-seed happening on a phone is remarkable for those of us who remember phones performing far behind consoles or desktop PCs. This version of the game includes all of the content and systems updates to the game that have been added over the years—the expansions that famously took the game from a rocky launch reception to a widely beloved redemption story. There are a couple of compromises, though, and it’s similar to what we saw with No Man’s Sky on the Nintendo Switch. Hello Games says the ability to build and explore player-made planetary settlements is limited to the iPhone 15 Pro and later, and multiplayer is not supported on any iOS device. “We are excited to see player feedback on iOS, and will support it appropriately based on how many folks are playing,” the company says, leaving the door open to perhaps not supporting all future content updates if the player base isn’t there to justify it. In addition to touch controls (which actually ought to be a good fit for a menu-heavy game like this), the iPhone version supports game controllers and casting to a TV, while the iPad version supports both of those things, plus mouse and keyboard input. Hello Games has been working on the iOS version for three years. It also recently released a well-received macOS port of the game. No Man’s Sky’s iOS port is part of what Apple is billing “indie games week,” which includes a few other titles of note, like the cat exploration game Little Kitty, Big City, and strategy game 9 Kings. While the other indie titles in Apple’s push have already started rolling out, No Man’s Sky will be a bit still: It will launch on October 28. It will also offer a free trial, and the final purchase price will be $19.99. The file size clocks in at under 5GB; remember, those millions of planets aren’t assets stored in a folder. Instead, they’re generated on-device as you go.

Over the weekend, the World Health Organization released a disease outbreak report on the events surrounding the death of a Russian infectious disease researcher earlier this month, highlighting that Russia has yet to report a confirmed cause of death. With that “key uncertainty,” the United Nations health agency said it remains impossible to assess the risk of the event or whether the public health measures Russia took were adequate to prevent spread of the unknown disease. The WHO reported that, so far, it has seen no evidence of ongoing transmission of the mystery disease. “However, confidence in the assessment remains limited by the absence of a confirmed etiological diagnosis, and the remaining uncertainty regarding the source and mode of exposure,” the agency wrote in its report. The agency laid out the order of events as information trickled out from Russian authorities, noting that it only became aware of the death via media reports on October 2. The WHO subsequently made contact with Russian authorities and requested information on October 3, 6, 7, and 8. Early media reports indicated that the lab worker had become ill on September 29, possibly following an incident at the lab, the Irkutsk Antiplague Research Institute (APRI) of Siberia and the Far East. Some early reports suggested that the researcher—who was identified by media as 28-year-old Darya Shipilova—died of the plague after breaking a test tube. On October 2, the Irkutsk branch of Russian health agency Rospotrebnadzor (RPN) said in a statement that a “special sanitary-epidemiological expert commission” had already met over a suspected case of a high-threat pathogen. Sanitary and epidemic control measures were put in place, including identifying, monitoring, and testing all contacts. At the time, RPN said that all contacts had tested negative for high-threat pathogens. On October 6, Russia’s National Focal Point for the International Health Regulations (NFP) first responded to the WHO’s information requests, saying that no case of plague had been recorded in Irkutsk Oblast. The NFP confirmed that an APRI lab worker had died from a severe case of pneumonia after developing symptoms compatible with an acute respiratory viral infection. The worker was tested for a number of high-threat pathogens, but all the tests had come back negative. Doctors categorized the cause of death as being from pneumonia of undetermined etiology. The NFP also reiterated information from RPN about the public health measures taken and that people in contact with the lab worker all tested negative for high-threat pathogens. The NFP added that the widest range of possible contacts were identified and tested in accordance with Russia’s public health laws. In follow-up responses, the NFP gave more details about testing and some results that weren’t concerning. It said authorities conducted 5,500 laboratory tests on contacts. They tested for a range of pathogens, including influenza, SARS-CoV-2, other respiratory infections, and bacterial infections, including plague and Streptococcus. Among the contacts, they identified two cases of COVID-19, three cases of rhinovirus, and one case of Haemophilus spp., a common bacteria group that includes those that can cause respiratory infections and pneumonia. They also found a few opportunistic infections. As of October 9, Russian authorities told WHO that they had not detected any high-threat pathogens in the laboratory’s research portfolio in any of the contacts, and they were no longer being monitored. The monitoring period was set for seven days for all the contacts. Authorities also added that the worker who died was not infected with plague bacteria, Yersinia pestis, and the worker had been vaccinated against the infection due to the occupational hazard. They added that no lab biosafety or biosecurity incident had occurred at APRI, and the deceased worker had not traveled outside the region within the two weeks before becoming ill. Reviewing all the information coming out of Russia, the WHO was largely at a loss as to what happened and what risk the event posed, concluding: “A key uncertainty for this event is the absence of a confirmed diagnosis in the deceased laboratory worker.” Without the cause of death, the agency can’t “independently assess whether the duration of the reported monitoring period for contacts was sufficient to exclude all plausible causes of the fatal illness.” They also aren’t ruling out the possibility of a laboratory-associated exposure.

When Russia began attacking Ukrainian data centers and telecommunication infrastructure, it apparently failed to prepare for possible retaliation. Now Russia’s digital services are facing widespread outages after Ukrainian drone strikes knocked out three of the largest data centers owned by Russian tech giant Yandex within several days. Pro-Kremlin bloggers are warning that Russia’s centralized digital economy could collapse because the country is not prepared for the “data center war” it started, according to posts translated by the pseudonymous independent researcher ChrisO_wiki. The Russian commentators also suggested that Ukraine’s decentralized digital services could prove more resilient than Russia’s system if the countries continue trading such blows. Russia initially launched a drone strike campaign targeting Ukrainian data centers and mobile phone networks in late September. The attacks temporarily disrupted Internet and cell phone service for up to 100,000 households in the Ukrainian capital of Kyiv and came as part of a broader long-range strike campaign that has involved Russian missiles and jet-powered drones hitting Ukraine’s power grid infrastructure, transportation hubs, and even residential apartment building blocks. But Ukraine soon began striking back at data centers owned by the Russian company Yandex, also known as “Russia’s Google,” in what Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has described as a “symmetrical response.” The first Ukrainian drone attack damaged a data center in the Sasovo region southeast of Moscow on October 8, which houses two of the three supercomputers that Yandex has used to train its YandexGPT AI model. It was not immediately clear how much damage may have occurred to the supercomputers, which are based on Nvidia A100 GPU chips. The Chervonenkis supercomputer has a computing performance rated at 29.42 petaflops and ranks at 101 on the Top 500 List of supercomputers, while the Lyapunov supercomputer is rated at 20.03 petaflops and ranks at 161. A second Ukrainian drone strike knocked out another Yandex data center in the Kaluga region southwest of Moscow on October 9. This is one of the largest data centers in Russia, with a maximum 63-megawatt capacity. A third Ukrainian drone strike then forced Yandex to suspend operations at its data center in Vladimir, east of Moscow, on October 11, according to BBC News. This facility houses the third of Yandex’s three supercomputers, Galushkin, which ranked 134 on the Top 500 list and is rated at 20.64 petaflops of computing performance. More importantly for Russian society, the damage to Yandex’s data centers has reportedly caused interruptions or outages to popular Russian payment apps and digital banking services, email, e-commerce platforms, video streaming services, ride-hailing and food delivery apps, and navigation and map services. Yandex’s search engine also accounts for about 70 percent of the market share in Russia. The disruptions are even more widespread, because the data center damage also impacts Yandex’s cloud computing service, which counts some of Russia’s largest companies as customers, according to The Moscow Times, an independent newspaper. These include large grocery and fast-food chains as well as a household appliance retailer. The Russian milblogger writing under the name Ratio Militaris warned that Yandex’s hypercentralized digital ecosystem had become a wartime weakness for Russia, in a post translated by ChrisO_wiki: Yandex and the digital ecosystem it created that had long been one of Russia’s advantages compared to even Western countries, has proven to be a weak link—including, and even primarily, due to hyper-centralization—both at the market level, where Yandex had become a powerful monopolist, and at the level of specific infrastructure solutions, where the entire power of the information giant was concentrated in a few (the exact number is no secret, but perhaps inappropriate to specify) large nodes—those aforementioned data centers. Russian companies like Yandex will also have a hard time establishing infrastructure backup systems outside of Russia to provide additional wartime resiliency. That is partly because of the “Sovereign Internet Law” passed by the Russian parliament and signed into law by President Vladimir Putin in 2019, according to Russian milblogger Artjockey, whose writing was translated by ChrisO_wiki. In its pursuit of a “sovereign Internet,” the government has effectively blocked Russian companies from using infrastructure even in friendly nations, while “unfriendly” countries have blocked access via sanctions anyway… it is virtually impossible to replicate Russian IT infrastructure in places like Belarus or Kazakhstan—though, in any case, the necessary capacity simply isn’t there. China might have the capacity, but that would require legislative changes to allow key services and registries to be moved there. By comparison, Ukraine has a much more decentralized digital ecosystem consisting of smaller data centers. It has also taken steps to build out backup infrastructure, including moving some equipment and networks underground or outside the country. Western tech giants like Microsoft and Amazon have also provided free cloud services to help Ukraine migrate crucial government services to other parts of Europe, enabling many essential Ukrainian digital services to keep going despite Russia’s sustained missile and drone attacks. Amazon Web Services alone is hosting more than 15 petabytes of data from over 60 Ukrainian government organizations, along with data from Ukraine’s largest private energy company, DTEK. Meanwhile, Russians may be waiting up to six months until Yandex can replace damaged data center equipment, according to an unnamed IT expert interviewed by Russian newspaper Kommersant. A lengthy timeline for repairs may not be so surprising when considering Amazon’s own experience after Iranian drone strikes knocked out multiple data centers in Bahrain and the United Arab Emirates earlier this year.

The Turing Trap is turning into a Talent Trap: The wrong AI strategy could strand millions of skilled workers

Fortune | FORTUNE | 2026-10-13

Visit Original Website
Byron Auguste is CEO of Opportunity@Work and served as Deputy Director of the National Economic Council 2013-15. Justin Heck is Opportunity@Work’s Senior Director of Research. Amidst the heated debate on the effect of Artificial Intelligence on our economy, our society, and even our survival lies a deeper sense of loss of control, with debates framed as “what will AI do?” This implicit passivity assumes that technology, rather than people, will determine AI’s impact on work. Before we buy into dangerous self-fulfilling prophecies, leaders in technology, business, society – and all of us – need to recognize the twin traps ahead, so we can avoid them. In 2022, Prof Eric Brynjolfsson identified and named the “Turing Trap”, the mistake of treating the goal of AI as imitating humans so machines can replace them, rather than building systems that complement and extend human capabilities. If the Turing Trap is a mistaken engineering perspective on AI, its damaging counterpart is the Talent Trap, a widely-held but deeply flawed view of workers. In Talent Trap thinking, society has a fixed amount of talent, developed mostly in schools, defined by educational attainment, and companies win by securing that limited pool of talent rather than growing it widely. It says some people are “talent” to be pursued, cultivated, and developed; others are “labor” to be plugged into a well-defined process determined by others, without the autonomy to exercise judgment. The Talent Trap worldview treats human workers as fixed realities, not as working learners. So as AI hits benchmarks on more cognitive tasks, those tasks, and eventually jobs, go away, pushing humans into more physically intensive tasks or creating new roles accessible mainly to those with new advanced degrees in emerging fields. It also places the burden of adapting workers to an AI economy mostly upon schools, universities, and formal retraining programs. The beliefs that could drive us into an AI Talent Trap are wrong. Human workers’ potential is not fixed, most learning is outside formal education, and job-relevant skills are best developed by working with other people and tools to solve customer, company, or community problems. Higher education has a vital role, but employers’ most important talent pipeline is the 10 million U.S. workers who parlay skills gained on the job into higher-paid, in-demand jobs each year, a talent pool five times more than new college graduates. Half these upwardly mobile workers are Skilled Through Alternative Routes (STARs), without college degrees, and they are concentrated in a surprisingly small set of ‘gateway’ jobs. Employers, not schools, shape this talent pipeline. At Opportunity@Work, we’ve spent years studying the skills and career pathways of STARs and other workers, with the goal of helping employers recognize talent they might otherwise overlook. Gateway jobs are around 75 occupations that account for 62% of the job-to-job changes that lead to higher pay. For example, every year, thousands of customer service representatives (a gateway job) become sales representatives or dispatchers, higher paying jobs that reward the same underlying skills: active listening, reading people quickly, staying calm under pressure, keeping a customer engaged when the stakes are high. Building skills in lower-wage origin jobs, deepening them in gateway jobs, and applying them to reach higher-paying destination jobs is how workers thrive and our talent pool expands. New research by Opportunity@Work and the Brookings Institution demonstrates that roughly half the opportunities to transition from gateway jobs into higher-paying work are highly exposed to AI’s growing capabilities, which could be used both to automate tasks and augment skills. Talent Trap thinking would treat the skills of the 18.6 million workers in the most AI-exposed gateway jobs as fixed, and focus on automating their roles. If that’s the main use of AI for customer support roles and admin assistants, the blast radius will extend to those still climbing toward those jobs, and to every employer who taps into the skills workers build doing them, like in their future sales force. The consequences of these choices for American industry, workers, and labor market will be much larger than the – also important – pathways for entry level college graduates. Instead of walking into an AI Talent Trap, what if business deployment of AI at work aimed to complement the fullest range of human talents to serve customers more deeply, in novel ways? First, the vast majority of U.S. workers would reskill for redefined roles in the context of their current jobs and in skill-based transitions between roles. Employers’ AI adoption would leverage frontline workers’ tacit knowledge and potential, with administrative assistants leading redesigns of business workflows and medical assistants engaging patients to address unhealthy behaviors. AI-augmenting hard-to-fill destination roles (e.g., in sales) to enable skills-promixate gateway workers (e.g., in customer support) would become a robust talent pipeline strategy. Fewer executives would cite AI as a reason or excuse for large layoffs. More companies would take an approach like Walmart, which announced plans to keep headcount steady even as “the skills of every single job will change due to AI,” and saw its stock rise twice as fast as the S&P index in the next 6 months. Regional industry networks would combine technology and talent to create more agile workforces, new capabilities, lines of business, and growing market shares. Core technology investments would shift. Frontier AI labs currently test and promote their latest models using technical benchmarks that measure their ability to replace humans at tasks, but could instead aim to hit benchmarks assessing the efficacy of humans and AI to accomplish goals and learn together. AI talent tools could leverage data on how workers progress in the absence of paper ceiling barriers to become a corrective to the Talent Trap, not its amplifier. New business models might even emerge, like metered augmentation, in which AI could augment missing skills, help teach those skills on-the-job, then certify a worker’s skill mastery. Given its transformative power to deploy the aggregated achievements of human intelligence for virtually any purpose we choose, “AI” should really stand for “Amplified Intention”. The goals we set for AI matter deeply enough to create self-fulfilling prophecies. If we invest trillions of dollars, intending to replace working learners with machine intelligence to solve the same problems we already solve today, we’ll eventually get that outcome. If companies avoid the false assumptions of the AI Talent Trap and invest instead in AI technology to augment and develop the fullest range of human talent and contextual knowledge to tackle unsolved problems, it could lead to a cognitive industrial revolution and workforce transformation that we can only begin to imagine. For months, the Supreme Court did not rule on the legality of electoral revisions on such a vast scale, allowing the exercise to become a fait accompli. Instead, it engaged in prolonged courtroom negotiations with the Election Commission over which documents Indian voters would have to provide to remain on the electoral rolls. There was an extended debate on whether Aadhaar, India’s national digital identity document, would be accepted. But even as the legal debates continued, exclusions from electoral rolls mounted. In the eastern Indian state of West Bengal, where Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP) had never won a state election, nine million people—around 12% percent of the electorate—found themselves excluded from the rolls. Faced with these exclusions, and still unwilling to rule on the legality of the SIR, the Supreme Court took the extraordinary step of creating an entire administrative machinery: tribunals staffed by judicial officers to hear appeals from excluded voters. When the petitioners warned that these tribunals would take years, if not decades, to resolve all the appeals, the Supreme Court judges effectively said that it was fine for excluded people to note vote in the upcoming election as long as they would eventually be put back on the rolls. The West Bengal election went ahead using the new electoral rolls and the BJP won. One indication of how flawed the SIR may have been was that around 90% of the voters in West Bengal, whose appeals were decided, were eventually restored to the rolls.  Nine out of every ten excluded voters had been wrongfully removed. Yet even as exclusions numbered in the millions in each state, the Supreme Court noted that the data did not indicate “systemic disenfranchisement.” Eventually, when the Supreme Court finally issued its judgment in the summer of 2026, it upheld the entire exercise. It affirmed the wide powers of the Election Commission to conduct the electoral revisions as it saw fit. A central argument against the exercise was that by requiring every elector, including those already on the electoral rolls, to prove their eligibility afresh, the Election Commission was treating each individual as presumptively ineligible to vote. This was a grossly disproportionate exercise of power, particularly because more proportionate methods were available to the election commission. The court rejected this argument too and, I believe, effectively immunized the commission from the requirement to act in a proportionate manner. As the legal scholar Vasudev Devadasan noted, the judgement had the effect of creating a "permanent class" of disenfranchised persons who simply cannot participate in political life. The consequences soon became apparent: the new BJP government in West Bengal threatened, on more than one occasion, to cut off welfare benefits for people excluded from the electoral rolls through the SIR. Although the Supreme Court noted that the SIR was not meant to determine citizenship, by the summer of 2026, that distinction was becoming difficult to sustain. Beyond the threat to people’s welfare entitlements, there were widely reported cases of passport renewals being denied because applicants had been excluded from the electoral rolls through the SIR. In 2020, the Indian government’s attempts to create a National Register of Citizens (NRC) had been widely criticized because of how it would force every individual to prove their citizenship to state authorities, and lead to widespread exclusion. After widespread protests across the country against it, the NRC was shelved. The SIR now appeared to be a way of introducing the NRC through the backdoor. As the SIR proceeded across multiple states—with large-scale exclusions in each—patterns began to emerge: disproportionate exclusions among vulnerable and marginalized people and of religious minorities, especially Muslims; and the use of algorithms to exclude even voters who possessed the required documents, citing "logical discrepancies" such an arbitrary age gap between parent and child, judged by arbitrary criteria that varied from state to state. Other disturbing facts also came to light. In certain neighborhoods, people had submitted pre-filled forms in bulk, seeking to have Muslims removed from off the electoral rolls by questioning their citizenship. India’s electoral law allows third parties to object to anyone’s  inclusion on the electoral rolls. In Delhi, the omission of some prominent people from the rolls provoked public outrage. The Election Commission then introduced a “VIP portal” through which their names could be restored, creating a two-tier system of class privilege. Against this backdrop, an investigative report by Ritika Chopra, a journalist with the Indian Express newspaper in the last week of September, landed like a bombshell. Her report showed that although the Election Commission’s decisions were supposed to be taken by consensus among its three commissioners, the chief election commissioner had been taking them unilaterally, often over the objections of, and sometimes without involving, the other commissioners. (The Election Commission responded by describing differing “views and observations” as part of “deliberation in any institution.”) More reports followed, documenting more unilateral decisions, opaque algorithms built by private corporations, and the increasing centralization of authority within the Election Commission to add or remove voters. It was a clear departure from India’s decentralized electoral system, which is designed to guard against decisions made in bad faith. These reports crystallized latent anger, particularly against the chief election commissioner. Under Indian electoral law, election commissioners are appointed by a committee in which the political executive holds a two-to-one majority; they are, in effect, executive appointments. The Supreme Court’s 2023 attempt to reform this system was bypassed by subsequent legislation. The court then left a challenge to that legislation on the back burner. All of this explains why the chief election commissioner became the primary focus of public anger. The story of the SIR is both an account of large-scale voter disenfranchisement in India and one of institutional crisis. Two failures converged: the failure to entrench the Election Commission’s independence in law and in the Constitution, and the failure of the Supreme Court to intervene despite evidence of disenfranchisement placed before it. Together they culminated in the furious popular protests in Delhi and the Indian government’s crackdown. It is difficult to see how this story ends. The right to vote is hard won and its loss keenly felt. History shows that once people lose confidence in the institutions meant to protect that right, restoring their trust is exceedingly difficult. A long shadow hangs over electoral integrity in India. Lifting it will require urgent, systemic reforms. Whether those reforms will come remains uncertain.

Public executions were once a staple of American life. From the beginning of the republic, they were carried out in town squares across the country. They were supposed to be moments of public education when citizens could witness justice being done and in which the condemned would deliver a “gallows speech,” acknowledging the sinfulness of the crime and repenting for what they had done. Parents and teachers would often bring children along to teach them a lesson. The events would sometimes feature a choir, a preacher giving an “execution sermon” and the condemned, who was typically a man being hanged, giving a long speech to the crowd. Occasionally executioners even achieved a kind of celebrity status. Arkansas’ George Maledon was so well-known that he was dubbed the “Prince of Hangmen.” Throughout Maledon’s career as a hangman, Arkansas newspapers regularly reported his identity and his role in executions. But by 1936 when Kentucky carried out the last public execution, all that changed. Their educative purpose had given way to a circus-like atmosphere with thousands of people assembling to witness a gruesome event, often tinged with the brutal white supremacy of the Jim Crow south. The New York Times recalls the event, which included an audience of 20,000 people, this way: Kentucky was the only state still conducting public hangings, a fate reserved for what was viewed by Kentucky’s citizens as the most heinous crime: rape. The condemned was a young black man named Rainey Bethea. He had been convicted of raping an elderly white woman, a wealthy widow who was found choked to death in her bedroom. Bethea’s execution carried with it the taint of a lynching as had previous public executions. And these executions seemed simply uncivilized, the sort of thing that decent people would not want to see. Once new execution technologies like the electric chair became the preferred execution method, putting people to death in the town square was no longer feasible. Scholars have claimed that there is something very different about the visceral experience of witnessing the state putting someone to death and the experience of watching that happen on television or a video feed. Wendy Lesser argues that such a viewing experience would be voyeuristic, allowing viewers to see from a safe distance something that is itself shameful. What Hegseth intends to do would indeed be shameful. It would be the equivalent of the government producing and distributing a snuff film, a film that captures a real killing and uses it to appeal to viewers’ sadistic impulses. Unlike lethal injection, the effects of which are often hidden behind a medicalized facade, the brutality of the firing squad is exactly what Hegseth wants Americans to see. Hasan will be seated in a chair with a hood placed over his head with a paper heart affixed to his chest. Late last month, Anthropic released a report arguing that the model GLM-5.3 poses a major cyberattack risk. The report’s core concern is that the powerful new model, released in August by the Chinese company Z.ai, is freely available for anyone to download and modify. Anthropic’s assertion is only the latest move in a long-running debate about whether frontier AI models should be made open, like GLM-5.3, or should stay locked behind the doors of the frontier labs. These days, much of the debate about how to handle open models is playing out in public—and there’s a lot of money resting on its outcome. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, who has a lot to gain if people continue buying their own hardware to run open models, advocates enthusiastically for the United States to develop more of them, whereas Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, who runs a company that could be undermined by cheap, powerful models, advocates for a more cautious approach. But beneath all these commercial incentives, there is real substance to disagreements about open models. “I think a lot of the way you come down on the openness debate is influenced by how you come down on the AI debate,” says David Gray Widder, an assistant professor at the University of Texas at Austin School of Information. Those who are concerned about AI safety warn that open models can easily be misused by bad actors, whereas those who focus more on AI’s practical potential urge that this power should not sit behind the closed doors of just a few big companies, and that open models could help spread it more widely. There are a range of ways to handle open models between banning them entirely and releasing them willy-nilly. But any approach that a government or a society might take still involves trade-offs between safety and democratization. Deciding whether to support or limit the production of open models requires understanding those risks and benefits in depth. Let’s start with the basics. A closed model, like the models that power ChatGPT and Claude, can only be accessed through a website, app, or API. An open model, on the other hand, can be directly downloaded: The details about its internal structure, encoded in a long series of numbers known as the model weights, are publicly available. Such models are sometimes called “open-weight models,” to distinguish them from models for which even more information, such as details about the training procedure, is available. Right now, closed models are more capable than open models—as of publication, the top eight models on the Artificial Analysis LLM leaderboard are all closed, though open models are only a handful of months behind in development. But there are several reasons why someone might opt to use an open model rather than a closed one. Because open models are downloadable, people can run them on their own computers or hard drives. They can even retrain them to behave differently, if they have the expertise and resources to do so. If you’re an academic researcher working to understand and improve AI systems, you almost certainly need to work with open models in your research. When working with closed models, researchers can’t do much more than send prompts and observe the responses. But researchers can observe the inner workings of open models, as well as the “chain of thought” that the models use to think through a problem before answering. They can also combine multiple open models to build new systems, compress them to make them more efficient, and fine-tune them for new applications. Businesses, too, have reasons to prefer open models. Even if you depend on an external provider to run them for you, open models still typically cost less than closed models, according to a recent report from Mozilla. And if businesses want to use AI to process sensitive data or proprietary information, running a local model on their own hardware may be the best way to keep their operations secure. But perhaps the most important quality of open models is that they challenge the tight control the frontier labs have over how most of us use AI. “The clearest, biggest argument [for open models] is around preventing mass concentration of power—it is about making sure that this foundational technology is a public good,” says Elizabeth Seger, senior policy advisor at the Tony Blair Institute for Global Change. “I think we’re at this juncture where the question of who controls the infrastructure that allows AI to operate is as big of a question as who gets to control information on the internet.” Seger sees the future of AI playing out in different ways, depending on the role that open models have. In one possible future, frontier AI systems are controlled entirely by select people, companies, or governments, and as those systems grow more powerful, those groups have increasing control over what our world looks like. The world’s most sophisticated hacking models, for example, are currently all made in the United States, so it’s entirely up to US companies and the US government whether other countries can use them to shore up their own cyber defenses. If those companies remain in the lead, we could in principle wind up in a world in which the United States is capable of hacking any other country, but no one is capable of hacking it. Frontier open models could change that balance. “If every individual had a loyal-to-them AI system that was safe [and secure], I think that would be a much less concerning world than one in which they have to just rely on a government that says, ‘Yeah, we’ve got a super intelligent AI system, it’s got this plan, it’s inscrutable to all of you, just trust us,’” says Hamish Hobbs, director of AI policy at the Centre for Long-Term Resilience. But open models are not a geopolitical panacea. Paul Azunre, who cofounded Ghana NLP, an organization that builds open-source AI tools for low-resource Ghanaian languages, notes that open models can also function as a way for companies to benefit from the labor and expertise of less-privileged nations and consolidate more power in their own hands. He identifies a pattern in which AI companies release models openly, observe how the community uses and modifies them, and then integrate those same improvements into their own products—without any credit or compensation for the people who originated those innovations. Mark Zuckerberg himself has said that the ability to integrate community improvements into Meta’s stack is a key benefit of releasing open models. “I think the ideal of open source is a very noble one, and there’s a lot of value that it brings,” Azunre says. “But the way it’s actually implemented in practice, it tends to be a very extractive approach.” The past few months have made it clear that safety remains a gnarly problem for closed models. There’s the autonomous hack that OpenAI agents executed against Hugging Face in July, but human misuse is a real threat too: According to a recent risk report from Anthropic, several extensive hacking operations have been undertaken with the assistance of Claude. So closed models are risky enough—and open models would likely be much easier to exploit. Before Anthropic passes a user’s query to one of its closed models, it uses automated systems to detect whether that query relates to hacking, biological weapons, or other risks. When it detects that users have outsmarted those filters, it can shut down their accounts. But when someone downloads a model and runs it on their own hardware, there’s no filter checking whether their prompts are safe, and there’s no way to revoke their access to the model. There are other important lines of defense. AI companies train their models to refuse harmful requests—so even if a bad prompt gets through Claude’s filters, there’s a good chance that the model will still say no. In principle, this same defense could work for open models, because it’s intrinsic to the model itself. Unfortunately, researchers have shown that it’s surprisingly easy to retrain a “safe” open model to agree to engage in the behaviors it was previously trained to avoid. Because of these facts, some people have argued that open models are simply too dangerous to release. An open model capable enough to walk someone through synthesizing a virulent new pathogen, for example, could prove catastrophic in the wrong hands. But it would be a vast oversimplification to deem open models dangerous and closed models safe. The defenses that AI companies build around their closed models are far from perfect, and people misuse those models all the time, as the Anthropic risk report makes clear. Even though open models have even fewer defenses against misuse, bad actors might still opt to use closed models because they are more accessible than open models with similar capabilities, which require lots of expensive computer chips to run locally (and even more chips if you want to retrain them). In fact, some experts who spoke with MIT Technology Review argued, by analogy to open-source software, that open models have safety advantages over closed models. Open-source tools, such as the Linux operating system, are often seen as safer than their closed-source counterparts because their vulnerabilities have been probed and patched by a vast community of online contributors. Similarly, communities of researchers can more easily test open models for security flaws, though fixing those flaws is trickier than rewriting a line of code. Open models can also be deployed more broadly than closed models for cyber defense. Any tool that can be used for hacking can also be used to prevent it: Defenders hack their own software to uncover flaws in need of patching. Anthropic has shown how this can work for closed models with its Project Glasswing initiative, which enables some trusted companies to use its most capable models for cyber defense. But plenty of other companies were left to fend for themselves. Open models, however, can be used much more widely. After it was hacked by OpenAI agents, Hugging Face emphasized the fact that it had to depend on an open model to analyze the hack, because questions about it triggered the closed models’ security filters. Nevertheless, many experts still perceive open models as riskier than closed models. The time-tested open-source security logic doesn’t necessarily hold up when dealing with tools as powerful as today’s—and tomorrow’s—models. “Where it gets complicated is when you start taking seriously the one-off instances of particularly catastrophic incidents that might be lower probability, but if they happen would be disastrous,” says Seger. If a wildly powerful open hacking model were dropped into the world today, for example, a bad actor could devastate our technological infrastructure before anyone had a chance to shore up their defenses. An event like that could be impossible to recover from. The risks can look even more dramatic when you consider what models might do of their own accord. The OpenAI Hugging Face hack only occurred because the company’s models were being tested with many of their cybersecurity guardrails removed, and open models have very few guardrails in the first place. If open models did decide to misbehave, it might be difficult to detect what was happening because they are so decentralized. For this reason, people who take seriously the purported catastrophic risks posed by rogue agents, such as Dario Amodei, tend to be more skeptical of open models. A few years ago, the debate about open models had an all-or-nothing framing: Either you supported open models because of their potential to fight concentration of power, or you rejected them because of the safety risks. These days, however, people are expending a lot of effort to find some middle ground. One way they are doing that is by devising techniques to mitigate the safety risks of open models. Most of the safety tools used for closed models, such as filters and monitoring, aren’t applicable to open models, but there are other possibilities. Some researchers are interested in trying to train models that are never exposed to training data that teaches them about bad or otherwise undesirable behavior—an open model that never learns about hacking, for example. But purging training datasets of all references to misbehavior without harming model performance remains a challenge. Other researchers are working to build models that are resistant to retraining, so that an open model’s safety training can’t be so easily removed. The theory behind these “tamper-resistant” models makes a lot of sense: If you repeatedly try to retrain a model to be unsafe and reinforce any resistance that the model shows to that retraining, eventually you’ll get a model that’s fortified against future retraining attempts. In practice, however, no current approaches to making models tamper-resistant hold up against all tampering attacks, according to a recent preprint. But Saad Hossain, an engineer at the AI medical imaging company Deep Breathe and the paper’s lead author, hopes that future techniques will prove more effective. “My reason for optimism is that it’s a very active area of research, and a lot of people are trying to come up with new and better methods,” he says. If the risks of open models are less black-and-white than they might at first seem, so are the benefits. Publishing the internal structures, or weights, of models might not be enough to disrupt AI-mediated concentration of power. “I think there are very genuine benefits to open source in terms of deconcentrating power, but they’re not sufficient in themselves,” Hobbs says. Open weights are a bare minimum standard for open models: Researchers can learn much more about how models work if they have access to details about their training, including the data on which they were trained, but AI companies are typically reluctant to divulge this information. Accessibility matters for open models too. Without computer chips that can run sophisticated models, you can’t actually use the model weights, even if you have them. Those chips have become prohibitively expensive as AI companies snap up more and more of them. “Openness is a floor, not a ceiling, for what we need to do to ensure that our technological futures benefit everyone,” Widder says. Real openness—not only open weights, but also open training data and accessible hardware—could also bring more risks, of course. If everyone has access to advanced AI hardware and advanced models to run on it, then anyone could potentially be a hacker. But there are strategies for mitigating these risks. Many experts advocate for some version of staged release, in which models start their life closed and gradually become more and more freely accessible as their safety is assured. Figuring out exactly how to implement such strategies will require striking a careful balance between the risks and benefits of open models. But if open models are a necessary element of a positive AI future, as some experts believe, that effort will be well worth it. Welcome to the second space race. You can be forgiven for experiencing déjà vu. Last time, it was the Soviet Union and the United States vying to be the first to plant some flags. Now it’s a sprint to the lunar surface between America and China—alongside a host of varyingly friendly and rival nations—plus a cornucopia of private spaceflight companies. And this time it looks like they’re getting ready to set up camp. There will be, the contenders say, bases on the moon, permanently inhabited castles under the stars. The notion of a moon base, fanciful as it might sound, isn’t new. But this year, under the leadership of Administrator Jared Isaacman, NASA has been flooding the zone with bold announcements. If all goes according to plan—a plan that will likely cost tens of billions of dollars to start—there will be drones, robotic landers, communication satellites, and a nuclear power plant; there will be plant terrariums and moonquake sensors; there will be mechanized scouts exploring craters for water. Initial astronaut missions to the lunar surface, which the US is currently planning to start in 2028, will be focused on exploration and technological experimentation. But the idea is that we will—some time in the 2030s—get to a point where there will be habitation quarters for long-term stays, surrounded by a network of scientific research stations, resource mining and processing plants, and landing pads for spaceships. “It’s going to be an expanse,” says Carlos García-Galán, NASA’s moon base program manager—one that will add up to “hundreds of square miles.” The effort is undoubtedly being fueled by nationalistic competition. China has near-identical plans to build its own base on the moon. And being first matters, because the “victor” gets to define the terms. What is the moon, exactly? Is it a militarized outpost? Is it a manufacturing hub? Is it a wilderness to be studied and preserved? But there are also other reasons to go. Earth’s companion contains primeval rocks that hold clues to the formation of the entire solar system. The lunar surface, with virtually no atmosphere in the way, is an ideal place to put telescopes that could examine the most ancient epochs of the cosmos. The far side of the moon would be “a fantastic place to explore the further universe,” says Josef Aschbacher, the European Space Agency’s director general. And it’s way more energy efficient to launch rockets from the low-gravity lunar surface than it is from Earth, meaning sending astronauts—or robotic spacecraft—farther into the solar system will become much less difficult. “The moon is critical. It will be a stepping stone for going further—that means to Mars and beyond,” says Aschbacher. But if these bold visions are to become reality, there’s a daunting amount of work to be done. A sprawling moon base must be safe for its astronaut inhabitants—protected from falling asteroids, endless cosmic radiation, razor-sharp lunar dust, and torturous temperature swings. It also must be somewhat self-sustaining, so that it can weather changing political fortunes and not cost ruinous amounts of money to maintain. For that, we’ll need to find a way to use the moon’s resources to keep the base operating—and ideally generating revenue. And we’ll need autonomous robots that can build, maintain, and expand the base even when their human masters aren’t present. “It’s almost like starting civilization from scratch,” says Simon Middleburgh, co-director of the Nuclear Futures Institute at Bangor University in Wales. Assuming we can overcome these starting challenges, decades from now—let’s say, by the year 2100—our initial moon bases could grow from barely visible structures into luminous cities, perhaps not too dissimilar from those dreamed up by authors and screenwriters the world over. “It’s like science fiction today that we really want to turn into a reality,” says García-Galán. Will anyone succeed? The moon is a tremendously hostile place to set up camp. In general, daytime temperatures can reach more than 250 °F; at night, they can plummet to less than –200 °F. Exposed to the vacuum of space, the surface is constantly showered in carcinogenic radiation and frequently hit by meteoroids. And its volcanic dust is sticky and corrosive, a destroyer of equipment and choker of lungs. Sadly, the lunar south pole—the area NASA is targeting—is even harsher. NASA wants to build a base there because its permanently shadowed craters are thought to harbor caches of water ice, which would be more precious than gold. Purify it, and you can water crops and hydrate astronauts; split it into oxygen and hydrogen, and you have breathable air and rocket propellant. But the temperature in those craters can be lower than –410 °F, making them some of the coldest places in the known universe. On the surface outside those pits, astronauts would be bathed in the scorching sun for 14 days before experiencing an equally long frigid night. But there is only so much money we can spend on safety. “What it comes down to is acceptable risk of death,” Philip Metzger, a spaceflight engineering researcher at the Florida Space Institute, said while pondering some of the dangers. “What can we reasonably survive without breaking the bank?” Temperature is one of the easiest problems to handle. Materials science has come a long way, and there are myriad metals, ceramics, plastics, compounds, and alloys that can resist wild temperature swings. Radiators keep things cool; heaters keep batteries from dying prematurely. Astronauts survive just fine too, with their fancy spacesuits—but that technology has proved itself only during brief excursions. It’s not yet clear how long this equipment could operate under such tempestuous conditions. The temperature in the moon’s south pole craters can be lower than –410 °F, making them some of the coldest places in the known universe. Outside those pits, astronauts would be bathed in the scorching sun for 14 days before experiencing an equally long frigid night. Space radiation, which can wreak havoc on electronics and trigger a cancerous division of cells, is far more pernicious. And as Stuart George, a physicist at NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston, explains, it comes in two unpleasant forms. The first is galactic cosmic rays, which rain down on the moon almost constantly. “They’re made by supernovas and neutron star mergers—all these kinds of things,” says George. And because the atomic nuclei, protons, and electrons that make them up are moving at significant fractions of the speed of light, these cosmic rays have oodles of kinetic energy, meaning they can punch through thin shielding (say, a metal wall) with ease. “They also have this habit where the heavier ions are fragmenting in that shielding and making secondary particles,” says George. That means if your shield is too thin, the astronauts behind it will be blasted with a surprisingly large dose of radiation. Then you have the sun. It may sustain life on Earth, but without our planet’s thick atmosphere and magnetic bubble to get in its way, moonwalkers will occasionally be blasted by one of its paroxysms: a solar flare (a burst of diverse types of radiation) or a coronal mass ejection (volleys of magnetized plasma). “During Apollo, they knew that if a large solar flare occurred while the crew was on the moon, there would be no way to keep them alive,” says Metzger. The crew could have developed acute radiation sickness and perished in a matter of days. The way to survive a powerful solar flare or coronal mass ejection is straightforward: “Get back inside a habitat where there’s more shielding,” says George—preferably a dedicated, highly shielded “storm shelter,” essentially a thick-walled bunker. The issue is that astronauts might be outside the base, driving a rover many miles away. Coronal mass ejections take hours to days to reach the vicinity of the Earth-moon system, but solar flares can do so in under an hour. And scientists can’t yet model the sun well enough to give much advance notice. “All of the capability to forecast solar storms is kind of like where weather forecasting [was] … in the ’50s and ’60s,” says George. As for those punchy galactic cosmic rays, the only way to avoid them would be to put a lot of stuff—lunar rocks, possibly—between the astronauts and the sky. “Build habitat modules underground,” says Chris Dreyer, director of engineering at the Center for Space Resources at the Colorado School of Mines. One option: Burrow living quarters into lava tubes, the naturally hollowed-­out volcanic tunnels created by molten rock eons ago. At this point, NASA hasn’t mentioned any plans to make its moon base subterranean, but it may need to if it wants to end up with a small lunar city. Radiation limits the time astronauts can spend on the lunar surface over their entire life—it can total around two years max, experts estimate, before the health risk becomes too high. Unfortunately, that’s not all space will throw at them. When the Artemis II astronauts swung around the far side of the moon earlier this year, they saw curious flashes light up the silvery land below. These were the explosive deaths of small meteoroids punching into the lunar surface. Earth is naturally protected from smaller space rocks, up to around 50 feet in diameter: They burn up inconsequentially in the atmosphere. “You don’t have that option on the moon,” says Philip Lubin, a physicist and planetary defense researcher at the University of California, Santa Barbara. Since smaller rocks plunge toward planets and moons far more frequently than larger ones do, this means the moon is often peppered by what are effectively tiny space bullets. They could easily puncture a spacesuit or the engine of a moon rover. How do you protect astronauts from the onslaught? “The simplest answer is burial,” says Nicholas Schmerr, a planetary seismologist at the University of Maryland—conveniently, the same way you’d deal with radiation exposure. Pile up moon rocks onto your base walls or put habitable structures in lava tubes. Inconveniently, there’s always a chance that a bigger asteroid will come along. Even buried habitation modules would have trouble surviving an assault from a 50-footer—about the length of a semi-trailer. That doesn’t mean a moon base would be helpless against bigger asteroids. Planetary defense has come a long way in just a few decades. NASA’s DART mission demonstrated that it could deflect an asteroid by ramming into the rock with an uncrewed spacecraft, and cutting-­edge experiments at clandestine labs around the US suggest that a nuclear bomb could swat away or entirely vaporize errant space rocks. An array of both ground-based and space-based telescopes specifically designed to spy killers—such as the ATLAS quartet of largely automated telescopes, and the soon-to-be-launched NEO Surveyor infrared telescope—are also aiming to find 90% of the near-Earth asteroids potentially capable of wiping out entire cities (those 460 feet across) in the coming years. All these protections apply to the moon. But asteroids 50 feet across are so stealthy that even NASA’s best telescopes would struggle to see them years or even months in advance. Odds are that they would first be seen not long before they were due to hit the lunar surface. Then what? It’s not unreasonable to imagine several turret-like launch sites on the moon that, owing to the lower gravity and airless environment, could rapidly fire projectiles toward an incoming asteroid. Hit it hard enough and it would break into fragments. Just make sure you shoot when it is as far away as possible: “If you were to break something up into a thousand pieces, then you have a thousand impacts on the moon,” says Lubin. “That might be worse than letting it hit one place.” Having your base destroyed by an asteroid would be awful. But a moon base is far more likely to be imperiled by dust—which, unlike asteroids, is everywhere, all the time. Volcanic lunar rocks, when broken down, turn into sharp, electrostatically charged, adhesive, acrid, abrasive particles. While on the moon, Apollo astronauts who inhaled some of this dust occasionally suffered from a strange “lunar hay fever,” which irritated their eyes and agitated their lungs. That same dust ate through all kinds of gear with disturbing ease. “It rips up seals, gears, motors, spacesuits, habitats—everything else it comes into contact with,” says Justin Cyrus, the CEO of Lunar Outpost, a spaceflight technology company that is providing NASA with one of the Artemis program’s moon rovers. NASA has already developed a defense against these dark shards: the electrodynamic dust shield, or EDS. The lightweight electrodes that make up this shield—which can be layered on machinery, walls, and perhaps even spacesuits—use opposing currents to blast away the electrically charged dust. The EDS worked wonders when tested in labs on Earth, and it also demonstrated its anti-dust magic when sent to the lunar surface in 2025 as part of a NASA-sponsored robotic mission. Amy Fritz, a dust-mitigation researcher at Johnson Space Center, says that the EDS will likely be widely featured all over a lunar habitat. But other dust-fighting instruments are being developed too—including a lint-roller-type object inspired by gecko skin and a sci-fi-esque blaster that uses ionized nitrogen gas. Its first spray will neutralize the charge of the dust to remove its stickiness; then a second burst will clear it off the surface. Irritatingly, the busier a base gets, the dustier things will become. As it expands to the size of a small city, rocket ships will need to deliver cargo and crew to the lunar surface on a frequent cadence. Each takeoff and landing will launch a salvo of dust in every direction, inundating the base’s infrastructure. “You have to worry about essentially being sandblasted every time,” says Fritz. Metzger calculates that even with landing pads more than 3,000 feet away from a base, just 10 to 20 liftoffs or landings would be sufficient to severely damage things like protective coatings on exterior walls and communications equipment. After a while, he says, “everything breaks.” Even if the moon base of the future is buried partly below ground, barricaded against micrometeorites and space radiation, and well equipped with dust-repelling technologies, it’s still a risky gig for any astronaut. Skipping around in a preternaturally agile way at one-sixth of Earth’s gravity will be entertaining. But “microgravity is not good for the human body,” says Dreyer. Muscle wastage, cardiovascular troubles, respiratory problems, and immune deficiencies can arise. Radiation, too, may always afflict lunar residents. And there’s also evidence that disease-causing microbes could stealthily sneak aboard spacecraft, even after stringent sterilization procedures. Those microbes may behave differently than they do on Earth; microgravity experiments (both real and simulated) have suggested that some become more aggressive and more likely to cause disease. “We call them the invisible crew,” says Lisa Carnell, the director of NASA’s Biological and Physical Sciences Division. Hoping to find a better way to protect astronauts, Carnell spearheaded an experiment called AVATAR (A Virtual Astronaut Tissue Analog Response), which launched aboard the Artemis II mission. It used thumb-drive-size chips, containing cells taken from the heart, lungs, or other parts of the body, that could be switched on to act like miniature organs. Scientists have previously used this organ-on-a-chip technology for purposes like testing out new pharmaceuticals in a highly controlled setting. For Artemis II, Carnell’s crew sent up chips containing bone marrow samples taken from the mission’s four astronauts. Bone marrow makes red and white blood cells, as well as platelets—the sort of stuff that affects almost the entire human body. So seeing how space radiation and microgravity affect it would give Carnell and her team vital insight into how astronauts’ biology might change on the moon. The advantage of organ chips is that you can make them for any astronaut and then send them into space ahead of time to see how that person’s organs will react to spaceflight. Researchers envision that data from such chips will be able to clue medics in to the specific preventive treatments or pharmaceuticals that will help moonbound astronauts on their journey. What is NASA likely to send up sooner rather than later? “Brain organoids,” says Carnell. Yes, she adds—that sounds super weird. But it would be good to know how a real brain might respond to being on the moon for several months before people start living there. Okay. Your vast moon base and its inhabitants are protected—as much as is possible—from the treacherous lunar environment. But astronauts’ time is extremely valuable, and humans are fragile creatures. You can’t make them do all the heavy lifting, which means that whether you’re trying to make a tiny lunar outpost or a small city, you’re going to need a lot of robots. “The buildup of the moon base, in the first years, will pretty much 99% be robotic,” says NASA’s García-Galán. Autonomy will be there right from the start. NASA’s new version of the moon buggy, its Lunar Terrain Vehicle, can be driven by the astronauts riding in it but can also be piloted remotely from Earth (or from elsewhere on the moon). There are visions, too, of adding modular robotic arms, towing vehicles, and cargo movers—all still in the preliminary stages of planning. Over the next few decades, though, we’ll likely want an army of robots to do our bidding: excavators to dig up lunar rocks that could be turned into concrete and bricks, plus other robots to flatten surfaces, dig trenches, create building foundations, pave landing pads, and build up dust-deflecting berms to keep the base from being sandblasted by launches and landings. Even solar panels and wiring can potentially be made from moon rocks—something that the spaceflight company Blue Origin demonstrated in labs on Earth in 2023 using a geologic simulant designed to match the composition of lunar regolith. Powering this robot army will require considerable energy. Two-week-long nights make solar power an impractical solution. Aside from a cornucopia of high-­capacity, extreme-temperature-resistant batteries, nuclear power is probably the only viable option. A small, car-size nuclear reactor, filled with an almost literal handful of uranium, could power a small village for a decade without needing to be refueled. And although nobody’s yet tested a nuclear reactor on the moon, few doubt that NASA and the US Department of Energy can conjure up a working design—one that perhaps wouldn’t be too different from those aboard nuclear submarines. A moon base in 2100 would probably feature multiple small, autonomously or remotely operated nuclear reactors, which—conveniently—the base-expanding robots could simply plug themselves into to recharge. But the biggest lunar question mark might be water, which planners want to support the astronauts—and to make fuel to launch things from the lunar surface. A process known as electrolysis, which uses electricity to snap apart molecular bonds, can be used to split water into oxygen and hydrogen, both of which could be used to make rocket fuel. Getting them this way could be far less expensive than sending either all the way from Earth. And as an added bonus, it would provide a source of breathable oxygen. Orbital data has revealed that there is some amount of water preserved in patches on the lunar south pole, but little is known about how much there actually is, what form it takes, or how easy it will be to extract. Will there be solid sheets of ice here and there, ready for us to excavate? Or will there be scattered ice crystals intermingled among the rocky grains, requiring some more inventive form of extraction? A lot of the moon’s water is thought to be hiding in deep, dark craters on the south pole, where the environment is so extreme that robots may freeze up, tumble over, or lose contact with the base. “To me, that is the biggest hurdle,” says Paul van Susante, principal investigator of the Planetary Surface Technology Development Lab at Michigan Technological University. Van Susante’s lab has been putting various construction robots through their paces in moon-like vacuum chambers filled with moon-like rocks. His team was part of NASA’s recent Break the Ice challenge, which invited engineers to build robots to test different ways to excavate such rocks. Some teams used an electric jackhammer and a scoop, while others used scrapers to peel off layers of the ground. The teams simulated the moon’s weak gravity using a crane to lift and offset the weight of the robots. Progress in this regard is speeding along. “I won’t say that’s solved, but we’ve got a lot of the puzzle pieces,” says van Susante. Mining the water will be just one part of an extensive operation. “When you extract it, it’s not going to be clean. It needs to be purified,” says Jessica Atkin, a space botanist at Texas A&M. The technology to do this already exists and works fine on terra firma. But things in space never go the way you expect. “There’s a water purification system on the International Space Station (ISS), and it is known for getting contaminated with microbes,” she says. But if you purify the water sufficiently—something that can handily be achieved on Earth but may falter in microgravity, where fluids flow in stranger ways—then your moon base can hit its stride. With an effective water recycling system, an increasingly populous base will never go thirsty—and, just maybe, you’ll have a lunar greenhouse. In a research station in central Panama, you can walk through a century of forest time, watching scrubby plots of adolescent trees give way to cathedral-­like canopies of mature growth. Forest ecologist Daisy Dent, a senior scientist at ETH Zurich, oversees this sequence of plots across the Barro Colorado Nature Monument, some of which date as far back as the 19th century. Not one of the trees she surveys was planted by human hands. She maps, measures, and tracks each trunk that emerges, revealing how waves of tree life rise from bare earth. Tropical forests like these are our planet’s biggest terrestrial carbon sink, accounting for around 40% of carbon stored on land. As part of an international network of researchers, Dent is deciphering the networks of animal and plant interactions that enable some forests to recover after destruction. The goal is a low-cost, high-impact alternative to typical carbon-­crediting schemes, which plant huge areas with a single species of tree. For over a decade, her research has built a body of evidence that such large “monocrop” programs could be expensive mistakes—and that more resilient forests can rebound naturally in just a few decades, re-creating ecosystems that suck up vast quantities of carbon dioxide. Few places are better to demonstrate this than Barro Colorado, a reserve covering the forested peninsulas and islands that formed when construction of the Panama Canal flooded the area in 1914. Under the management of the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute, generations of ecologists have amassed more than a century of records. That means Dent can trace why some plots rebound after clear-cutting for farming and forestry while others falter. Fruit-eating animals—including spider monkeys, bats, and parrots—are responsible for planting more than 70% of trees in tropical forests, and Dent’s dataset can tell which built each patch of trees by depositing seeds in their nutrient-rich droppings. Many of these creatures worked together to plant a diverse 70-year-old patch whose canopy is approaching the stunning structure and richness of an ancient jungle. In contrast, a patch of similar age populated by narrow trunks of a single long-lived species, Gustavia superba, hints that tropical rodents called agoutis are more or less the sole animal architects behind this more homogeneous forest. The data she collects helps write a playbook for when to let ecosystems regenerate and when to give them a helping hand. One 2024 estimate in Nature found that some 215 million hectares of forest worldwide (an area larger than Mexico) could regenerate naturally. “Tropical forests are underpinned by this massive network of interactions,” Dent says—interactions between life-forms like the fungi that help trees take up nutrients, as well as pollinators and seed-distributors. Supporting those relationships is key to growing forests that lock up carbon and help sustain local communities and wildlife. Just over a decade ago, as the landmark Paris Agreement was being codified in 2015, forest recovery seized global attention as a solution to the climate crisis. Global leaders rallied behind plans to regrow a trillion trees, after the ecologist Thomas Crowther highlighted their potential to capture a decade’s worth of global carbon emissions. But the biggest question has lingered: How, exactly, do you regrow two trees for every star in the Milky Way? While startups have proposed seed-spraying drones and nutrient-coated seed bombs, Dent has spent two decades studying what happens when we let forests rebuild themselves. As a PhD student around the turn of the millennium, she researched old-growth forests in northern Borneo but felt hopeless watching entire landscapes felled for palm oil plantations. Rather than despair, she turned to restoration. Then, as a postdoc in 2005, she joined Panama’s Prorena, a joint initiative between the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute and the Yale School of Forestry and Environmental Studies, which aimed at determining how best to establish diverse native forests on clear-cut land. Back then, secondary forests—ones that grow naturally after the original forest was lost—had been “a bit of a niche research area,” Dent says. But as farming and human infrastructure have driven deforestation, resurgent forests now account for some 70% of all tropical canopies. Prorena initially followed the usual playbook of transplanting nursery trees to rangelands, but Dent began to question whether planting was necessary. In 2009, she published an encouraging paper showing that animal populations rebound quickly as forests naturally regrow. In 2010, she began working in Barro Colorado on the plots established by the pioneering American ecologist Julie Denslow. In 1994, Denslow identified plots that had been regenerating naturally for 20 to 100 years, and Dent would add younger ones to continue the sequence. The site provides not just general data on forest regrowth but in-depth demographic information over time, says Robin Chazdon, a professor emeritus of ecology and evolutionary biology at the University of Connecticut who collaborated with Denslow when she established the plots. “It tells you more about the actual processes that are happening in the site,” she says. For example, previous research had shown that fast-growing “pioneer trees,” the first to thrive in the damaged land, are eventually overtaken by larger carbon-storing trees. Dent’s research helped fill in gaps about the mechanics of how this happens—or doesn’t happen—in individual cases.
