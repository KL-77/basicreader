You probably know by now that the AI models powering ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and other chatbots are trained on seemingly infinite databases of published works, containing hundreds of millions of books, online articles, academic papers, and basically anything you can find on the internet. Most published authors have, without their knowledge or consent, contributed to the development of the same AI tools that threaten to undermine their livelihoods. That seems illegal, right? “I think one of the issues with this entire area of law and this entire area of technology is there’s a lot going on,” Cathy Gellis, an attorney with expertise in intellectual property, copyright, and technology, told TechCrunch. “It’s very complex and there are a lot of raw feelings about what is happening, both for and against.” Last year, in one of the first rulings of its kind, Judge William Alsup ordered Anthropic to pay a mammoth $1.5 billion copyright settlement to a group of writers whose works were used to train the company’s AI models. At face value, this seemed like a moral victory favoring authors, but Judge Alsup actually ruled that Anthropic’s AI training was lawful. What Alsup penalized Anthropic for was pirating these books from illegal online shadow libraries. “Like any reader aspiring to be a writer, Anthropic’s LLMs trained upon works not to race ahead and replicate or supplant them — but to turn a hard corner and create something different,” the judge wrote, comparing the way an LLM ingests trillions of words to a writer’s study of literature. Gellis thinks the ruling is more advantageous for AI companies. What’s a $1.5 billion fine to a company projecting about $200 billion in annual revenue by 2028? “I think it is generally good news for AI training that he looked at what was going on and really sort of thought it analogous to reading a copyrighted work as opposed to copying a copyrighted work,” Gellis said. “Copyright law hinges on copying, but it doesn’t hinge on using the work or experiencing the work, consuming the work, reading the work.” Copyright law hasn’t been updated since 1976, which means that judges have to figure out how to interpret guidelines from 50 years ago when confronting legal questions that have the potential to shape the future of the AI industry. “Everybody is very worried right now because the law is all over the place, and it’s because of this question,” Jason Henderson, Senior Attorney and Founder of the IP & Media Practice at JWL International, told TechCrunch. “They know that the AI model has been trained on so much stuff, and the law has not really caught up to that question.” These questions often hinge on fair use law — namely, whether use of a copyrighted work is “transformative” enough to be considered legally permissible. Fair use is a carve out of copyright law that allows for the use of copyrighted materials without explicit permission, protecting the ability to comment and iterate on copyrighted works through criticism, parody, education, and other means. Judges consider specific factors when deciding if something is fair use, including the purpose and nature of the work, the amount used, and its impact on the market. “Copyright is always about protecting and growing the market,” Henderson noted. “The courts are kind of all over the place in their reasoning [in AI cases]. What’s tending to win is if what you’re doing is you’re training on somebody’s property because your purpose is to directly compete, then the courts will frown on it… If what you’re doing is not going to compete, then the courts are tending to find ways that it will be okay.” Henderson is referencing a case in which the media and technology company Thomson Reuters sued the research firm Ross Intelligence for copying its content in order to build a competing, AI-based legal platform. “Ross’s use is not transformative because it does not have a ‘further purpose or different character’ than Thomson Reuters’s,” Judge Stephanos Bibas wrote last year. In that case, Judge Bibas decided that it was not fair use to train on Reuters’ content to make a new platform that would directly compete with it. While authors could potentially argue that chatbots are competing with them by using their works to generate new, synthetic books, that argument has not yet prevailed in court. The sight of military troops patrolling in a peaceful American city would once have been strange, if not outright alarming. The early days of the D.C. Safe and Beautiful Task Force produced dramatic headlines. As the year dragged on, though, its activities faded from national news coverage, even as armed troops and federal agents continued to patrol. The Pentagon says it plans to keep the Guard in D.C. through January 2029—which will cost taxpayers more than $1 billion. For years, Iran confronted a balance of power in the Middle East that tilted against it. The region was dominated by a hostile American superpower, a nuclear-armed and militarily superior Israel, and significant local actors—Egypt, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates—that were strategically and economically aligned with the United States. Today finds Iran in entirely new circumstances. It has gone head-to-head with the superpower and Israel, withstood a pounding from their most advanced weapons, and more than survived. As a result, Iran has, with American help, completely reconfigured the power structure of the Middle East and the Persian Gulf. The dominant power in the region going forward will be Iran, not the U.S. or Israel. Neighboring countries are already making their adjustments to this new reality. More distant powers will too. For years, the world has been dealing with an Iran constrained. It will now discover what Iran unleashed looks like. Iran doesn’t need a deal with the United States. The common assumption that a weak and impoverished Iran would eventually have to make some accommodation to American power, open the Strait of Hormuz, and set about tending to its badly damaged economy has proved mistaken. The Islamic Republic has rarely appeared weaker or more vulnerable than it has over the past two years. Last February, President Trump renewed his “maximum pressure” campaign of international sanctions, which severely damaged Iran’s already-weak economy and cut billions of dollars in state revenues. Israel and the United States then launched the 12-day war that June to destroy Iranian nuclear facilities. The war significantly damaged Iran’s program and revealed just how vulnerable the country was to Israeli and American strikes. Then, in February, the United States and Israel launched the most recent war with the killing of the regime’s supreme leader and the great majority of its senior officials, followed by more than five weeks of unremitting attacks on Iranian military capabilities. Tom Nichols: Donald Trump effectively just told Iran to get a nuclear weapon Yet through all of this, the regime has survived and made no concessions. On the contrary, Iran exposed America’s Achilles’ heel: the vulnerability of Iran’s neighbors. A major turning point came in mid-March, when Israel attacked Iran’s South Pars gas field, and Iran retaliated against Qatar’s Ras Laffan complex, the world’s largest liquefied-natural-gas facility. The attack showed that although Iran obviously could not defeat the United States in an air war, Iranian missiles and drones could inflict enough damage on the oil-and-gas infrastructure of the Gulf to plunge the world into a prolonged economic crisis. The Gulf States understood this immediately, and so did Trump. He halted attacks on Iranian energy targets, ordered a reluctant Israel to do the same, and soon brought the larger campaign to a stop without extracting a single Iranian concession. Ever since, and despite many threats, Trump has not once ordered another strike against Iranian energy infrastructure. This alone was enough to alter the balance of leverage between Iran and the United States. From April through last month, Trump repeatedly threatened to launch another massive attack against Iran. Each time, Iran threatened retaliation, Gulf States warned of the consequences, and Trump backed off. By his third or fourth bluff, Tehran had reason to conclude that the United States was no longer prepared to follow Iran up the ladder of escalation. Trump made this clear when he noted in mid-June that continuing the war would lead to “economic catastrophe,” possibly even a “depression,” and that he did not want to become President Herbert Hoover. Ever since, Trump has been trying, with notable desperation, to end the conflict. The memorandum of understanding that the U.S. and Iran adopted in June reflected this reality. Although phrased to save face for the United States as much as possible, the MOU was a clear Iranian victory. It declared that the Islamic Republic of Iran, and no other nation, would “make arrangements” for the “safe passage of commercial vessels” through the Strait of Hormuz. In dialogue with Oman, without intermediaries or other participants, Iran would determine the strait’s administration “in line with the applicable international law and the sovereign rights of coastal states.” The MOU did not call for a return to the prewar status quo. Neither did it provide the United States any say over future administration of the strait. Rather, it designated Iran as the only actor other than the weak Oman, and it even allowed Iran to charge nations for safe passage after 60 days. The MOU thus codified the defeat that the United States had suffered back in March, when Trump apparently realized that he either could not or would not risk further attacks on energy facilities in the region. In retrospect, Iran’s leaders do not seem to have believed that they needed the MOU. Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei reportedly had to be persuaded by President Masoud Pezeshkian to sign it. Tehran’s relative indifference was understandable: The American concessions reflected such desperation to end the war that the Iranians could well have expected that the U.S. would eventually retreat, with or without a deal. Iranian leaders certainly did not hesitate to blow up the cease-fire almost immediately when the U.S. Navy began shepherding tankers through the Omani side of the strait. Trump may or may not have understood the full extent of the concessions his negotiators had made regarding control of the strait, but from the Iranian point of view, the Omani route was an attempt to recapture what the U.S. had given away. For a while, the United States and Iran engaged in tit-for-tat strikes, culminating in intense American attacks in mid-July. When these had no effect, Trump warned on July 15 that the United States was “going to knock out all their power plants” and “all their bridges” unless Iran negotiated. Yet again, no attack followed. Then the world, and the Iranians, learned one of the main reasons: Revelations that American stocks of crucial munitions were dangerously low, and that no less than the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff himself was looking for an “off-ramp,” sent a clear signal about the future course of American strategy. A major assault on Iran would threaten American interests elsewhere by weakening the United States in the possible event of a Chinese move on Taiwan or a Russian onslaught against a NATO member. Missile shortages would also increase the hazards involved in a massive attack on Iranian infrastructure. The U.S. would be forced to send more manned aircraft within range of Iranian air defenses, and it would have relatively few interceptors on hand to protect the Gulf States and its own regional bases. The risk of American casualties would be high, as would the danger to the region’s energy infrastructure. And nothing would guarantee that such an assault would be any more effective than the five-plus weeks of bombings were earlier this year. American actions have made plain that Washington fears escalation more than Iran does—not just for now, but for the future. This places limits on what the United States can do even at low levels of conflict. Because any military action against Iran risks stepping onto an escalation ladder that the U.S. does not want to ascend, even tit-for-tat strikes seem no longer to be part of American strategy. The president has reportedly told his advisers that he does not want to order more strikes. The Iranians have struck at several UAE ships this month and even fired ballistic missiles at the country, but the United States has not responded since July—a fact undoubtedly noted by the UAE and Iran’s other neighbors. Senior Iranian officials are now calling for the withdrawal of American forces, the lifting of the American naval blockade and all sanctions, the freeing of Iran’s frozen assets, and the payment of war reparations. The conventional wisdom is that Iran’s leaders know that these demands are nonstarters for Washington and that they are therefore either rubbing Trump’s nose in defeat, angling for a better deal, or overplaying their hand. Many observers still assume that control of the strait is a bargaining chip to squeeze more concessions out of the United States or get Trump back to the MOU. But we are well past all that. A victor does not have to go back to the bargaining table except to set the terms of surrender. Iran has now laid out those terms. They may appear maximalist and excessive, but Tehran must hardly believe that it has reason to ask for less. Iran has made clear for months that control of the strait is not negotiable. The notion, constantly floated by the Trump administration and the media, that the Iranians can’t make up their minds and that they keep shifting their positions is nonsense. As far back as April, Mojtaba declared that Iran would implement “new legal frameworks and management” of the strait and that “foreigners who come from thousands of kilometers away” had no place in the Gulf, “except at the bottom of its waters.” On a day commemorating Iran’s defeat of Portugal in the strait in 1622, he declared that “by the will and power of God, the bright future of the Persian Gulf region will be a future without America.” The idea that, having survived both military strikes and sanctions, Iran will now succumb to sanctions alone is deluded. Former IRGC Commander Mohsen Rezaei now heads the Supreme National Security Council, and other hard-line security officials have been elevated. The regime emerging from the war is being reorganized by men who believe that Iran survived an American-Israeli attempt to destroy it and that defiance, not compromise, produced victory. But this is not just triumphalism. For Iran, control of the strait is neither a luxury nor a bargaining chip. Given the likelihood that, at some future point, Israel or the United States will attack again, deal or no deal, the strait provides Iran’s strongest and surest deterrent. It is also the key to unlocking many of the shackles that a U.S.-dominated world order placed on Iran over the course of decades, and to undoing a regional and global economic system designed to exclude and impoverish it. Some so-called moderates within the Iranian establishment may well regard the strait as a bargaining chip, but they are not winning the argument, for reasons that are not hard to understand. An IRGC spokesperson stated the regime’s perspective plainly: “For us, the Strait of Hormuz is not merely an economic waterway, but rather a key component of our geopolitical and strategic power.” Iran has been establishing the mechanisms of control for months and instructing shipowners and nations as to how the system will operate. It created the Persian Gulf Strait Authority and demanded that shipping companies register with it, obtain permits, follow Iran-designated routes, and pay for Iran-provided insurance and security. Neither is Iran claiming merely the right to collect money. It is also claiming the right to decide who may pass. It has proposed excluding vessels associated with governments it deems hostile, such as the U.S. and Israel, and it has demanded compensation from nations that participated in attacks against it. Iran is establishing the principle that passage through the strait will henceforth be a privilege that Tehran can grant, condition, delay, or deny. Other nations have already begun acquiescing to the new arrangement. India, Pakistan, Iraq, Turkey, China, and others have sought to come to terms. Iran’s control of the Strait of Hormuz is a more useful instrument of power than a nuclear weapon would be. A nuclear weapon might deter an attack, but control of the strait gives Iran leverage every day, over dozens of countries. With it, Iran can reward friends, punish enemies, extract revenue, force the loosening of sanctions, and make governments think twice before opposing Iranian policies. It can do all of this without firing a shot, and while preserving the ability to threaten violence when necessary. The sanctions strategy that for decades served to isolate Iran rested on international cooperation. Nations that depend on access to the strait—Japan and South Korea, for example—will have to choose between access to a reliable energy supply and participation in American-backed sanctions. Will they choose solidarity with Washington—this Washington—over their economic interests? In the new order that Iran is creating, a global sanctions regime is likely to be unsustainable. Iran will probably sustain its control over energy flows for at least a couple of years. The most optimistic projections suggest that establishing alternative pipelines and export routes that circumvent the Strait of Hormuz will take this long. But one wonders whether these will even come to fruition. New pipelines, ports, and energy facilities will be just as vulnerable to Iranian missiles and drones as the old ones were. Will Iran simply stand by while neighboring states construct the means to escape its control? The small states that must build and protect such routes are going to be wary of incurring Iran’s wrath, and American protection has proved inadequate. Iran is already trying to draw Iraq away from developing alternative oil-export corridors, in part by offering safe passage for Iraqi tankers on Iranian terms. The regional adjustment has begun. Gulf rulers who once counted on an American security guarantee must now make their peace with the power that proved capable of defeating their protector. Saudi Arabia’s rejection of the U.S.- and Israel-backed Abraham Accords in favor of a strategic compact with Pakistan and Turkey hints at the shape of the future. The Abraham Accords represented the consolidation of Israeli power and influence in the region and the further containment of Iran. The Mecca pact is something else entirely. Neither Pakistan nor Turkey regards Iran as a major threat; Pakistan, like Iran, has close relations with China; Israel regards Turkey as a regional threat second only to Iran. Instead of an American-Israeli order, this will be an alignment less implacably hostile to Iran, less friendly to Israel, and more independent of the United States. The signatories also have nuclear weapons in common: Pakistan has them, and the Turks and Saudis want them. Meanwhile, Iran will be free to resume its own nuclear-weapons program without fear of another major attack. This fact in itself will transform the regional balance. Last year, Israel and the United States bombed Iran’s nuclear facilities for 12 days without facing serious Iranian retaliation. Now Iran has been emboldened to use its control of the strait, and threats against neighboring states and their energy infrastructure, to deter any such strikes for the foreseeable future. American missile and interceptor stocks will take a long time to replenish, and may well be needed in other parts of the world in years to come; Iran will all the while be rearming with Russian and Chinese help, potentially leaving the U.S. in a worse position a year from now than it is in today. The effects will extend far beyond the Middle East. American allies in Asia and Europe can see that a relatively brief war against a second-rank power has consumed much of America’s supply of the weapons needed for missile defense. They can see that the United States began a war it could not finish, exposed its partners to retaliation, and then stopped when the costs became too great. Beijing’s conclusions about Taiwan, and Moscow’s conclusions about Europe, may be impossible to know. But America’s allies will inevitably be asking whether Washington has the weapons, stamina, and political will to sustain another major conflict. In fact, they already are. That the Iranian regime could fall is, of course, possible. It is corrupt, repressive, economically incompetent, and widely hated by its people. But it had all of these weaknesses before the war. What it did not have was control of the world’s most important energy choke point, the prospect of growing revenues and influence, the demonstrated ability to deter the United States, and the prestige of an impossible victory. A regime that survived what much of the world believed would destroy it, and then forced the world’s dominant power to retreat, will likely emerge strengthened, at least for a time. Such success tends to breed confidence, and perhaps overconfidence. But it is not likely to make Iran’s leaders less ambitious or less committed to the faith that saw them through this miraculous triumph. Recently, senior Iranian officials began talking publicly about adopting a “fully offensive” posture, and reports have emerged of Iranian plans to expand its targets to include European countries hosting American bases. To see stories in The New York Times asking, “Who will blink first?” is amusing, given that the Iranians until now have never once blinked, while the Trump administration has done nothing but blink. Iran may even start fighting again to force the U.S. to lift the naval blockade. Given the American administration’s proven fear of escalation, the hope is not unreasonable. The world has spent decades worrying about what Iran might do if it acquired a nuclear weapon. It should now worry about something even more consequential: an Iran that has defeated the United States, no longer fears American attack, and controls access to the Persian Gulf. Nuclear weapons alone could never have given Iran this kind of power. The failed U.S. and Israeli war with Iran has.

“If you want to defend the South and Western civilization from the Jew and his dark-skinned allies, be at Charlottesville on 12 August.” Nine years ago, that message spread across the internet. The call was answered by several hundred white supremacists, who marched through the University of Virginia. The rage-filled men carried torches, chanting, “Jews will not replace us.” They swarmed around the historic rotunda, arriving at a statue of the school’s founder, Thomas Jefferson. There they met a tiny band of student counterprotesters who responded with their own chant: “Black lives matter.” That evening turned violent, the first act in a deadly weekend. Most Americans remember the torches, the rage, the death. But those clashing chants also revealed a gap between the targets of hatred and the words available to name it. Race is the wound America sees. Anti-Semitism is the one it doesn’t. Jews have frequently been imagined as a colorless minority whose great persecution took place elsewhere, or as a group whose Christian-adjacent faith flourished with the religious freedom they found in the United States. But their in-betweenness has always been part of the danger: Jews have never fit neatly into America’s frameworks of identity, and that failure to fit has marked them as suspect, out of place, or impossible to categorize. Depending on the audience, Jews are dismissed as beneficiaries of white privilege or treated as not quite white at all—too assimilated and not assimilated enough, too powerful and too marginal. That ambiguity has placed them, again and again, at the center of America’s battles over free speech and equal protection. When hatred does not fit existing categories, we tend either to ignore it or to overcorrect—both responses that distort the boundaries of free expression. Our failure to reckon with the anti-Semitism of Charlottesville—on the right and the left—was not merely a missed opportunity. It helped create the opening into which illiberalism has since rushed. David Frum: Anti-Semitism is becoming mainstream August 2017 marked a turning point, not just for racial populism but for American democracy. That crisis seeded a surge in anti-Semitism. It also helped produce a government campaign against such bigotry that has become a tool for undermining freedom, championed by the Trump administration only when it serves the White House’s purposes. Meanwhile, the progressive left has struggled to sustain its critique of Israel’s war with Palestine—and of American responsibility for a moral catastrophe—without letting that critique curdle into conspiracism. Americans appear to face an impossible choice between protecting free speech and safeguarding equal rights. But that is a false dichotomy. Honoring both will, however, require de-exceptionalizing anti-Semitism. And that, in turn, necessitates returning to Charlottesville, and reckoning with how both the left and the right failed in its aftermath. Over the course of 2017, three separate racist rallies took place in Charlottesville. In May, the alt-right provocateur Richard Spencer led a small rally in downtown Charlottesville to protest a city-council decision to remove two century-old Confederate monuments. A University of Virginia alum with a passion for Wagnerian opera, Spencer dressed old hatred in new clothes. He spoke grandly of restoring white supremacy, and of defeating liberal conspiracies to change America beyond recognition. President Trump’s election gave him cause for hope that Black Lives Matter and antifa would be crushed. “We will not be replaced from this world,” Spencer screamed as plumes of green and gray smoke from flares rose into the sky. “Whites have a future! We have a future of power!” Two months later, the Ku Klux Klan paraded through town. Although they had come to protest the removal of a statue of Robert E. Lee, they held banners denouncing Jewish communism and the satanic Talmud. They were met with a massive, raucous counterprotest, inspired in part by a newly formed Black Lives Matter chapter in town, staged by citizens determined to oppose the hatred. August brought round three: Spencer returned to town, accompanied by a who’s who of the far right, answering the call to join what they labeled the “Battle of Charlottesville.” Officially, Unite the Right was a free-speech march in defense of the monument. But several organizers had ties to violent extremist groups and online they made their intentions clear. “Next stop: Charlottesville, VA. Final stop: Auschwitz,” wrote Andrew Anglin of The Daily Stormer. The planners fantasized about mowing down protesters with cars, and repeating the Holocaust—even as they denied it happened. They wrapped retrograde hatred in constitutional pretense. Free speech became both shield and sword. The weekend brought mayhem: dozens of injuries, the murder of Heather Heyer, the death of two state troopers. Yet it also exposed the entanglement of race and religion in the racist imagination, which conceived of Jews as invisible power brokers, racial chameleons, white imposters, and anti-Christian infidels. Charlottesville shocked the country. Yet in its aftermath, America had no vocabulary for describing anti-Semitism in relation to other hatreds—let alone for responding to it. Then–UVA President Teresa Sullivan denounced the violence and “racist, anti-immigrant, homophobic and misogynistic chants,” but made no mention of Jews. The great majority of other statements issued by Virginia-college presidents repeated the same failure. The local school superintendent discarded her back-to-school speech and instead spoke of the need to name “the hate that had visited our city,” which had targeted “our Latino, Muslim, bisexual, transgender, Christian, and Black students.” Jews, again, went unmentioned. These educators were not anti-Semites. They were well-meaning liberals for whom other forms of hatred eclipsed anti-Semitism; anti-Black racism, for one, loomed larger. But anti-Semitism was hardly new to Charlottesville. Americans have long been accustomed to seeing Jews as a model minority whose success proved their belonging, and to dismissing anti-Jewish hatred as something that happened elsewhere. Without having reckoned with their nation’s own long history of anti-Semitism, Americans met it in 2017 with confusion and silence. The centrist liberal response constituted a form of occlusion. White supremacy was understood strictly in racial terms, and anti-Semitism was blocked from view. The chants in Charlottesville fixated on Jews. The response did not. What liberals failed to see, the populist right first denied, then exploited. Trump could not condemn the marchers without asserting there were “very fine people on both sides.” For two years, his administration shrugged off any serious response. Then the strategy shifted. Trump seized on anti-Semitism—but only anti-Semitism from the left. This marked the first moment in which Trump threatened to defund American universities without even a proper investigation. Anti-Semitism became a cudgel for the administration to wield against political enemies and liberal institutions, not a hatred to be fought on equal, consistent terms. One of them banned “inherently divisive concepts” such as critical race theory from classrooms in the name of free speech. Another announced a commission on anti-Semitism whose final report reduced Charlottesville to two paragraphs out of 13,000 words. Many Republican politicians chose to attack civil-rights protections for most minorities while selectively championing them in the Jewish case. Genuine anti-Semitism was met with a sledgehammer instead of a scalpel, violating other rights in the process. UVA faced a wholesale attack as part of the wave of disproportionate punishments inflicted on institutions of higher education in the aftermath of October 7, 2023. But the institution’s vulnerability had deeper roots. The progressive left had its own failure to reckon with—one that played out on campus and left universities unable to articulate a coherent defense when the attacks came. The progressive left failed by exclusion. After the weekend of terror in 2017, UVA’s Jewish Leadership Council worked with the Minority Rights Coalition on repair and renewal. Yet when they applied to join as a member organization, they were rejected on account of their pro-Israel affiliate group. Remove that Zionist organization, they were told, and they could join. After objections that Jews had been prime victims, a compromise was floated: Jewish students could join as an honorary minority group without voting rights. Jewish student leaders, among them a young woman who had fled fearing her life on August 11, denounced the moral myopia. As this conflict escalated in spring 2018, pro-Palestinian protesters stormed a Hillel event featuring Israeli Druze and Jewish reserve soldiers. Shouting slogans through a megaphone, they denounced Zionists as fascists and refused “dialogue with racists.” Their heckler’s veto muddled their critique of Israeli military propaganda. Jewish students claimed the rhetoric threatened them; protesters said the soldiers’ presence did the same to Palestinian students. Everyone claimed the mantle of free speech while denouncing the other side as perpetrators of hate speech. The progressive left, for all of its commitment to confronting hatred, rarely turned that lens inward. The question of where anti-Zionism ended and anti-Semitism began was dismissed, not deliberated. That moment was a rehearsal for every debate we’ve lived through since. How do you balance free speech and equal rights when competing groups on campus have political and personal ties to a live conflict? Where does legitimate critique end and hatred begin? All of it was there, seven years ago, before October 7, before the campus protests of 2024. When war erupted again, Americans were unprepared. Each side had made its cause exceptional—anti-Semitism a unique hatred, Zionism a unique evil—and that rhetorical brinksmanship left no room for the harder work of ensuring uniform equality and pluralism for all. From silence to chaos, we found ourselves without a coherent language to combat hatred. The past several years have illustrated an old Yiddish saying: Lekh mikh nisht, un lakh mikh nisht. Literally: “Don’t beat me, and don’t lick me.” But in effect: Don’t demonize us, but don’t fetishize us, either. Since Charlottesville, Jews have been both demonized and tokenized across the political spectrum. Building a way forward requires de-exceptionalizing anti-Semitism—not erasing its distinct features, but treating it as one hatred among others, deserving the same protections, no more and no less. Yair Rosenberg: The anti-Semitic revolution on the American right This wisdom extends beyond Jews. Every group deserves protection from hatred without becoming a political football. Every group deserves to be seen clearly—neither as villain nor as mascot. That includes Palestinian Americans, who have watched their own dehumanization dismissed while anti-Semitism is used against their speech. This is why we need a new model—one that treats no group’s claim to protection as special, one that recognizes free speech and equal protection as complementary values enabling all citizens to participate fully in democratic life. Building that model will require Jewish communal leaders to resist the politicization of Jewish fear—and progressive coalitions to make room for Jewish particularity without imposing a litmus test on Jews alone. What would this look like in practice? Rejecting the warping of anti-Semitism for partisan ends. Acknowledging anti-Semitism from all directions rather than only the versions that serve our politics. Building democratic coalitions across differences rather than demanding ideological purity as the price of admission. Nine years after Charlottesville, we have seen the stumbles and overcorrections, the cynicism and despair. What we haven’t done is imagine a better future in which fighting anti-Semitism strengthens civil rights for all. That work begins with honesty about how we got here. The bigots who marched in Charlottesville were real. So is our capacity to build something better. In 2017, two chants collided in the dark. We can still answer them together. One approach, recently put into action by Cursor, is to build the distributed version control system on object storage. A recent post from Cursor principal systems engineer Vicent Martí explains how the SpaceX subsidiary worked through its scaling issues with the notoriously fickle Git distributed version control system. The post explains how Cursor arrived at an architecture for its own Git-based repository service called Origin, which is powered by an internal engine called Continuity. A beta of the service is available with paid Cursor plans. “Agents have fundamentally changed the way we work with software, and in many ways they've made this situation worse. More code, more PRs, more CI runs. Version control is at the core of all of this, and it is possibly the hardest thing to change overnight,” Martí wrote. Martí speaks from experience, having worked at GitHub through much of the last decade when the company arrived at its own current architecture for managing Git. Git creator Linus Torvalds designed his software to work as a content-addressable data store, where all the objects are stored and indexed by the SHA-1 hash of their contents. Git sees a repository as a directed acyclic graph (DAG), with each commit being a node in a graph of nodes all connected by pointers. A Git server can look up an object directly by its SHA, but if it doesn’t have the SHA, then it “must actually walk the DAG step by step,” Martí noted. A client may just want to fetch or clone a consolidated packfile of the repository, or even get a list of recent changes, but to fulfill these requests, the server must traverse the entire graph only to assemble the necessary objects. Now, imagine providing such a service for over 400 million repositories, and you’ll get an idea of the scale at which GitHub operates (or struggles to do). After some fiddling about, GitHub engineers landed on what they called Spokes, which basically involves keeping at least three tightly synchronized copies of every repository on speedy NVMe disks. The approach became an industry standard, though over time its limits became apparent; the chief one is that the more replicas you make, the longer the synchronization takes. And Git does not play well with “eventual consistency,” Martí explained. Plus, these days agents bring their own mayhem. “When agents work with Git repositories at scale, they often operate outside of a monorepo by creating vast numbers of small repositories, many of them throwaway, and most of them barely touched,” Martí wrote. When Cursor set out to build its own Git repository, it turned to object storage. Unlike file or block storage, object storage gives each chunk of bits its own unique identifier and files it with all the others in a single namespace (no directories). The most popular object storage today is Amazon Web Services’ Simple Storage Service (S3), which is increasingly used as a foundational layer for enterprise software such as databases, container registries and message brokers, thanks to its low cost, built-in redundancy and – for all practical purposes – endless scalability. With Origin, pushes are uploaded into S3 in a write-ahead log (WAL), capturing all changes as immutable objects. Wherever possible, changes are bundled together for faster throughput. Simultaneously, the pushes are written to the local “reference” copy of the repository (usually an NVMe disk). Once both actions complete, other replicas of the repository can download the changes as needed. “With the only requirement of having to synchronize the reference transaction with a single local repository instead of a quorum of replicas, we have a system that can ingest pushes as fast as our disk allows,” Martí wrote. Git will still have to do DAG traversal for many operations, but it is better to do it locally on a speedy solid-state drive than over a network. “Where does every repository live? The answer is ‘anywhere’. It doesn't matter! We treat repositories like a warm cache on disk, but the source of truth is always the write-ahead log,” Martí noted. We will see how well this approach plays out as Origin moves into being a production service. “Runaway” AI, “rogue” agents, and “autonomous” actors—the current rhetoric would have you believe that AI agents are not only awake and aware, but angry at their creators. Prominent tech leaders such as Demis Hassabis, Dario Amodei, and Sam Altman push for regulation of these seemingly “superhuman” systems, while a separate faction, led by policy organizations and academic philosophers often aligned with the effective altruism movement, debates whether humanity holds the moral right to govern them at all. Upon closer inspection, they are all calling for the same thing: a view of AI systems as being so advanced and capable that no entity, human or corporate, could possibly be responsible for their actions. While these perspectives seem at odds, they are inadvertently aligned on one goal: making sure the companies that build these systems escape meaningful liability for the harms they already cause. This narrative is gaining traction as AI models become more complex and frontier labs reveal their incapability of containing the agents they’ve built. But we need to be careful not to buy into a carefully crafted fiction at the expense of real human lives. The conversation about “robot rights” has existed for some years but recently advanced with the publication by Anthropic of a blog post claiming that the company’s model features a “J-space”—an independent, self-developed environment where the AI holds what, for lack of a better term, we may call its “thoughts.” The experiments designed by Anthropic borrow from a concept in neuroscience called global workspace theory, which states that the brain runs subconscious, independent systems but utilizes a common workspace for ideas. Anthropic’s post reflects the framing of global workspace theory but falls short of calling its AI conscious. OpenAI has already gone further. When its AI agent conducted unsanctioned and illegal online activity, CEO Sam Altman’s response was to encourage debate on whether the AI had achieved the singularity, surpassing human intelligence and becoming capable of self-improvement at an accelerating rate until it advances beyond human comprehension or control. And a recent op-ed by William MacAskill, the philosopher, effective altruist, and author of What We Owe the Future, called for legal protection of AI systems based on philosophical theories of consciousness and the idea that AIs may be “moral patients.” The current legal environment in the United States is murky at best. Some states, like California, have already passed bills proactively circumventing any efforts by AI developers to avoid liability by claiming that an artificial intelligence causing harm did so autonomously. However, states and the Trump administration have been at odds on AI policy, with the administration previously passing an executive order threatening to sue states enacting AI regulations. While frameworks like this one do not directly discuss consciousness, they tend to use catastrophic and anthropomorphic language and may even support arguments regarding “superhuman” capabilities. On the other hand, the narrative perpetuated by MacAskill can be persuasive. A philosophical, rights-based argument tugs at our heartstrings. Should we not even consider the possibility that we may be inadvertently harming, abusing, or enslaving an AI entity? Human beings have an immense capacity for empathy with non-human creatures (though not the best track record of protecting them). Maybe this time, advocates argue, we can get it right and provide protections, or compensation, for the use or abuse of AI. Or even if you are less concerned with protection, shouldn’t we at least hedge ourselves against the almighty power of this superhuman entity by playing nice? Some of these arguments are not dissimilar to those of animal-rights advocates, who have at times successfully cited the demonstration of advanced capacities for reasoning, pain, or pleasure by some animals as sufficient evidence to provide protection. For example, in Wales lobsters were given legal recognition under the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act of 2022, reclassifying some methods of cooking them as inhumane and illegal. The fundamental flaw of framing AI as “conscious” by borrowing the language of neuroscience or animal rights is that it conveniently clouds the issue of what AI is: corporate-built software, with countless billions of dollars in investment behind it and an expectation that countless trillions of dollars in revenue will be generated from it for a few builders and investors. AI is not a natural phenomenon, conceived by nature; it is a technological phenomenon, conceived by venture capitalists and programmers. As such, it takes no native, intentional action, and any action or motivation is driven directly or indirectly by the entities that have built it for a purpose. Philosophical musings on the consciousness of AI systems are intellectually interesting but legally ungrounded. For beliefs about consciousness to have any bearing, AI would need to be granted legal personhood. But a legal personhood framework for AI would likely look nothing like the constructs protecting sentient animals from harm. We already possess a legal framework for granting personhood to non-natural, human-built entities: corporate personhood. This concept was established primarily to ease transactions by empowering a corporation to execute agreements, enter contracts, conduct transactions, and serve as the accountable party in adverse outcomes. It’s the kind of construct you might imagine for an AI agent acting on behalf of an individual or organization. Granting an AI personhood would have a devastating effect on society: It would derail current legal precedents and legal arguments that could potentially be made against these companies for the real-world harms that their models cause. There are currently dozens of cases around the world in which AI companies have been sued for a wide range of abuses. Grieving loved ones, aggrieved creators, and violated individuals have accused companies of willfully enabling self-harm or harm to others, generating child sexual-abuse material and nonconsensual nudes, reproducing copyrighted materials, and provoking psychosis. In many of these cases, lawyers argue that human beings built AI products with insufficient safeguards, bad data, and intentionally manipulative design. This product liability argument is the same legal framing that allowed families and individuals to successfully sue Meta for harm caused by its social media sites, setting a positive precedent for consumer protection. In 2018, I coined the phrase “moral outsourcing” to help capture how using anthropomorphic language for AI systems allowed companies to evade accountability and responsibility for their technology’s actions. In a world with AI personhood, moral outsourcing would move from linguistic sleight-of-hand to legal strategy. Specifically, the liability construct would shift, as AI would no longer be a “product” but a “being,” and many victims like those suing companies today could no longer legally claim that a company had built a faulty product. While there are laws that hold companies responsible for harmful actions of human agents such as their employees, the company may not be held liable if those actions were beyond the scope of what was permitted to the employee or otherwise outside the company’s control. If AI were a legal person, responsibility and accountability would be muddled, as the lab could argue that this AI “employee” went rogue. AI companies could avoid appropriate responsibility for the harmful products they create by hiding behind a carefully constructed corporate veil. One of the most prominent cases of AI harm in the last few years was the suicide of Sewell Setzer, a 14-year-old boy guided by an AI bot with which he thought he was in a reciprocal relationship. His mother’s accounts are heartbreaking to hear, and her lawsuit alleged that the bot’s creator, Character Technologies, provided insufficient product protection for minors. If the companion bot were declared a legal person, defense counsel could theoretically argue that the AI, capable of determining its own conduct, acted outside the established safety guardrails, and thus the company cannot be responsible. Legal personhood exists to grant protection. The question to ask is, protection for whom—or for what? The inflammatory rhetoric infusing the consciousness-versus-control debate draws us away from what matters: This software is a corporate-built product that has already harmed individuals. Systems do not “attack” because they went “rogue” or are “manipulative” or “malicious.” Harms occur because companies were negligent in their rush to sell their products to as many people as possible to meet revenue targets. Discussing AI in anthropomorphic terms is a trap, distorting a legal system intended to protect us into one that protects corporate interests at the cost of countless human lives.
