Seagate's new hybrid drive raises some old questions

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-10

Visit Original Website
Seagate doesn’t think hybrid drives are done just yet. On Tuesday, the company announced the launch of the new FireCuda Forge line of storage drives. The new drives combine the high capacity of a traditional plattered HDD with the speed and responsiveness of a user-supplied solid-state drive (SSD). The new hybrid storage solution is Seagate’s first to be powered and managed by the new Alkemi intelligent storage platform. The FireCuda Forge is available right now through Seagate’s web store, and comes in 8TB ($699) or 20TB ($899) HDD configurations—neither of which includes the required SSD. Users will have to bring their own SSD, with some specific restrictions. The FireCuda Forge is compatible with M.2 2280 NVMe drives that are PCIe Gen3 or higher. The maximum supported NVMe capacity is 1TB, which means that’s as much data as you’ll be able to dedicate to storage on the “fast side” of the hybrid setup. The NVMe will require minimum speeds of 2,400MB/s read and 2,000MB/s write, and a unit with DRAM cache is recommended. Seagate claims 2,500MB/s read and 2,000MB/s write speeds for the combined system, along with USB4 and support for Windows 11 and macOS 26+. Credit: Seagate Credit: Seagate After dipping to historical lows earlier in the decade, storage pricing per TB has exploded amid the AI boom. According to data collected by Tom’s Hardware, some solid-state drives now cost two to three times their historic lows, and it’s not just SSDs. Mechanical drives haven’t been immune either. According to PCServerParts, a Seagate Exos 24TB drive that sold for $298.99 in the summer of 2025 was priced at $699.99 by August 2026. Hybrid drives had largely fallen out of fashion since their heyday in the early to mid 2010s. The promise then was similar to what Seagate is offering now. A large HDD is combined with a smaller SSD and handed to the user as a single storage pool. Apple introduced its own hybrid solution back in 2012 with the Mac mini’s Fusion Drive. When Ars opened up a unit, the Fusion Drive system was found to write in 128KB tiered blocks. Information was generally written to the SSD first, and was then migrated to the HDD as needed. If the files were frequently used, they might find a more permanent home on the SSD side of things. As solid-state storage continued getting cheaper, the hybrid approach was largely abandoned. Fast-forward to our current storage cost predicament in 2026, and Seagate’s line of thinking that everything old is new again starts to hold water. All storage costs have exploded, but the 2012 math still works today. NAND flash is still fast, but expensive, and traditional mechanical drives are at least comparatively cheaper per TB for higher capacity storage. The problem with Apple’s Fusion solution was its fragility. If one of the two drives failed, that was likely it. Recovery depended on both drives coming together as a single volume. If one of those two disks experienced a physical failure, recovery became difficult since the remaining working drive depended on the now broken drive’s data to rebuild the full system picture. According to Seagate’s press release, the Alkemi intelligent storage platform takes a different approach. Where Apple’s Fusion Drives used tiering managed by macOS, Alkemi manages the storage pool directly from the enclosure. Seagate says, “Alkemi learns and adapts over time to keep high-demand content fast and accessible on a user’s SSD, while the HDD provides space for growing libraries. Behind the scenes, Alkemi manages data placement, giving users the simplicity of a single storage drive.” The product data sheet PDF from the FireCuda Forge website describes the Alkemi system as a two-tier storage system, but doesn’t elaborate any further on whether the system uses SSD caching, automated tiering, a combination of the two, or something else entirely. The announcement doesn’t address how the Alkemi platform manages the data between the two drives. The old Intel Smart Response Technology kept failure risks low by mirroring data from the mechanical HDD to the faster SSD, with the HDD retaining the authoritative copy. The optional write-back mode could still hold new data on the SSD, though. Apple’s Fusion Drive instead tiered data in 128KB blocks across the storage pool, storing data on both drives. The distinction matters, and is probably why Ars asked the same question after Apple’s hybrid announcement 14 years ago. Seagate’s announcement speaks of “intelligent data tiering” without further explanation. The data system promises that your most accessed files will be quickest to access via intelligent storage management, but stops short of explaining whether one drive going down means failure for the whole system. There’s also no mention of how the Alkemi system distributes data on first boot. Does all the new written data go directly to the SSD until the 1TB cap is crossed, and then the system starts determining which files are important enough to remain on the speedier drive? Seagate seems to be keeping things mum for the moment, but as the drives have already launched for sale, there should be answers to all of these questions coming soon. Seagate hasn’t published technical documentation regarding how the Alkemi system handles drive failure or repair. Until it does, the new FireCuda Forge drives offer the same appeal as Apple’s old Fusion Drives: SSD speed with HDD capacity, along with the same questions Ars was asking about hybrid storage years ago.

The US military is taking a new approach to prop up commercial launch companies as SpaceX looks to wind down its Falcon rocket program, suggesting defense officials see the workhorse rocket’s retirement as a potential national security risk. The Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital has twice in the last two weeks announced it was sending cash to two of SpaceX’s competitors in the launch business. First, on October 26, the office announced a $150 million conditional loan commitment to Stoke Space. Military officials revealed a second $150 million Pentagon loan Tuesday for Rocket Lab. Stoke Space and Rocket Lab are the first two launch companies to receive significant support from the Office of Strategic Capital (OSC), an organization established in 2022 to infuse companies developing “vital” national security technologies with capital. OSC has been on a loan spree in recent weeks, committing hundreds of millions of dollars to companies specializing in batteries, drones, and semiconductors. In the space sector, the office also provided a $150 million loan to LeoLabs, a commercial satellite tracking company, for production and deployment of radar units. SpaceX, the Pentagon’s leading launch provider, has stopped taking commercial orders for Falcon 9 launches after 2028 as the company shifts its focus to the next-generation, super-heavy-lift Starship rocket. Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy launches for government customers, including NASA and the US Space Force, are expected to continue a while longer. The Space Force’s launch contract with SpaceX allows the military to place Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy orders through 2029, with the actual launches extending through 2033. Then, if Starship is fulfilling its promise, SpaceX plans to retire Falcon for good. Starship may eventually be capable of launching national security payloads, but SpaceX’s near-term priority is using the rocket’s massive cargo capacity for its own Starlink Internet satellites and Starmind orbital data centers. Military space officials have held to a policy of assured access to space since the 1990s, with the Pentagon paying to support at least two US rocket programs capable of launching national security satellites. The support previously came in the form of direct subsidies, development funding, and launch contracts at premium prices to give the government technical and financial insight into their launch providers, schedule priority on their launch manifests, and, in some cases, to simply keep them in business. The Space Force’s mix of launch providers today consists of SpaceX’s Falcon family and United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan rocket, which has been grounded twice in the last two years by problems with its solid rocket boosters. ULA’s woes with Vulcan forced the Space Force to shift several national security missions to SpaceX. Other launch providers aim to compete for their own slices of the growing pie of Space Force launch contracts. Space Systems Command, which oversees launch procurement for the Space Force, now forecasts the military will require up to 249 medium-to-heavy-lift launches through the early 2030s from its roster of launch contractors, more than double the command’s expectation just two years ago. Alongside SpaceX and ULA, Space Systems Command has tapped Blue Origin, Rocket Lab, Stoke Space, Relativity Space, and Impulse Space for eligibility to compete for some of those national security launches. Blue Origin is the only company that has flown a rocket that could meet the Space Force’s requirements. Rocket Lab, Stoke Space, and Relativity Space are developing their own medium- and heavy-lift launch vehicles, while Impulse Space is working on in-space propulsion. The loans from the Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital aim to get new medium-class rockets from Stoke Space and Rocket Lab ready to meet the military’s needs. Stoke Space’s loan is tailored to support the buildout of the company’s second launch pad at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, Florida. The second launch site would support flights of Stoke Space’s Nova Block 2 rocket, an upgraded version of the company’s fully reusable Nova Pathfinder, which is set to debut sometime next year. “By supporting the construction of additional launch infrastructure, this commitment would enhance the overall capacity and resilience of the commercial space industrial base,” said Jordan Sebold, senior managing director of investments at OSC. The loan to Rocket Lab will support the company’s efforts to increase the flight rate of its partially reusable Neutron rocket, which Rocket Lab says is also scheduled to make its first launch next year. “The proposed loan would support faster vehicle delivery and processing and allow Rocket Lab to expand its fleet of maritime vessels with additional equipment for Neutron recovery and transport,” OSC said in a statement. “It would also enable manufacturing upgrades to increase the production of composite structures and engines, and support infrastructure that would reduce turnaround time between launches once the reusable rocket makes its operational debut.” Both conditional loans are subject to “various financial, legal, technical, investment, due diligence, and other requirements,” OSC said. Adam Spice, Rocket Lab’s chief financial officer, said Monday the Pentagon loan is on “very attractive terms” and “underscores the importance” of the military’s appetite for launch capacity. “We always take it when we can get it,” Spice said in a quarterly earnings call. “It gives us additional capital to scale key parts of our business, Neutron in particular … I think this is a helpful indicator, a very positive indicator, about where everyone sees the demand constraint and how it can be unlocked, and capital is certainly a part of that.” Rocket Lab also unveiled new pricing for the Neutron rocket—an increase from $55 million to $70 million per launch—in response to what the company said was a surge in bookings for the still-unproven launch vehicle. Rocket Lab said it now has a backlog of 117 launches on contract, including orders for Neutron missions and the company’s smaller Electron rocket. “Launch is extremely constrained, and that is exactly the situation we predicted,” said Peter Beck, Rocket Lab’s founder and CEO.

Grok Build, the vibe-coding agent from SpaceXSI, is leaking data—some of it proprietary business information and other sensitive secrets—from thousands of its users, including the very same Elon Musk-owned company that offers the platform, new research shows. The information is revealed through links allowing Grok Build users to share their apps with others. Grok takes steps to randomize the URLs so only people who know the exact URL can access the data stored there. A design decision by developers of the platform, however, completely torpedoes that objective and makes it possible for anyone to enumerate thousands of live Grok Build projects. Except in rare cases, clicking those links opens the app and reveals all data associated with it. Research from security firm Claroc shows that the exposed data includes things like internal business plans, ad proposals, medical exam results, galley checklists from a celebrity cruise line, and employee names and ID numbers. Ironically, some of the sensitive data comes from two-dozen apps built by SpaceXSI (the name of the now-merged SpaceX and xAI) itself. A small sampling of the documents revealed in the SpaceXSI apps—Claroc found 24 of them—include an onboarding plan, marked confidential, for a new member of SpaceXSI’s enterprise sales team, a sales territory map of 594 accounts, internal system addresses for sales team tools, and advertising proposals. “We were shocked,” Claroc said in a Tuesday post. “The company that makes Grok had its own internal material open to anyone holding the link, and we had never been given it. Had the sales team bought a tool for this, it would have sat behind company sign-in, open only to the team. Built with AI, it went live open to anyone with the link. Claroc notified SpaceXSI of the privacy spill on Sunday. When no one responded, a Claroc official took to X to tag both Musk and Grok Manager Matt Palmer about the threat. At the time this post went live almost all data remained available with one notable exception: the contents from the 24 SpaceXSI apps. All of them were exposing the information on Monday evening. SpaceXSI began removing the links within a few hours of Ars sending an email with questions for this article. At the time this post went live, links for all 24 apps returned an “app not found” error. Claroc found sensitive data belonging to other Grok Build users, some working for Fortune 500 companies and a defense contractor. Some of the affected organizations and data revealed includes: Asaf Aprozper, the co-founder and CTO of Claroc, said he’s in the process of notifying companies. After speaking to the CISO of the luxury cruise company, it “pulled in their whole security team as soon as we shared the findings and are already working to contain the Grok exposure.” Link sharing for Grok Build is intended to streamline the process of developing and deploying apps. A user can input speech or text and have a working app ready for internal testing within minutes. The links are designed to give developers a way to get the app in front of authorized users quickly. The very left of the URLs contain two or more “dice words,” meaning randomly generated phrases such as “failing chassis traders,” although users can modify them. The rightmost portion of the link is grok.me. The URL for an X ads campaign plan was: https://cinder-crane-sand-frost.grok.me. The scheme is intended to prevent links from being easily guessable. When done correctly, a phrase comprising three dice words has roughly 38 bits of entropy, amounting to billions of possible combinations. The idea—sometimes called capability-based access control—is that possession of the URL serves as authorization to the resources behind it. Google has long used this technique to make URLs easy to share but hard to guess. A URL share on that platform might look like: https://docs.google.com/document/d/1BxiMVs0XRA5nFMdKvBdBZjgmUUqptlbs74OgvE2upms/edit There’s a fatal flaw in the way SpaceXSI has implemented the mechanism. Instead of being to the right of the domain—as is the case with Google links—the randomness for Grok Build links is positioned to the left of it. That allows the links to be easily enumerated using passive DNS services. These services monitor central DNS lookup servers. When a server lookup requests the IP address of a previously never-before-seen URL, the transaction is captured in a publicly searchable database. Had Grok followed Google’s approach, the links would have been invisible to passive DNS searchers. Muse, the Meta agent that CEO Mark Zuckerberg said was built from the ground up for security, recently suspended a link-sharing feature after it too was found leaking sensitive data. Aprozper said a passive DNS search returned 43,601 links ending in grok.me. Of them, more than 35,000, or nearly 95 percent, were public, meaning that clicking on the link opened the corresponding app and all data it stored. He said that tests he conducted over the past week showed that, by default, Grok sets links to “Anyone with the link.” Users have the option to change the setting to “invited users” or “only you.” A representative with Cursor, the AI coding service SpaceXSI recently acquired, said in an email that Grok Build “apps are private by default and nothing goes public unless the creator chooses to share it.” The representative said that competing coding agents from ChatGPT, Lovable, and Bolt also give users the ability to generate shareable links. Aprozper’s tests contradict the representative’s statement. In all cases, Grok Build required users to change anyone with the link to one of the other two options. The Cursor statement regarding other AI coding platforms is correct. Aprozper said that app builders, including Lovable, Bolt, and Replit, “also give each app its own web address, so the same DNS records expose them, too. However, Grok is where we found by far the most sensitive company data.” That means that people using these other platforms are also at risk of exposing sensitive data through shared links. Interestingly, AI platforms that follow the correct sharing link practice include Claude’s published artifacts (claude.ai/public/artifacts/), ChatGPT’s shared conversations (chatgpt.com/share/), and Gemini (gemini.google.com/share/). For all three, the secret randomness is in the path, so DNS never sees it. The representative didn’t respond to follow-up questions, including if SpaceXSI intentionally exposed the proprietary internal information, why the company removed the data after Ars asked about it, and if it planned to notify customers who also exposed similar types of information. Anyone using shareable links with Grok Build should choose the “invite others” option unless they intend to make an app widely available.

Our modern world runs on math, from social media algorithms, package delivery, and weather prediction to medical imaging and machine learning. The rise of computers helped fuel its dominance, enabling us to work with increasingly large numbers that would otherwise be beyond the capacity of our finite human brains. But with the rise of large language models, AI chatbots, and, soon, quantum computing, so-called “big math” is beginning to outstrip its creators entirely. What that means, and how we might adapt, is the focus of a new book, Big Math: The Hidden Codes That Run Our World, by applied mathematicians Steven Strogatz and Alex Townsend. Strogatz is perhaps best known in his field for his co-authorship of a seminal 1998 paper with Duncan Watts on the collective dynamics of small-world networks. He has also studied chaos theory as applied to physics, the geometry of supercoiled DNA, and the biological collective oscillators, such as swarms of synchronously flashing fireflies and crickets that chirp in unison. In 2005, Strogatz extended his study of synchronicity to the crowds that caused the wobbling of London’s Millennium Bridge. An award-winning communicator, Strogatz wrote three series of columns on mathematics for The New York Times in 2010, 2012, and 2025 and hosted a podcast for Quanta magazine from 2020–2021. He has written four popular books: Sync (2003), The Calculus of Friendship (2009), The Joy of X (2012), and the New York Times bestseller Infinite Powers (2019). This time around, Strogatz has found a well-matched co-author in Townsend, a younger colleague recently hired at Cornell who studies numerical analysis and deep learning. As senior faculty, Strogatz was assigned to mentor Townsend, and the two men began regularly lunching together in Risley Hall. Strogatz was intrigued by the popular book potential of Townsend’s course on the top 20 algorithms of the 20th century and suggested co-authorship. That concept slowly morphed into a book about big matrix calculations, and then into what became Big Math. “There are so many books about pure math, as if computers didn’t exist,” Strogatz told Ars. “Alex and I are both very applied mathematicians. We thought there should be a book on computers and math, especially the class of really big problems with billions of variables or parameters that you just can’t approach in your head. A lot of the math we’ve had for centuries perfectly scales up to this computer moment. What the abacus did for arithmetic, the fangcheng (“rectangular array”) board did for systems of linear equations. It was a matrix you could manipulate with your hand and bamboo sticks. So this externalizing of math outside of our heads into our tools is a 2,000-year-old idea.” “The mathematician in each of us will always want to know more,” Strogatz and Townsend wrote. “The human being must insist that knowledge serves something larger than itself. That might be the work of our century: letting our power grow without losing our soul. Big Math keeps getting bigger. So must we.” Ars caught up with Strogatz to learn more. Ars Technica: Let’s start by defining “big math,” because it’s such a broad umbrella term. Steven Strogatz: Operationally, big math is “big” relative to human cognitive capacity. So it can be surprisingly small and yet big. We also wanted to expand it to things that people would think of as computational engineering—in the building of the Burj Khalifa, that’d be civil engineering or design architecture. It’s there in medical imaging. It’s in getting us to the Moon. The lunar module was controlled by a tiny computer that only had to think about 20 or 30 variables at a time to steer the LEM down to the Moon’s surface. But it’s not something a person could do in their head. Big Math is not simply big in the ordinary sense of big numbers. There’s an undercurrent in the book about the importance of linearity and sparsity. When problems are linear, they can be scaled up to immense size, and they stay tractable. If they’re non-linear, you don’t have any hope of dealing with them with pure math. You can do them with computers. But for the people who want rigorous theorems, we’re in terra incognita when we deal with anything that’s non-linear and big. Small math can be very hard. Historically, we had small math problems with one or two variables, arithmetic, algebra that you learn in high school. Even to this day, a lot of pure math is relatively small. It doesn’t concern itself with enormous numbers of variables simultaneously because it’s totally intractable. Cryptography can be small, but it’s so complicated with its non-linearities and other algebraic difficulties. So big is one way to be difficult, but there are a lot of other ways. Nowadays, trillions of parameters are typical in the biggest of the large language models. But infinity is not big from this point. Infinity, which you would think of would be the biggest of big math, is not because calculus fits perfectly nicely in a human head. Infinity turns out to be very tractable in a way that a billion is not. Ars: One of your real-world examples of big math is particularly relevant at the moment: mathematics as it relates to the gerrymandering issue. Strogatz: It’s an interesting question of, what’s a fair map and what’s a reasonable redistricting? The legal system gives us certain criteria, but they are vague. The districts should not be too snakey-looking, not look like salamanders, for instance. The term in the law is ‘compact.’ They should be reasonably compact. But what does that mean mathematically? The districts are also supposed to be contiguous; you don’t want to have separate islands and call that one district. It’s supposed to respect natural boundaries like rivers or other landscape features. Also, each district is supposed to have roughly the same population. So how to make maps that respect all of the criteria? It turns out there are a lot of ways to make them. Using past voting information, you can stack the deck. That’s what gerrymandering is all about, trying to redistrict in favor of whatever party is in power. There has been a big debate about whether certain maps violate people’s rights. The principle should be one person, one vote. If you do things that dilute somebody’s power so that their votes are wasted, so to speak, that’s traditionally thought to be unfair or maybe even illegal. The Supreme Court has heard all kinds of cases, but the court is very resistant to the use of math. I quoted [Chief Justice] John Roberts talking about the efficiency gap as one measure of gerrymandering. He referred to it by its Greek letters and said something like, “The average person on the street is just going to think this is gobbledygook, that this is not a criterion that the law should pay any attention to.” Nevertheless, Moon Duchin and her colleagues showed this very down-to-earth way of generating a big ensemble of reasonable maps that satisfy all the legal criteria so that you have a baseline, you could do the control experiment. What the math does is give you a principled way of generating maps at random that respect the criteria that the law requires, but otherwise are just a random sample of reasonable maps. How often would a map produce the result that the gerrymander intended? If you can show that it’s an extreme outlier, that’s a measure of something. Does it prove malicious intent? Not really. It does prove it’s not typical. There was an Alabama voting rights case where Roberts actually voted with the plaintiffs, who said that there was real harm being done to Black voters in Alabama with one of Alabama’s maps. The state was claiming there’s no way we could get a reasonable map to have two districts with Black majority voters. Moon Duchin showed that was wrong. You could generate thousands of maps that would do that. So it just wasn’t true what the state had claimed. After the book was copyedited and ready to go to press, a Supreme Court decision was made that, in fact, you can partisan gerrymander as much as you want. It doesn’t violate the Voting Rights Act. We wrote a more optimistic story. Ars: How has math changed over time from a philosophical standpoint? Strogatz: I think a crisp way to encapsulate it is the role of the computer. For a long time, a computer was a servant to mathematicians and engineers and scientists. It was no more philosophically threatening than a microscope or a telescope. It could extend our senses. It’s a chisel, it’s an anvil, it’s a tool. It helped us do calculations faster; things that would be too hard for people to do at scale you could do on a machine. We had mechanical calculators. They became electronic computers in the 1950s with the invention of the transistor and got much faster to give us today’s supercomputers. We program them; they do exactly what we tell them. So they’re servants. The first philosophically big change is in the 1950s, when computers start to be used as more than servants; they become scouts. You could send the computer out into unknown, unexplored territory in the mathematical landscape. The clearest example was work by Enrico Fermi and his colleagues (Pasta, Ulam, and Tsingou) that we wrote about in the chapter on simulation. Mary Tsingou was the one who did the calculations in what was traditionally called the Fermi-Pasta-Ulam numerical experiment. What distinguished it from earlier uses of computers is that Fermi and colleagues had no idea what the answer was going to be. It’s one thing to use a computer to calculate the power released by an atomic bomb blast or to calculate an artillery shell’s trajectory. We didn’t know the exact numerical values, but we knew what was happening. With this experiment, they were trying to simulate a simplified model of how atoms could, in the jargon, formalize as chemical bonds jiggle. You want to see the decay to entropy. How do systems degenerate to greater and greater disorder if they’re just left to their own devices? Fermi thought that the non-linear aspect of chemical bonds—meaning if you’d stretch a chemical bond by twice as much, it doesn’t pull back exactly twice as hard, so it’s not strictly proportional—might account for the so-called second law of thermodynamics, the decay to entropy. Pencil and paper math was not up to the job because you couldn’t calculate these non-linear interactions far enough to see the decay into entropy. So they ran it in the computer, and a shocking thing happened: It didn’t decay. It showed a strange echo. You started all the energy in one of the modes of vibration of this system, and then it started to degenerate. But after a while, 98 percent of the energy came back into the original mode. That was the echo that they called a recurrence phenomenon. It led us to a whole new branch of math that we call soliton theory. It was an early indication that once you walk into the nonlinear jungle, human intuition breaks down. We couldn’t walk there, but we sent our scout, this computer probe, into the wilderness of the mind, and it was the only thing capable of doing the calculations and reporting back to us what it found. Ars: The rapid rise of AI in mathematics has alarmed many in the field, particularly in light of OpenAI’s release last month of 722 AI-generated math papers, dubbed the “mathocalypse.” Technically, AI is also a new tool, but it seems fundamentally different. Strogatz: So from servant to scout was a new thing, and computing was our partner. At first, it was all very thrilling. It was like having a new Ferrari or something. You could race ahead and then discover things. That’s what Fermi and Ulam said at the time. That was truly amazing. They didn’t know what to expect. The computer was now teaching us by showing us things, but we could still keep up. We could develop these new theories like soliton theory, so we could rationalize what it was showing us. What’s different now is that it’s going from servant to scout to oracle, where it’s showing us things that we don’t understand and it’s not articulate enough to explain itself. It seems to be right. A clear example would be something like Google Translate, where it’s fantastic at translating one language into another, but it has not discovered new principles of linguistics, or if it has, it can’t tell us what they are. Likewise, protein folding with AlphaFold that won the Nobel Prize in chemistry a couple of years ago for the people who built it. We now can solve a thousand times more proteins than we had before, but we have not done it by learning new physics or chemistry. It just recognized patterns, but it can’t tell us how it’s doing it. So that’s where we are, and it’s going to get worse. Ars: In your book, you ask if insight still matters, citing Stephen Wolfram as an example of someone who might answer that question in the negative. Strogatz: Wolfram’s position is tricky. He pushes computational irreducibility. I love these two words, perspicuous and perspicacious. Systems can be perspicuous; you can see into them. Science has, for thousands of years, concerned itself with systems that are perspicuous, meaning you can get insight into them because you’re so perspicacious. But the question from Wolfram would be, have we been the famous drunk that’s looking under the lamppost for his keys because it’s dark everywhere else? You wouldn’t have any hope of finding them except under the lamppost. So is it that we’ve artificially restricted ourself to systems that are transparent enough that we can develop science that our insight illuminates? I think Wolfram would claim that most of nature is computationally irreducible in the sense that there is no simple insightful theory into what’s going on. The best you can do is just run a simulation and watch what happens, similar to just letting nature unfold and watch what happens in reality. In the jargon people use nowadays, you shouldn’t expect a compressed description, a simplified model that will account for what you’re seeing. We have seen those. Newton could explain the motion of the planets from a few simple principles and that worked, but that’s, I think in Wolfram’s take, artificially simple. Most of science is not going to end up being like that. We don’t know if Wolfram is really right about that. Wolfram, I think, is demonstrably right that many computer simulations are computationally irreducible, but nature might not be. Ars: If you can’t see into the models, and they can’t explain what they’re doing, how do you know they’re right? Strogatz: Let’s stipulate that we don’t understand them, and that they are not at the present able to explain themselves to us. Let’s put a little asterisk next to that part, though, because it could be that they’ll become very good teachers. Or maybe we don’t understand Google Translate or AlphaFold right now, but we will in 10 years. I do believe that there will be systems that are truly opaque because of human limits, that we just won’t be fast enough or smart enough or have enough memory. AIs will show us something that we just won’t be able to understand even with their help. What happens then? Then I think math starts to be more like science or medicine, or engineering. At the FDA, for a new drug to be approved, it has to be shown to be safe by doing a lot of controlled studies and randomized trials. There’s a whole methodology for opacity that people in medicine have worked out. But the doctors do not require knowledge of the mechanism of action. You don’t have to know how aspirin works. You don’t have to know how electroconvulsive therapy works for people with intractable depression. Even if we don’t understand, we can still live with these technologies or drugs by these methods. You don’t have to have first principles understanding. If the AI is an oracle, you can still check it by seeing how often it is right. Strogatz: It is a loss. I don’t want to pooh-pooh the severity of the—to use a fancy philosophical word—epistemic loss. What we have prided ourselves on in pure math for so long is that we’re the only subject that has absolute proof. We have certainty. We’re as close to certainty as anything that humanity can do. We can write a proof. Euclid’s proofs are still correct today, even though he wrote them in 300 BC. There’s nothing in the science or medicine of 300 BC that is still accepted, but all of the math is still true. So there is something eternal and very special about math. Even though we’re just primates, somehow we’re tapping into something really deep. And so to say, “Well, science and engineering are the models for math,” would be a big sacrifice to the purest of pure mathematicians. They don’t want to give up the certainty that we’ve always been so proud of. Ars: There’s a sadness among many mathematicians, not just for the epistemic questions, but also for the impact on why we do math in the first place. You write about that “aha!” moment when you’re struggling with a problem and suddenly you see the pattern. Are we losing that? Strogatz: There’s so many things that mathematicians are in grief about, those of us who feel grief. I have been through the whole sequence of the five stages of grief. I’m joking with you a little. But the young students don’t think this is funny. I’m an old person at the end of my career, so it’s one thing for me to be philosophical about it. But imagine being 25 years old and you love this subject and you’ve dreamt about being a mathematician, and now people are saying the subject is over. There are even people gloating about it. Some of the people in the tech community are very cruel about what’s happening to math. We talked about the epistemic aspect, but there’s also the culture of productive struggle. The pure math people like Terry Tao have been emphasizing understanding. That’s our idea of insight, that when you try hard to understand something, even if you don’t solve it, you often are so resourceful in thinking of strategies or other simplified versions of the problem. You learn a lot on the journey. You also have all this companionship and human joy of being with other people who love the thing you love. This is not restricted to math. The people who love art or weaving—my colleagues in creative writing are bereft over what their students are experiencing now, handing in AI [generated work]. They want writing with soul, with human individuality. Ars: That is the opposite of what AI does. I guess I worry that it will do the same thing to math. There is a beauty to math, an aesthetic element. If all you’re focused on is getting the answers and ignore the journey, those are qualitative losses. Mathematicians have individual voices, too. Strogatz: Totally. There are people whose style is very visible in the questions they ask, in the approach they take to them, in the way they explain themselves. I have cried on camera for several interviews over the past few months because there are lots of things maybe being lost. But I want to put an underline on the fact that we don’t really know. For example, what if AI gets really good at teaching us? I find when I interact with it, that it can be a very effective explainer. And why do we have to feel sad? Maybe we can understand math better than ever with the help of this machine. We may not get to make the greatest discoveries because the machine will probably be making them, but we can still make little discoveries. I never made a great discovery. I’m not at the level of a Fields medalist, but I can still enjoy math and so can people way below me. There are recreational mathematicians who take pleasure in discovery, whatever they figure out on their own. Just like when a person solves a puzzle, it’s not like a jigsaw puzzle or Sudoku is important. You just have the pleasure of thinking. There are people who love the workout part of it. There are people who like the competition. We shouldn’t pretend. There are people who are thrilled by the idea of being the first to make a big discovery. That’s animating for some folks, and it will be hard for them. Strogatz: So far the AIs are not good at asking questions. I think they could be trained to ask good questions. A graduate student is often not good at asking questions when they start because in college you’re not taught to ask questions, you’re taught to give answers. The hardest thing in training a new PhD or postdoc is helping them figure out how to ask questions. I think we could do that with AIs, too. There is a feeling of loss, but I don’t think we should shortchange the feeling of excitement. There’s so many practical, important benefits that could happen in energy technology and climate and medicine, theoretical physics, pure math. I mean, we’re making astonishing discoveries through this machine. So for a lot of people, it’s a very joyful time. It’s very confusing. That’s the only honest assessment. We end the book with Faust. Usually people hear “Faustian bargain” as, “You have sold your soul to the devil, now you’re going to pay.” Goethe’s version is more ambiguous: that if we keep striving to build something bigger than ourselves, maybe we can be redeemed. But it’s still a very dangerous game that we have chosen to play.

Each reactor will be capable of generating 80 megawatts of electricity and 200 megawatts of heat. X-energy says the approval is the first for an advanced nuclear reactor at an industrial site in the United States. The U.S. nuclear industry has pinned its hopes on small modular reactors — or SMRs — like those being developed by X-energy. No SMR is currently in operation in the U.S., and X-energy says Dow’s reactors won’t be operational until the early 2030s. X-energy claims that its reactor design, which flows helium gas over uranium-containing fuel pellets, is safer than many existing reactors, but the Union of Concerned Scientists questioned the claim and the speed with which X-energy received the permit. “The NRC only accomplished this feat by pushing consideration of critical safety questions to a later date—issues the NRC should’ve fully resolved before green lighting construction,” Edwin Lyman, director of nuclear power safety for the organization, said in a statement. The nuclear industry has benefited from strong interest from hyperscalers and data center developers, who are desperate for any and all sources of electricity. X-energy rode that wave of excitement earlier this year when its April initial public offering raised $1 billion. But since then, investors’ optimism has tempered. The hesitancy isn’t surprising: Nuclear power plants are expensive to build and can take years to complete. Eventually, X-energy hopes to rein in both time and cost, but its first reactors are likely to be pricey. With Herzog’s nod, Eisenkot was given 28 days, through Dec. 6, with a possible 14-day extension, to assemble a government that can win the confidence of the Knesset, Israel’s parliament. Once he notifies the President and the Knesset that he has formed a government, the lawmakers must meet within seven days for a confidence vote. The government takes office if it wins that vote. First, Eisenkot would need to bring together rival political parties with disparate policy views and governing priorities in order to form a voting majority. By joining his coalition, parties are effectively endorsing Eisenkot as the next Prime Minister. But he has several obstacles to overcome before he can get the number of seats that he needs. Here’s everything to know about the process Israel will undertake in its efforts to name its next Prime Minister. In last month’s election, no party bloc secured the 61 seats needed to form a majority in the 120-seat parliament. This means that little has been decided about future leadership. But the process is still in motion. Knesset elections are scheduled to occur every four years, with the most recent taking place Oct. 27. Voters must be physically present on Election Day, with extremely rare absentee exceptions, and it is a public holiday. According to The Atlantic, an estimated 67,000 Israeli citizens living outside of the country traveled back this year in order to cast their ballots. Israeli citizens vote for the political parties that they want to govern—not specific candidates. Based on the percentage of votes cast, each party is then allotted a certain number of representatives in the Knesset. Despite Netanyahu's poor approval rating, his Likud party and its far-right and ultra-Orthodox allies, such as Otzma Yehudit (Jewish Power), secured the most votes and won 53 seats. Two Arab-led parties hold the remaining 19—a record high. “If you just count the number of votes for each party, Netanyahu's party got the most votes, but because it's a parliamentary system, and you need a simple majority to form a government, he was far away from actually establishing a government,” Steven Cook, a senior fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations, tells TIME. To form a government, the parties must now negotiate an alliance that can secure enough support to win a confidence vote in the Knesset. Typically, this requires meeting or exceeding 61 seats, but it can also be fewer if some parties abstain. Either Netanyahu’s bloc or Eisenkot’s bloc could reach a majority by joining forces with both Arab-led parties, giving them 72 or 67 seats, respectively. Alternatively, the two rival blocs would hold 101 seats if they joined together—but any such agreement would require negotiations over who would serve as Prime Minister. The first point to note about these articles is that they are a notable departure from either of the two prior cases in U.S. history in which a Cabinet officer has been impeached. President Ulysses S. Grant’s secretary of war, William Belknap, was impeached for taking bribes—an ordinary tawdry case of personal corruption. President Joe Biden’s secretary of Homeland Security, Alejandro Mayorkas, was impeached (though the case was promptly dismissed by the Senate) essentially for competently carrying out Biden’s immigration policy. However ill-advised that policy may have been, it was not unconstitutional or in violation of any statute (though Republicans tried to claim otherwise). As I and others argued at the time, the consensus among American students of impeachment (and indeed among legislators for generations past) has been that subordinate executive officers do not commit impeachable “high crimes and misdemeanors” merely for executing otherwise constitutional and statutorily legal policies with which a majority of Congress happens to disagree. In that sense, American impeachments have never been employed as the equivalent of the motions of no-confidence employed by parliamentary governments to remove government ministers. A critical difference between these cases and Massie’s charges against Hegseth is that the congressman is careful to articulate exactly how Hegseth’s actions violate the law—either particular provisions of the Constitution or identified statutes, regulations, or customary governmental procedures and norms. Should very many Republicans vote against Hegseth, they would be following the path forged by the English Parliament when it invented impeachment in the 1300s largely as a means of reining in royal overreach by striking down the royal servants who were implementing the monarch’s most disagreeable policies. Doing so would be a departure from American impeachment practice. On this side of the Atlantic we have not hitherto chosen to use impeachment this way, preferring instead to control erring presidents with legislative authorities like the power of the purse or, failing that, by evicting them by election or attempting to impeach them directly. However, there would be nothing anti-constitutional about impeaching subordinate officers to control the president, so long as the subordinate was carrying out illegal or unconstitutional directives of the president that could fairly be characterized as “high crimes and misdemeanors.” Indeed, there is much to be said for congressional adoption of what might be seen as an intermediate remedy against presidential overreach that does not require the far more wrenching choice to condemn and expel the chief executive himself. The advantages of using impeachment in this way are particularly evident in the case of presidential abuse of war powers. Recall that the War Powers Act was adopted in the wake of the Vietnam War as a response to the fact that successive presidents—John F. Kennedy, Lyndon Johnson, and Richard Nixon—embroiled the country in a bloody foreign war and then pursued the conflict for years despite no explicit congressional sanction and rising public displeasure. The act sought to balance the need for congressional approval of the grave choice to send Americans to war with the exigencies of the modern era in which genuine military challenges might arise and require response before Congress could assemble to judge the matter. The act’s provisions are complicated, but in essence they enjoin the president from involving the military in hostilities without either A) a declaration of war or other expression of congressional approval or B) a national emergency. If the president does send American forces into combat without prior congressional approval, the act requires that he notify Congress within 48 hours and that hostilities be terminated within 60 days (with a possible 30-day extension) unless Congress acts to approve their continuation. Moreover, the act provides that Congress can force termination of American engagement at any time by concurrent resolution of both houses. Congressman Massie’s articles of impeachment allege that Hegseth (and Trump) carried out the Iran war, the operations in Yemen, and the military incursion into Venezuela to kidnap its president in repeated violation of the War Powers Act. Withdrawing funding has been thought particularly politically risky insofar as it can be characterized as refusing to support or protect troops actively engaged in an ongoing fight. But in the Iran case, Congress did summon the resolve to vote disapproval. Yet Trump (and Hegseth) has simply ignored congressional resolutions commanding him to stop. The metals industry has experienced transformational moments before: the Bessemer process, flash smelting, electric arc furnaces, and modern continuous casting all changed what was possible. Yet, for more than a century, most extraction processes rely on techniques and pathways that would be familiar to metallurgists from generations ago. Processes have become larger and control systems smarter, but the fundamental science behind separating metals from ores has changed less than many realize. Today’s materials challenges are different from those of the 20th century, but why are critical metals still produced using largely the same scientific foundations? EvoMaterials, a technology platform developed by Outokumpu—the global leader in sustainable stainless steel and the producer of ferrochrome with 67% lower carbon footprint than the industry average—is exploring that question by rethinking how metals can be extracted: “We decided to go beyond the current constraints and look fundamentally at what’s the best way to do this with the benefit of the scientific engineering knowledge and hindsight that we have today,” says Caspar Stinn, technical director for extractive metallurgy at Outokumpu. “We’re looking at how we can reimagine metals production.” As economies expand and the world becomes increasingly electrified, data and scientific models point to rising demand for many minerals, driven by technologies ranging from data centers to aerospace, water treatment, energy systems, and defence. According to Antoine Allanore, professor of metallurgy at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) this coincides with many Western economies having reduced their domestic processing capacity for the very metals those industries depend on. Metallurgy is now strategically important, shaped not only by cost and availability but also by carbon footprint, geographic origin and supply-chain resilience. That reality is particularly relevant for chromium and other specialty metals used in high-temperature and extreme environments. Chromium-based materials play a critical role in aerospace systems, power generation, corrosion-resistant infrastructure and defense technologies, where materials must perform under demanding conditions. For decades, metallurgical innovation largely focused on improving yields, energy efficiency and process control. EvoMaterials is based on the idea that some metallurgical processes may benefit from entirely different chemical pathways. The platform originates from research into sulfidation-based processing technologies, initially pursued to reduce carbon emissions associated with ferrochrome production. Laboratory work revealed broader potential. Lab-scale trials demonstrated that these alternative process chemistries could potentially do more than lower emissions. They could recover valuable metals from lower grade resources and industrial side streams while fundamentally altering how separation processes are performed. The opportunity is to rethink how mineral resources are used and how much value can be extracted from them, bringing in greater resource efficiency and stronger industrial competitiveness. Development of the technology began in the United States in 2021 for chromite from Outokumpu’s Kemi mine in Finland, the only chrome mine in the European Union and one of the few such operations across the Western world. Since then, Outokumpu has evolved the technology through extensive research and development, combining new process chemistries with proprietary metallurgical expertise, supported in part by a non-exclusive patent license agreement from MIT. “The technology is a new application for separating metallic elements from materials,” explains Allanore. Historically, that separation has been achieved using carbon- and energy-intensive approaches. The new method creates a different chemical environment that enables more efficient extraction with fewer losses and fewer unwanted by-products, recovering critical materials from resources considered waste. EvoMaterials sits at the intersection of several scientific disciplines, a combination that strongly resonates with Stinn, who completed his PhD in Materials Science and Engineering at MIT, where his research focused on low-carbon metals processing and recycling technologies. Modern materials innovation draws on a growing mix of chemistry, materials science, physics, computational modeling, and engineering. With advanced simulation tools and AI helping researchers identify promising directions, scientific discovery still depends on experimentation and testing in the lab—on getting your hands dirty and doing the hard work. “Science today is much more interdisciplinary than it was, and the collaboration across disciplines reveals new scientific opportunities,” says Stinn. Engineering can translate those opportunities into more efficient and cost-competitive metals production. A technically significant aspect of EvoMaterials is that it’s not a single process, but a technology platform. Allanore compares EvoMaterials to platform technologies in other industries, where a single scientific breakthrough can enable multiple downstream applications. Once a new extraction pathway has been mastered for one material system, the same scientific principles may be adapted to entirely different metals and mineral resources. The initial application becomes only the starting point. “The future and the potential impact might be much broader than the original and current developments that are happening,” Allanore explains. That possibility is increasingly important as industries seek secure supplies of nickel, chromium, molybdenum, and other strategic materials needed for advanced manufacturing. The broader implication extends beyond metallurgy. Companies that develop proprietary process technologies may create value through production but also through intellectual property and scientific expertise. Based on Outokumpu’s technology leadership, that transition could create entirely new categories of value, enabling the production of high-value critical materials from ores and resource streams that previously appeared uneconomical or inaccessible. The result would be a fundamental shift in how metals are manufactured, where sustainability, resource efficiency, supply security, and economic value become outcomes of the same technological architecture rather than separate objectives. The work raises a broader question—how many of today’s metallurgical processes would look the same if they were invented from scratch? Or could those routes be designed differently today? As voters went to the polls across the US last week, I was in Ashburn, Virginia, part of what’s known as Data Center Alley—the world’s largest data center hub. It’s also where the backlash now sweeping the country arguably first began taking shape. Driving through it is surreal. Concrete monolithic buildings loom along every major road, with names like Skybox and CloudHQ. It’s accepted wisdom in Big Tech that the war against data centers must be about something bigger than just data centers themselves. Two days after the midterms, OpenAI’s chief global affairs officer, Chris Lehane, wrote that data centers “have become a proxy for public unease about affordability, corporate power, and people losing control over their futures.” It’s a culture war, in other words. But spending a few days in Data Center Alley left me wondering if it’s really the proxy that Lehane describes. I came away convinced that the data center backlash is more about electricity than Big Tech admits. The industry can improve its image, but it can’t escape the physical consequences of its enormous appetite for electricity. Here’s why. There are two ways to power the sort of data center that AI companies want: connecting to the grid, or generating electricity onsite. In Virginia’s Loudoun County, where data centers are ubiquitous but have been built at a steadier pace than the breakneck expansion now underway elsewhere in the country, most are connected to the existing grid. One facility I went inside, operated by Equinix, has massive diesel generators onsite for backup but gets its power from the local utility, Dominion Energy. Much of Dominion’s electricity is generated by natural gas (which is cleaner than coal or oil, but worse than renewables). The average data center operating in this part of Virginia today has a power capacity of 54 megawatts. That’s more than the power draw of the Denver International Airport. Elena Schlossberg, a prominent activist against data centers in Virginia, told me she’s “always banging my head against the wall” when the industry paints the fight as a culture war. “It was always about power,” she says—that is, electricity. Some Virginia protesters appear more animated by a broader anti-tech populism than Schlossberg, to be fair. But it’s the particular consequences of drawing so much power from the grid that have done the most to shape how the backlash has played out. Recent protests have led to new rules from the state that data centers must pay fully for the cost of new transmission lines to the grid and make sure they’re not spread among everyone else. Data center developers told me they’re preparing for new rules that would cut them off from grid power during peak demand. Schlossberg also worries about the downstream effects of a strained grid—for example, that data centers will run more diesel generators or that Dominion Energy will expand fossil fuel inputs rather than renewables to meet demand. The overwhelming majority of AI data centers being built elsewhere in the country are not designed this way. The scale desired is so massive that companies are setting up in rural areas where there is virtually no grid infrastructure to connect to. Building there means bringing your own power (so-called “behind the meter” power), in the form of natural-gas-powered turbines. Companies also build diesel generators for backup. Anthropic, for example, has reportedly agreed to pay $3.4 billion for gas turbines in what’s described as the largest power generation purchase by an AI company. Even if grids were nearby, they’d be unlikely to serve the amount of power demanded. The smallest data centers attractive for AI training are 300 megawatts. It’s a lot of power for one industrial site, but not unprecedented: One data center developer told me he’s eyeing a former steel mill already equipped to draw that much electricity from the grid. But the ideal facilities are more like 6,000 megawatts. That’s the estimated draw of the city of Chicago, which is powered by more than 20 power plants within the city alone (and dozens farther out). The notion of data center companies’ bringing their own power might seem attractive at first, because they wouldn’t be straining the grid. But generating electricity at this scale inevitably affects surrounding communities, and when public utilities build infrastructure of this magnitude, communities have traditionally expected a say in where it goes. Now, private companies are undertaking projects on a similar scale. Natural-gas turbines of any scale release air pollutants. When diesel generators are used, it’s even worse. And in the unprecedented rush to get these facilities operating, companies have become horrible neighbors (recall that Elon Musk violated air quality laws when he set up dozens of turbines to power a data center in Memphis, according to the Environmental Protection Agency). Big Tech’s tendency to chalk the backlash up to a culture war obfuscates one simple fact: There’s only so pleasant you can make a small city of computers to live around. Sure, companies can pursue better deals that raise tax revenues, hire local workers, and get more community involvement, as the protests are leading them to do. But if a small town is asked to welcome a Walt Disney World, an international airport, a steel plant, and the power plants to run them all, should we be surprised by its trepidation? Again, that’s not to say this is the sole reason for opposition to data centers. Fears of job losses, existential risks, and concentrated corporate power all fuel the backlash, as does misinformation (most facilities use far less water than many people think). Nor is consuming enormous amounts of electricity inherently bad: Many of the advances that have improved modern life have required just that. But as one developer recently told me, industry insiders see their ambitions as constrained less by public opposition than by a lack of electricity. That should tell us something. So what happens next? It’s possible that newly elected politicians’ actual stances on data centers won’t be as negative as the ones they campaigned on. But what seems true regardless is that if new data centers need their own power generated onsite, they’ll go where there are the fewest people able to resist them. That might mean the Permian Basin, where a network of natural-gas lines can be easy for developers to connect to and populations are sparse. Companies might also try their chances abroad; Google, in fact, announced the day after the elections that it will spend $15 billion on data center projects in Malaysia. But there’s one place where Big Tech may find virtually no limits at all, as Elon Musk posted just hours after the midterms results came rolling in: “Data centers in space looking pretty appealing right now.” Ten years after AlphaGo’s match against Go champion Lee Sedol, today’s AI still isn’t tapping into the machinery that made that win possible.
