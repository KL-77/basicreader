Dartmouth's provost warned us about AI cheating—but his own AI use cost him his job

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-30

Visit Original Website
On August 9, 2026, Dartmouth College provost Santiago Schnell wrote a Washington Post op-ed called “Universities are fighting AI cheating. But there’s a deeper problem.” This week, Schnell discovered the depths of the problem himself as he resigned from the university after an advisory committee found that he failed to disclose significant AI use in an academic paper and in the Washington Post op-ed, even though publishers required such disclosure. Schnell didn’t make it especially easy for the committee to investigate his AI use; the report uses the term “insufficient cooperation.” He did turn over his Dartmouth laptop, but he did not turn over logs from his personal LLM accounts related to the writings in question. Nor did he agree to meet with the committee. The committee did get access to the Claude enterprise account Schnell had used through Dartmouth, though. This shows him using the LLM as a tool for revising his papers, finding and fixing bugs in code, and editing or generating figures. For instance, Schnell would run his papers through Claude and ask things like: I am getting read[y] to submit the enclosed paper, its supplementary materials and the enclosed repo to ChemSystemsChem. This paper is not biological, but rather chemical. Imagine that you are a reviewer for this paper with expertise in nonlinear chemical dynamics and mathematical chemical kinetics. What would be your referee report for this paper? This is quite a bit different from asking Claude to write your entire paper for you, and Schnell has long insisted that he used AI as an editorial aid and discussion partner. He told Semafor that he had used chatbots “to refine a few arguments” and “check for grammatical errors.” He told one journal editor that he “used AI as an editorial tool” to “help refine sentences, improve clarity, and the expression of arguments.” He had not disclosed his AI use to an academic journal, he said, because he “did not understand that this type of AI-assisted editing required disclosure.” Indeed, Schnell’s own description of what he did with AI doesn’t sound, on the long list of AI-powered sins, like it would rank near the top. But it’s hard to know. As the Dartmouth committee points out, the full scope of Schnell’s AI use was unclear to them. The committee believes that Schnell’s public statements may understate “the nature of the Provost’s actual AI use” and says that Schnell regularly “went beyond his other public descriptions of his use of AI in writing.” We do know that Schnell also asked LLMs to take TeX files and “make the corrections to the files, and the repo. If you have to generate new figures, please provide them to me. Let me know what you change in the repo, and text files so that I can take a look into it.” Whatever you think of his actions, Schnell repeatedly failed to disclose them. It is certainly possible that he thought his AI use was on par with a spelling or grammar checker, though one Claude exchange raises questions about whether Schnell wanted to hide traces of LLM activity in his work. From the committee report: Provost Schnell’s Claude logs reflect that he uploaded to Claude a manuscript of a forthcoming article entitled Academic Freedom and Catholic Mission: The Procedural Overlap Zone in University Governance and prompted Claude: “Are there many signatures of AI writing in this article?” Schnell later submitted a revised manuscript that incorporated the kinds of changes Claude suggested. Schnell has now resigned. Dartmouth, though, is barreling ahead with AI. The university calls itself the place that “launched the field of artificial intelligence” back in 1956, when “the term was coined and the foundational question was asked: Can machines solve problems once reserved for human minds?” Seventy years later, Dartmouth is hosting a conference called “What Must Remain Human?” that will feature a student hackathon sponsored by Anthropic and speakers like Mira Murati and Yann LeCun. Despite the scandal, Dartmouth President Sian Leah Beilock wants more AI. (She wrote a piece for The Atlantic in September called “Why I Want More AI at Dartmouth.”) What’s crucial, though, is that the school’s leaders should “hold themselves to the highest standards,” she said. This appears to mean one main thing: disclosure. In announcing Schnell’s resignation, Beilock wrote that “each of us brings a different perspective on how and when to incorporate AI into our work and our lives. Moreover, keeping Dartmouth at the forefront of AI inevitably means leading through an environment that is fluid and fast-changing. That is why, as leaders, it is so critical that we model the transparency and human judgment that we expect from our students and scholars.” What does “disclosure” mean? In a September 2026 paper on AI detection tools, called “Who Wrote This?”, Schnell himself goes to great lengths to argue that “human-written” or “AI-written” is far too simple a binary. In his view, everything will become a mix of human judgment and AI work, and there’s nothing wrong with that. The key for him is that the human author takes full responsibility for the text, however it was composed and edited. In the paper, Schnell lays out his method for producing it. It’s worth quoting at some length: In September 2026, I asked OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Pro whether an AI-written essay could ever be identified with certainty, and I suggested connecting the problem to Turing’s imitation game and to the “reverse” Turing test posed by AI-text detection. GPT-5.6 Pro connected the question to binary hypothesis testing and statistical decision theory, proposed early structures and titles, identified candidate literature, and generated portions of early drafts. I chose the problem, journal, audience, and eventual direction of the argument. I decided which examples and results should remain central, including the decision to retain the Liang study while confronting it with important counterevidence. I checked the cited literature, rejected or corrected claims that I judged inaccurate, corrected mathematical and conceptual errors, and reorganized the argument. In revising the manuscript, I rewrote most sections and added substantial explanations. I occasionally asked GPT-5.6 Sol to provide language-editing assistance for sections I had rewritten. I created the figures and made the final decisions about the mathematics, interpretation, sources, organization, and wording. I take full responsibility for the resulting manuscript. Before submitting the manuscript for publication, I also asked GPT-5.6 Pro and Anthropic Claude Fable 5.1 to review it critically and identify issues that reviewers might raise. I evaluated the issues they identified and made corrections where I judged them warranted. Despite all this assistance, Schnell resists the idea that AI is a “co-author.” Instead, he writes, “Calling [the paper] ‘coauthored’ would introduce a different confusion. Mathematical tools have long made substantive contributions without thereby becoming authors… The point is not that the software contributed nothing. It is that contribution and authorship are different roles.” He is not against disclosure, as he makes clear by describing his method. Yet curiously, when submitting other papers for publication, he did not disclose his AI use even when required by publishers. Confused about the rules in an age where even universities are pushing AI? So are many students. New survey data unveiled at this weekend’s conference shows that 61 percent of college students think AI helps explain difficult concepts, and half believe that they produce better work after talking through issues with AI tools. But “70 percent of the college students in the survey indicate that take-home work has become unfair due to AI, with 39 percent feeling pressured to use it because their peers do.” Among Dartmouth students, 92 percent of them fear “that AI can hurt writing skills.” And many of them believe their peers are using far more AI than they actually are. Not everyone is racked with existential doubt over how to deal with AI, though. Dartmouth student Eli Moyse wrote in a September op-ed that Schnell should be fired because “a fetishization of AI to earn publicity for the College has rotted the brain of our top academic.”

Over 25% of people lie to get antibiotics—and their lies aren't even good

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-30

Visit Original Website
A significant number of patients in the US lie to their doctors to get antibiotics they likely don’t need, according to two new studies. While that may not be a startling revelation, the studies drills down on the phenomenon, finding that the lies patients tell are generally bad ones—and yet, they work almost all the time. In the two linked studies published Friday in JAMA Network Open, a team of researchers led by Alistair Thorpe at the University of Utah analyzed online survey data from over 1,000 adults. They found that over a quarter of people reported having lied to a clinician at least once to get an antibiotic prescription for themselves or a child in their care. Patients lied by either exaggerating symptoms, making up symptoms, or—most often—both. But interestingly, the lies people told to get antibiotics involved making up or exaggerating symptoms of infections that wouldn’t be treated with antibiotics. For example, the most common falsehoods were symptoms associated with colds, such as fever, cough, and sore throat. That’s despite the fact that colds are caused by viruses, not bacteria—which antibiotics treat. Further, while the data suggests many patients were being dishonest, clinicians responded with what could be considered their own form of dishonesty; in most cases, the dubious fibs led to antibiotic prescriptions. Specifically, 77 percent of adult patients and 87 percent of caregivers said their lies worked, leading them to get the antibiotic prescriptions they wanted. “These findings signal a significant issue for health professionals making antibiotic prescribing decisions and for the fields of antimicrobial stewardship and public health,” Thorpe and colleagues concluded. For the study, the researchers reviewed survey responses from 1,371 online respondents and found 959 responses that were valid and complete. Of those, 268 (28 percent) respondents reported misrepresenting their symptoms to get antibiotics. Of those, 108 reported exaggerating symptoms, 19 reported fabricating symptoms, and 141 reported doing both. In a separate analysis of 263 respondents who identified as caregivers, 63 (27 percent) reported misrepresenting symptoms of a child to get antibiotics. The most common reported reasons for lying were that people: felt their illness or the illness of a child in their care was particularly severe; believed that antibiotics were the only thing that would help them; believed antibiotics would help them return to responsibilities (work/school) faster; or felt they should have personal autonomy (thinking they know more about their health status than the clinician or feeling like they should get to decide their own treatment). Those who misrepresented their symptoms tended to be younger and feel their symptoms were not taken seriously by clinicians. They were more likely to believe that antibiotic resistance is a hoax, such as a scare tactic by the government or a gimmick by drug companies to justify charging more for antibiotics. People who lied were also more likely to have more underlying conditions, view lying as more acceptable, and report needing help reading health-related materials. On the other hand, those who didn’t report misrepresenting their symptoms tended to have greater antibiotic knowledge and aversion to taking antibiotics. For people who reported misrepresenting symptoms, the online survey gave them additional questions about the types of symptoms they fabricated or exaggerated. The most common falsehoods, reported by 65 percent of dishonest patients, were to misrepresent cold-related symptoms. After that were misrepresented stomach symptoms (49 percent), joint or muscle pain (46 percent), eye symptoms (34 percent), rashes and other skin conditions (32.5 percent), urinary symptoms (31 percent), or a general feeling of being unwell (37 percent). Respondents could also report not remembering what they lied about. “To be clear, none of these are specific indications for antibiotics,” Jeffrey Linder, of Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine, wrote in an accompanying commentary, highlighting that these lies shouldn’t have helped patients get unnecessary antibiotics. “In fact, of the 8 categories offered that patients could have exaggerated or made up to get antibiotics, only 1 of them (urinary symptoms) clearly justified antibiotic prescribing,” he wrote. While symptoms of respiratory infections can be due to bacterial infections in some situations—namely, acute sinus infection, strep throat, or pneumonia—those conditions have specific clinical guidance and/or associated tests to confirm the diagnosis, Linder highlights. Given the magnitude of the deception, the findings raise concern that the ill-gotten antibiotics could contribute to the rise of antibiotic-resistant infections. Misuse and overuse of antibiotics are known to drive the development and spread of drug resistance. This week, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention released an updated report on antimicrobial resistance threats, finding that drug-resistant bacteria and fungi are now causing 9.7 million infections and 50,800 deaths in the US each year. But in the accompanying commentary, Linder said that an even more concerning consequence may be the “erosion of trust” between clinicians and patients. Linder acknowledged that drug resistance is a concern, but said that on the basis of an individual patient, in most clinical circumstances, the development of drug resistance is “probably irrelevant.” To Linder, the more pertinent question doctors must face is: “Is this prescription more likely to help my patient or hurt them?” The answer is that it’s more likely to hurt; unnecessary antibiotics provide no benefit, but pose risk of adverse events, allergic reactions, and disruption of healthy microbiomes, which can provide an opening for opportunistic pathogens, such as Clostridioides difficile (which is classified as a “serious threat” in the CDC’s newly updated antimicrobial resistance threat report.) “For these non-antibiotic-appropriate diagnoses, prescribing antibiotics is medically incorrect and could itself be considered a form of dishonesty or lying mainly to assuage the patient,” Linder wrote. That dishonesty is corrosive, he said, quoting philosopher Sissela Bok, who wrote extensively about lying and, even specifically, about lying to get antibiotics. In 1978, Bok noted that antibiotics are “sometimes used as placebos” and many parents “come to believe that they must ask for the prescription of antibiotics every time their child has a fever or a cold.” But Bok argued that when clinicians prescribe medications more likely to harm than help, such “practices lacking in candor” threaten “the entire institution of medicine.” Linder concludes by reminding readers that “Clinicians’ obligation is to act in patients’ best interest, even if patients are hoping we do something different, even if patients do not like it, or even if patients lie about their symptoms.” Thorpe and colleagues, meanwhile, conclude with a call to address the reasons patients lie in the first place, writing: “Strategies to improve communication about antibiotics are needed to address patient concerns, support understanding of appropriate antibiotic use, and strengthen patient-clinician relationships.”

As AI tools and agents have continued their march across all aspects of digital life, many companies are trying to use LLMs to improve the experience of purchasing goods and services on the Internet. Thus far, though, a vanishingly small number of people seem to be taking advantage of those capabilities, at least when it comes to using the popular food delivery app DoorDash with an outside AI agent. In a third-quarter earnings call Thursday, DoorDash co-founder and CEO Tony Xu responded to an analyst question by noting that “AI agents are generating several hundred orders [on DoorDash] across the time that they’ve been up and running.” In a marked understatement, Xu called this a “very low use case” for a company that handled just shy of 1 billion orders total in the third quarter of 2026 alone. Speaking generally, Xu said he sees the potential for AI agents to be genuinely helpful for “long consideration purchases” and major decisions where agents would be “doing work on your behalf proactively in the background.” However, Xu added, “when it comes to a task that’s as simple as ordering food, we tend to see that come directly to DoorDash.” To be fair to the AI boosters, DoorDash only rolled out its Model Context Protocol (MCP) connector less than a month ago. The connector provides a standardized, open-standard way for third-party AI tools to easily order through DoorDash’s platform. Without that kind of linkup—which is still only available to a small number of “early beta partners” and mainly used to streamline corporate lunch orders—AI agents have to go through a bit more effort to access DoorDash through a web interface, for instance. Xu’s discussion of “third-party agent” orders also doesn’t include DoorDash’s own internal AI tools. Those include an “Ask DoorDash” feature the company rolled out this summer that can, for instance, create an online shopping cart from a photograph of a recipe or suggest an order based on a vague description like “dinner for a family of four.” DoorDash hasn’t shared detailed statistics on that kind of usage, but the company said last month that Ask DoorDash baskets get built “five times faster” than those built without, and Ask DoorDash orders “average nearly 50% higher basket value and about 60% more unique items than the same consumers’ traditional orders.” The company also said nearly half of Ask DoorDash orders end up at “local spots the consumer has never tried before,” helping with discovery of new area restaurants. That’s all to say that DoorDash’s current experience with agentic ordering may be a bit like Pizza Hut’s first tentative online ordering experiments in 1994. Back then, it would have been hard to picture today’s world, where more than half of Pizza Hut orders are placed online. Maybe ordering food and other goods using AI agents will be just as commonplace years from now, and we’ll look back at these early statistics with bemusement. For now, though, Xu at least seems relatively cool on the early numbers. “My take on all things technology is you never want to fight gravity,” he said regarding AI agents. “But at the same time, you don’t want to be delusional.”

Rare meteorite from Mars gives us a view into its ancient crust

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-30

Visit Original Website
Crater counts tell us that most of the Martian surface is more than 3 billion years old, but pieces of Mars we have on Earth tell a different story. The three main classes of Martian meteorites—shergottites, nakhlites, and chassignites—are all 2.4 billion years old or younger. Until now, only two genuinely ancient Martian meteorites were known: Allan Hills 84001, found in Antarctica, and a jumbled rock from northwest Africa nicknamed Black Beauty. Now we have a third one. A team led by Yang Liu, a planetary scientist at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory, has shown that a Martian meteorite called Teghaza 001, found in the Sahara Desert in Mali in 2022, formed at least 4.1 billion years ago. “It’s very old and similar to some of the oldest rocks on Earth,” Liu says. “We don’t fully understand why old Martian rocks are so rare on Earth,” Liu said. Part of the answer may lie in how a piece of Mars gets here. It takes a large impact to launch a rock off the planet, and not every rock can survive that. “If the impact hits into loose powder, it may not get cemented to become a rock and fly off Mars’ gravity,” Liu said. “So the natural impact already acts as a filter. Weak rocks do not make it off Mars.” That left scientists with a biased collection. “Even to date, none of our collected Martian meteorites are similar to what we found on the surface,” Liu said. The partial exception is Black Beauty, which has a bulk chemistry that matches orbital measurements. But Black Beauty is a mix of soil and many small rock fragments. Teghaza 001 is a single, intact igneous rock. Teghaza 001 was acquired through the desert meteorite trade, where hunters comb the Sahara for dark stones that stand out against the sand. Its eight stones, weighing just under 800 grams in total, were bought by a private collector, who sent a piece to the University of New Mexico for classification. There, planetary scientists Carl Agee and Karen Ziegler measured its oxygen isotopes. “Each planetary body has a very characteristic oxygen isotope value,” Liu says. Teghaza 001’s values matched those of other Martian meteorites. At first, the rock was classified as a shergottite, the most common (and young) type of Martian meteorite. But Agee noticed some oddities and reached out to Liu. One of Teghaza’s minerals had been altered on the inside while its outer part stayed pristine, the reverse of how weathering on Earth works. The oxygen isotopes looked a bit strange, and there was some manganese-rich material similar to what Liu had studied in Black Beauty. “When I got to the sample, I looked around, I said, ‘That’s boring,’” Liu said. “The high-manganese material is actually terrestrial, but I quickly realized the sample is very different, very special.” Teghaza 001 is made mostly of feldspar, a pale mineral common in the crusts of rocky planets. It has darker pyroxene crystals mixed in; according to Liu, those resemble some rocks exposed in the lunar highlands. Its crystals are rather big, up to a millimeter across, which scientists interpret as an indication it cooled down slowly. “For slow-cooled rock, they tend to have time to grow bigger,” Liu said. By comparing the crystals with lab experiments and terrestrial rocks, the team estimated Teghaza 001 cooled by roughly 0.01° Celsius per year, indicating it formed from magma that never erupted but instead solidified inside the crust. The team dated the rock in several independent ways. Zircon grains gave about 4.11 billion years. Phosphate minerals and rubidium-strontium dating gave similar ages. “Doesn’t matter what type of zircon we are analyzing or what mineral we are analyzing, we get this very consistent age. And we didn’t expect that,” Liu said. It is notoriously hard to reset the age of zircons, and the impact that launched the rock, while strong enough to forge high-pressure minerals, wasn’t hot enough to do it. “To change it, you really require some kind of hydrothermal fluid,” Liu said. Sodium-rich rims on feldspar grains and sodium trapped in the zircons support the idea that hot water percolated through the rock shortly after it formed. So 4.1 billion years marks its last soaking, the youngest of what were probably several alteration events. “The crystallization age would be older than 4.1 billion years,” Liu said. Teghaza 001 is also unusually rich in oxygen-18, a heavier form of oxygen, with about two parts per thousand more than other Martian meteorites. Neither crystallization nor gas escaping from magma can produce a difference that big. Because water that reacts with rock at low temperatures near the planet’s surface forms minerals rich in heavy oxygen, the team thinks the magma either swallowed bits of crust that had already interacted with surface water or reacted directly with fluids that had picked up the same heavy signature. This implies that “There was active circulation of water through Martian crust,” Liu said. “When this rock was forming, we do have a hydrosphere on Mars, and that hydrosphere is reacting with Martian crust intensely.” The water must have been circulating through the crust and reacting with magma before it had even finished cooling. “It’s actually more dynamic and more active than we thought,” Liu said. She argues that this finding fits the picture of a wet early Mars and points to potentially habitable environments in its crust. Teghaza 001 also reveals details about Mars’ interior. The rock crystallized near the surface, but the magma it formed from came from much deeper. Ratios of trace elements that resist alteration, like the zirconium to titanium ratio, differ from those in every other Martian meteorite. The team’s melting models matched the rock’s chemistry best when its mantle source contained garnet, a mineral that is stable in the Martian mantle only at depths of more than 160 kilometers. Studies of other Martian meteorites suggest that as an early ocean of magma solidified on Mars roughly 4.5 billion years ago, the mantle separated into three to five reservoirs. Teghaza 001’s source does not match any of them. “In the first few hundred million years, the Martian mantle was evolving in a way that’s more complicated than we thought,” Liu said. Comparing the rock’s chemistry with orbital maps, the team suggests it most likely came from the southern highlands, northwest of the giant Hellas impact basin. “No matter how many meteorite samples we have, we will always have this question: Where are they coming from on Mars?” Liu said. Orbital data can narrow a source down to a patch of roughly 100 by 100 meters at best, which often amounts to one pixel in our remote sensing data. When Teghaza 001 left Mars is also an open question. “We only did the minimum required to make the world know this sample is special,” Liu said, adding that two or three groups are already studying it to get further insights. The real game-changer, Liu said, would be samples collected with careful geological context, like those Perseverance has gathered in and around Jezero Crater. Liu would like to see some from terrains as old as Teghaza 001 or older. “If these samples come back, there are so many discoveries that can be made,” Liu said. “This could revolutionize our understanding of Mars and the early solar system.” Liu’s study on Teghaza 001 is published in Science: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adz5646

In an announcement released Thursday, Fujifilm said it will invest roughly 10 billion yen (about $63 million) to increase its production capacity fivefold for an unlikely beneficiary of the AI era: magnetic tape. Fujifilm attributes the surge in archiving demand to the spread of generative AI, which the company remarked is producing “exponential growth” in data needs worldwide. The 5x increase in production capacity will be measured against fiscal year 2025 production levels. The investment in Fujifilm’s Kanagawa Factory, Odawara site will make it “the largest investment Fujifilm has made to date in dedicated LTO tape facilities.” The project will run from fiscal years 2026 through 2028 to fund manufacturing expansion and additional personnel, primarily to produce the latest-generation “FUJIFILM LTO Ultrium10” tape. Mainstream consumers may have abandoned tape decades ago, but the enterprise world never did. Cloud giants rely on tape to back up vast troves of data. Fujifilm’s work with magnetic tape as a data storage medium dates back to the 1950s, and in 2000, the company released its first LTO Ultrium storage tape. In 2011, the company was the first to commercially produce tape with barium ferrite magnetic particles, and in 2025 it debuted the LTO Ultrium10, the first storage solution in the medium to use what the company calls “fine hybrid magnetic particles” by combining barium ferrite with strontium ferrite tech. Each generation of tape (currently Gen 10) has seen meaningful increases in both compressed and uncompressed storage limits. According to Fujifilm, “major cloud service and data center operators” have been expanding their use of magnetic tape because it can store large data volumes at relatively low cost. Tape backups can be easily disconnected and stored in a drawer, a vault, or a separate facility. Fujifilm highlights the ability to air-gap tape cartridges as a last defense for user data: “Given the sophistication of cyberattacks, magnetic tape is attracting increasing attention as a means of protecting data from threats such as ransomware.”

Thousands of “ghost students” use AI to steal millions in student loans, DOJ says

Visit Original Website
The US has charged five individuals as part of the first broad federal crackdown on “ghost students”—fraudsters who steal the identities of hundreds of Americans to pose as fake college students and steal student loans. In a press release Thursday, the Department of Justice said it had uncovered a large-scale scheme relying partly on AI. The DOJ seized more than $750,000 in cryptocurrency from “a network associated with Kenyan nationals” after data analysis helped flag likely fraudsters. Assistant Attorney General Colin M. McDonald of the DOJ’s National Fraud Enforcement Division credited new fraud-detection measures implemented by the Trump administration for the arrests. He said that “this is the first international seizure of its kind in Kenya,” while warning fraudsters that the crackdown has only just begun. “If you steal American identities, if you steal taxpayer dollars, if you exploit programs meant to help students build a future—we will find you,” McDonald said. “Whether you are in Nebraska, New Jersey, Virginia, or Nairobi, we will pursue you.” At a press conference, McDonald was joined by FBI and Department of Education officials to explain how the scheme works. It starts with bad actors purchasing personally identifiable information (PII) of Americans—most frequently targeting high school graduates who never attended college and went straight into the workforce. Once they have that sensitive data, they fill out the Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA) while seeking to enroll in universities and community colleges. If accepted, student aid is distributed first to schools to cover tuition and fees, but any leftover money is deposited into bank accounts controlled by the fraudsters. Dozens of Americans have opened bank accounts to act as money mules for fraudsters, the DOJ said. To keep money flowing, bad actors use AI to fill out applications, complete coursework, and even make it appear that a student is attending virtual sessions. “It looks like the ghost student is actually completing the class,” McDonald said at the press conference. “Some even create the appearance of a fake student in an online classroom to trick schools into believing a real person is present. The goal is to keep the ghost student in school for as long as possible, semester after semester after semester.” So far, the DOE has “identified over 830,000 potentially fraudulent federal student aid accounts created by using this PII of US citizens,” officials said at the press conference. If all those accounts are ghost students, total losses come close to $130 million, the DOJ said. For Americans whose data is stolen, there’s little opportunity to discover the scheme until the government comes collecting on loans years later. In the meantime, victims’ credit can be damaged. Once they detect the scheme, they’re stuck defending against demands to repay inflated student loans, which McDonald admitted is messy. “The Department of Education does a great job to help unravel that mess, but it is a mess that no one should have to endure,” he said. In connection with the arrests, officials said they “identified more than $32 million in actual loss tied to these specific subjects” and “we believe that actual total is substantially higher,” with “millions more suspected.” The DOE’s Office of Inspector General first detected the fraud patterns that brought the alleged scheme to light. The FBI then helped identify how many accounts were impacted, tracing money to US and foreign bank accounts and crypto wallets. In one case, a defendant “used multiple bank accounts across several financial institutions to receive over 700 fraudulently obtained federal student aid refund deposits, totaling approximately $1,439,548,” the DOJ said. In another, “one person received more than $1 million in deposits that were linked to more than 300 fraudulent student accounts,” Inside Higher Ed reported. At the press conference, First Assistant US Attorney Theophani K. Stamos for the Eastern District of Virginia said that data analysis “identified unusual, suspicious patterns of behavior” that the FBI said indicated “coordinated fraud was happening involving FAFSA.” Those patterns gave investigators “a solid basis and a solid lead to open further inquiries that have led to indictments,” Stamos said. To keep the crackdown going, his office has sent “approximately 50 referrals to 26 FBI field offices across the country” to hunt down stolen taxpayer funds. “We are just getting started. Working with partners around the world, we are sending a clear message: student aid belongs to real students, the data always leaves a trail, and we will follow it wherever it leads,” Stamos said. The DOJ suspects this scam has been running since at least 2021, growing increasingly sophisticated as AI opens new avenues for loan theft. “These fraudsters are exploiting Americans who have never been to college before, preventing the Department of Education from cross-checking applicants,” the DOJ said. “Many known victims have reported attaining only high school diplomas—and are shocked by their faked attendance at an institution hundreds of miles away.” At the press conference, Undersecretary of Education Nicholas Kent accused the Biden administration of leaving Americans unprotected. “When we walked in the door last January, we were extremely troubled to find that the Biden administration had turned off nearly every fraud detection measure,” Kent said. Trump’s DOE said it introduced online identity verification requirements that Kent claimed would stop ghost students from ever receiving funds. Relying on automated systems to flag behaviors and reject fraudulent FAFSA applications, Federal Trade Commission Chairman Andrew Ferguson, vice chair of the White House Task Force to Eliminate Fraud, insisted that fraud could be easily stopped without allocating staff and resources or overly burdening the US government. “The computers help us find it, and we keep the money in the agency,” Ferguson said. The automated system isn’t perfect, however, and advocates have warned that false flags burden schools, meaning students’ access to aid could be delayed as administrators work through the added identity-verification processes. Glitches have already affected fraud detection and student applications. In August, the DOE confirmed a technical issue from June 29 to July 6 prevented fraud evaluation results from being shared with certain FAFSA forms. After detecting the glitch, the DOE reprocessed those applications. Colleges and universities are also considering adding fees to stop the ghost student fraud. Some advocates have acknowledged that the fraud deprives real students of funds. But they’re concerned that both the fees and online identity verification could become barriers to access to higher education for low-income students. To verify their identities through the online FAFSA portal, students are expected to have government-issued IDs on hand in “real time.” They hold the IDs up to their cameras, and automated systems review them. University of Bristol’s Prof Michelle Spear looks at how your brain could be responsible for things that go bump in the night, not ghosts. A version of this article was originally published by The Conversation (CC BY-ND 4.0) It’s three in the morning. You’ve woken from a dead sleep because you’ve heard a thud in the darkness or a floorboard creaking downstairs. Or perhaps you’ve woken because you have the unmistakable sensation you’re not alone. During daylight, these phenomena might be dismissed as the heating, your neighbours or the sounds of an old house settling. But in the middle of the night, they can feel a tad more sinister. Your mind might immediately start reeling, jumping to the worst-case scenario – wondering if you’ve been broken into, or if you’re being haunted by some supernatural force. In reality, the explanation is far more simple. Things really do go bump in the night. But occasionally, the culprit is closer to home than we might imagine. Falling asleep is not the neurological equivalent of switching off a light. The brain moves gradually from wakefulness into sleep, accompanied by changes in sensory processing, muscle activity and awareness of the world around us. But these changes don’t always occur in perfect synchrony. So for brief periods, characteristics of sleep and wakefulness can overlap. The results can be surprisingly spooky. If you’ve ever been on the verge of sleep only to feel that you’ve fallen off a kerb, missed a step or dropped through the bed, you’ve probably experienced a hypnic jerk. Also known as a sleep start, this is a sudden involuntary contraction of one or more muscles as we move into the earliest stages of sleep. It’s an example of how the transition between wakefulness and sleep isn’t always a smooth one. Hypnagogic experiences can also involve vivid sounds, images or sensations. So as you’re on the edge of sleep, you may think you hear footsteps or whispers – or you may even think you see a figure in the room. Exploding head syndrome – perhaps the most alarming name for a harmless sleep condition – is another example of how the boundaries between sleep and wakefulness can become blurred. As someone is falling asleep, or occasionally waking, they suddenly perceive an enormous noise: an explosion, gunshot or slamming door – yet nothing in the outside world has made a sound. Its precise cause remains uncertain. But the phenomenon demonstrates something important: hearing a sound and a sound actually occurring are not quite the same thing. The conscious experience of hearing is constructed by the brain. So neural activity generated without an external sound can still produce an auditory experience that seems entirely real. The brain doesn’t even need to be asleep for this to happen. If you’ve ever thought you’ve heard someone call your name – only to turn around and find no one there – you’ve been tricked by your brain. Another possible explanation for the sounds we think we hear at night involves source monitoring. This is the brain’s ability to distinguish events generated internally from those coming from the outside world. Thoughts, memories and imagined speech are internally generated, yet normally retain the quality of being ours. But occasionally, that attribution goes awry – allowing something generated within the brain to feel as though it came from somewhere else. Around sleep, that boundary becomes particularly interesting. Another common – but frightening – experience that can happen at night is waking up with the sudden feeling that someone else is in the room. This experience is usually the result of sleep paralysis. During rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, when most of our vivid dreaming occurs, the brain temporarily suppresses activity in most muscles. This protective mechanism, known as REM atonia, prevents us from physically acting out our dreams. But during sleep paralysis, our waking awareness returns while this physical paralysis persists. You know where you are, but cannot move. Yet elements of dream perception can remain active. This is why you may have experienced the sensation that someone – or something – is in the room with you, despite clear sensory evidence of the contrary. One clue as to why this happens comes from experiments examining how the brain distinguishes itself from others. In one study, participants controlled a robotic device positioned in front of them, while a second robot, which was placed behind them, reproduced these movements by touching the participants’ backs. But when researchers introduced a slight delay between the movement and the touch, some participants began to feel as though somebody else was touching them. Some even sensed another person standing behind them. While this experiment doesn’t explain every felt presence that can happen during sleep paralysis, it does reveal something remarkable: the distinction between ourselves and others is something the brain has to construct. Usually, movement, touch and our sense of body position agree about what belongs to us. But if that relationship is disrupted, an internally generated sensation can begin to feel like it originated elsewhere. Now picture this happening in a dark bedroom, when you’re partly awake yet still on the edge of dreaming and unable to move. The sensation that somebody else is there becomes rather less mysterious. Perception is not a passive recording of the world. It combines incoming sensory information with expectation and previous experience to construct the most likely explanation. But at the boundary between sleep and waking, this process can become less reliable. We may hear sounds that were never made, experience fragments of dreams intruding our bedroom or interpret sensations arising from our own bodies as the presence of somebody else. Earlier this week, the New York Times published a splashy feature detailing how allies of Mary Peltola, the Democratic nominee in this year’s Senate race in Alaska, worked to “stack” the ballot with candidates who could help her defeat the Republican incumbent, Dan Sullivan. As a result of these efforts, when Alaskans go to the polls next week, appearing alongside Peltola and Sullivan on the ballot will be one Daniel J. Sullivan, a retired teacher who voted for Peltola in previous elections—and who, according to the Times, was recruited by Democratic operative types to play the spoiler role. The Times’ implicit framing of Daniel J. Sullivan’s candidacy as an election-swinging dirty trick requires a few caveats. First and most importantly, Alaska uses ranked-choice voting, which means that Sen. Sullivan supporters confused by their two options can simply vote for both. Second, Peltola beat Sen. Sullivan by eight points in the August primary, and Daniel J. Sullivan picked up 2.5 percent of the vote—significant, sure, but nowhere close to Peltola’s margin of victory. Third, Alaska officials have taken great pains to distinguish the two Sullivans on the ballot, displaying their names differently and designating Dan Sullivan as the “incumbent.” Also, just speaking practically here, if you believed that the presence in a tightly contested Senate race of a person with the same name as a vulnerable incumbent in a tough year for Republicans was a complete coincidence, I have on good authority that the bridge to nowhere is getting built after all, and I would sell it to you for a favorable price. None of these facts have prevented President Donald Trump and his fellow Republicans from getting very angry about this stunt. Before the Times story was published, Justice Department officials, at Trump’s direction, had filed a lawsuit seeking to block state officials from certifying the results if Peltola beats Dan Sullivan by less than Daniel J. Sullivan’s share of the vote. (Again, thanks to ranked-choice voting, this scenario is extremely farfetched, but stay with me.) After the Times story, Trump went much further, calling for criminal charges against Peltola and the “FAKE Dan Sullivan.” He also demanded that both of them be removed from the ballot, which would give Republicans nervous about losing the Senate one fewer seat to worry about. Sure enough, on Wednesday, Alaska Gov. The reality he is glossing over is that, as you would probably expect, there have already been legal challenges to Sullivan’s candidacy, based in part on some of the details in the Times story. In June, though, an Alaska court determined that under state law, he has the same right to run for office as Sen. Dan Sullivan or anyone else. What Peltola’s campaign (allegedly) did might be partisan skullduggery, but it is probably not illegal, and no one is getting kicked off the ballot or going to prison over it. This case started back in May, when Daniel J. Sullivan filed to run for Senate as a Republican, despite never having previously been affiliated with the party. After the state GOP complained, the Alaska Division of Elections determined that under state law, Daniel J. Sullivan was ineligible to run because he did not file “in good faith for the purpose of genuinely pursuing election,” and instead sought to “confuse or mislead” voters. The letter noted, for example, his request to be identified as “Dan,” like the incumbent. It also alluded to the subject matter of the Times story, highlighting his work with a political consultant who is a “known longtime supporter of Democratic candidates,” including Peltola. Sullivan appealed, and in June, an Alaska trial court judge reversed the Division of Elections’s decision. The gist of Judge Thomas A. Matthews’s order, which the Alaska Supreme Court later affirmed, is that under state law, the qualifications to run for Senate straightforwardly track the language of the U.S. Constitution: Candidates must be at least 30 years old and citizens for at least nine years, and live in the state they seek to represent. Given that Daniel J. Sullivan checks all three boxes, Matthews found that officials had no authority to invent an additional “good-faith” requirement and use it to “exclude an otherwise qualified candidate.” Matthews’s opinion does not suggest that he is naive about what’s going on here. But critical to his analysis is the fact that under state law, the Division of Elections could have previously taken steps to prevent this from happening, but didn’t: He points out that other states have dealt with same-name races, and that many states have rules for weighing the “good faith” of prospective candidates. The challenge they face is that, as the Times acknowledged, ballot-stacking techniques are “common in politics,” “generally considered legal,” and have been “employed by both parties in Alaska.” Peltola knows this better than most: In 2024, when she lost her bid for re-election to the House, Republicans went to court to ensure that Eric Hafner, who is serving a 20-year federal prison sentence in New York for threatening to kill judges, remained on the ballot as a Democrat. During the primary campaign in this very election, outside groups were pouring money into the state, hoping to sabotage Peltola by placing other Democrats on the general election ballot. Intuitively, recruiting a candidate with the same name as another candidate feels like a more nefarious form of ballot-stacking. On Truth Social, for example, Trump claimed that the Times revealed that Democrats had lied “under oath in Federal Court,” which is an accurate description of a crime, but not a thing that happened here. (Even that conviction was overturned on appeal.) Whatever you think of Daniel J. Sullivan’s candidacy, it is not an illegal effort to stop people from voting: He is running for office, under his real name, in the state where he lives. Anyone who gets “tricked” into voting for him (and only for him) is still, in fact, voting. By loudly investigating the matter now, they are leaving open the possibility of challenging the results, not with oafish phone calls or a horde of Trump supporters storming the Capitol, but with the institutional power of the Justice Department. Still others understandably took offense at the notion that Alaskans are not smart enough to figure out how to vote. This is shady business. Donald Trump Sure Is Corrupt. If Only There Were a Constitutional Remedy for That.

This is the fifth in a series of articles, “Try, Try Again?,” drawn from my new book—Great and Dangerous Offences: The Case For (and Against) Impeaching Trump a Third Time—due out in early 2027. The most common ground for impeachment throughout Anglo-American history has been corruption, the misuse of the status and powers of office for private gain, either for oneself or for one’s family, friends, or political allies. Both Parliament and the American Framers abhorred official corruption and made it impeachable because it undercuts the basic contract between rulers and the ruled—that the rulers will not place their private interests, whether in wealth or increased power, over the interests and well-being of the nation. Donald Trump has broken this covenant by repeatedly, corruptly accepting payments and gifts from foreign powers, thereby violating the foreign emoluments clause and betraying the national interests of the United States. This conduct is a clear impeachable offense. Corruption can take many forms, from simple bribery to complex networks of exchanges of money, commercial concessions, and political favors. An especially insidious form of corruption, one especially to be feared from a monarch or president, is the sale of the nation’s interests to a foreign power. The American Framers repeatedly mentioned corruption as being, next to treason, the most necessary ground for presidential impeachment. George Mason spoke of the “corruptibility” of presidents as a primary justification for impeachment. Gouverneur Morris specified “corruption” as one of the “few” offenses for which he would favor impeachment. Among the definitions of impeachable conduct provisionally approved early in the Constitutional Convention were “treason, bribery, or corruption” and then “treason or bribery.” “Bribery,” a specific form of corruption requiring a quid pro quo exchange of favors for something of value, is part of the final constitutional definition of impeachable conduct. Late in the convention, the delegates adopted the additional phrase “or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors” in important part to expand the reach of impeachable corruption beyond technical bribery. The Framers’ fear of corruption is also evident in the Constitution’s emoluments clauses. The domestic emoluments clause bars presidents from taking anything beyond their established salaries from either Congress or the states. The foreign emoluments clause prohibits all federal officials from accepting gifts of valuables, office, or title from any foreign country without express congressional consent. Critically, unlike bribery as traditionally defined in the criminal law, the prohibition on emoluments is not conditional on any corrupt bargain by the public official. Likewise, emoluments are barred regardless of whether the official takes or refrains from any act whatever. The Framers understood that, even for the most virtuous, receipt of favors creates a predisposition to do favors in return. Once that disposition exists, it is only a tiny step to corrupt action. Therefore, mere acceptance of an emolument, without any accompanying promise or reciprocal official act, violates the Constitution. Violation of the foreign emoluments clause is impeachable corruption. At the Philadelphia convention, Morris several times insisted on the need for impeachment in the case of a president in the pay of a foreign power. At the Virginia ratifying convention, Edmund Randolph said explicitly that “there is another provision against the danger … of the President receiving emoluments from foreign powers. If discovered, he may be impeached.” During Trump’s first term, foreign governments or entities closely affiliated with foreign governments paid large sums to his hospitality businesses for lodging, food, entertainment, meeting services, and the like. Although the foreign emoluments clause permits receipt of emoluments from foreign kings, princes, or states with the “Consent of Congress,” Trump never sought the required consent. In his second term, Trump’s pursuit of prohibited emoluments has been far more egregious, in both its brazenness and the amounts involved. However, it is useful to think about his first-term behavior in order to frame the issues surrounding his second-term conduct. From the beginning, Trump’s business arrangements presented the troublesome question of whether domestic or foreign government payments to a president avoid the prohibition on emoluments so long as they take the form of a commercial exchange. Trump supporters argued during his first term that an arm’s-length business transaction is not a “present” or “emolument.” To this Trump added the more general plaint that a businessman should not have to surrender his business (or even abandon its active management) to be president. Both contentions are flawed. In the founding period, the word emolument was used to mean not only salary but far more commonly profit, gain, advantage, or benefit. Even an arm’s-length commercial transaction implies mutual benefit. The seller provides a good or service and receives in return value exceeding the cost to him of the good or service—a profit. When the federal or state government or a foreign power buys from a president’s business, it confers on him a profit, gain, advantage, or benefit. It does not matter for emoluments purposes that there is no corrupt intention on either side. The point of the emoluments prohibition is to eliminate the need to prove such things. It is the conferring of the benefit that is prohibited, and the Constitution is emphatic in banning foreign emoluments “of any kind whatever.” Likewise, the contention that a president should not have to alter his profit-making activities to enter public office is wrong. The Framers wrote the salary and emoluments provisions into the Constitution to ensure not only that the wealthy could afford to serve the country but also that the poor and those of middle station would not become wealthy (or the already-wealthy wealthier) by entering public office. Trump’s position is that wealthy people like himself should be able to occupy the presidency while surrendering no private advantages, and indeed should be entitled to leverage public office to create greater personal wealth. In short, he claims a right to engage in the precise form of corrupt self-enrichment that has since the 14th century been the quintessential impeachable offense. Moreover, the Constitution does not ban foreign emoluments absolutely; it merely requires that Congress consent to them. A businessman president who wants to both serve and continue his foreign commercial connections simply has to ask for permission, and in doing so, provide the people’s representatives sufficient information to assure them that giving permission does not impinge on larger national interests. Trump has never asked. During either of his terms. And Congress has never given permission. The most notorious gift to Trump from a foreign power was Qatar’s 2025 transfer of a luxuriously appointed Boeing 747-8 jet worth $400 million. The announced terms of the gift were that the United States would upgrade the plane for use as Trump’s presidential aircraft, known as Air Force One, but that at the end of his term, it would pass to the ownership of the Trump presidential library. In the summer of 2026, Trump seemingly began to waver on whether the Qatari plane would go to his library, or whether he might try to decommission a different presidential plane and transfer that one. The question remains unresolved. The terms of the gift as originally announced plainly violated the foreign emoluments clause. It was a gift from a foreign government to Donald Trump, to be enjoyed by him while president and to pass into the ownership of his presidential library thereafter. Congress never approved either the original gift or the proviso that it would later become the property of an entity controlled by Trump. Even if Trump ultimately changes his mind about taking the plane with him when he leaves office, the congressionally unapproved gift nonetheless violates the Constitution. The distinguishing features of the Qatari airplane are its size, comfort, and the luxury of its interior furnishings, points Trump has gushed over. Foreign gifts made for the personal pleasure or enjoyment of the president during office do not escape the emoluments clause if someone else gets to enjoy them thereafter. If, for example, a foreign country gave a president an entirely new wardrobe of tailored suits and casual wear, that would be a prohibited emolument, even if the president promised that, upon leaving the White House, he would donate the whole lot to the Smithsonian or Goodwill. Of greater ongoing concern than the Qatari airplane is the flood of commercial agreements the Trump Organization, controlled by Trump but nominally operated by his sons Eric and Donald Jr., has entered into with foreign entities. For example, only weeks before the plane gift in May 2025, the Trump Organization announced its participation in development of a $5.5 billion luxury golf resort in Doha, Qatar. Trump would doubtless argue that the resort deal is a purely private business transaction between developers in Qatar and his family business, thus not qualifying it as an emolument from a foreign state. But the Qatari development company with which the Trumps are partnering is reported to be Qatari Diar, which is owned by the Qatari government. Moreover, Qatar is an absolute monarchy ruled by a hereditary emir without whose approval no decision by a government-owned entity to do business with a U.S. president’s family could possibly occur. In 1993, the Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel concluded that “business corporations owned or controlled by foreign governments will fall within the [Foreign Emoluments] Clause,” a result it reaffirmed in 2009. Likewise, any contention that money received by the Trump Organization is not an emolument to Trump because his sons run the company is a threadbare fiction. So far as can be determined, Trump owns all of or controlling interests in all the entities that make up the Trump Organization. Money paid to it is money paid to him. The golf resort deal is, therefore, a prohibited foreign emolument. Worse, the Qataris’ multiple gifts to President Trump are not merely technical violations of the foreign emoluments clause, but are a classic instance of why the Framers put that clause in the Constitution to begin with. Qatar has huge stakes in its relationship with the U.S. government. U.S. trade in goods and services with Qatar exceeds $10 billion annually. More importantly, as graphically demonstrated during the ongoing Iran–U.S. war, the United States is the principal security guarantor of Qatar. Indeed, in September 2025, only four months after Qatar gave Trump an airplane and his family business a major real estate concession, President Trump issued an executive order declaring that “the United States shall regard any armed attack on the territory, sovereignty, or critical infrastructure of the State of Qatar as a threat to the peace and security of the United States,” and stating that in the event of an attack, the United States would defend Qatar. What we have here is not a plane gift made as a friendly intergovernmental gesture between traditional allies or a normal, arm’s-length real estate deal that just happens to involve Trump’s family business, but what normal people would see as bribes paid by a foreign government to the president of the United States to induce him to take official actions favoring Qatar. And bribery is an impeachable offense. Moreover, the Qatar golf resort deal is not the only one of its kind. According to one source, as of July 2026, 24 Trump-branded real estate projects were underway in foreign countries. Not all of the contractual counterparties to these agreements have the direct government connection of Qatar’s Qatari Diar. But some do, as was true in December 2024, a month after Trump’s second election, when the Trumps secured participation in a real estate development supported by Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman. In addition, LIV Golf, a professional golf league financed by the Saudi sovereign wealth fund, paid the Trump family an undisclosed amount to host an April 2025 tournament at the Trump National Doral resort in Florida. Like Qatar, Saudi Arabia depends heavily on the United States for security support. Moreover, in the same period that the Saudis were paying to rent a Trump golf course and Trump businesses were moving into Saudi real estate with Prince bin Salman’s blessing, Trump’s administration was negotiating an agreement (on which Trump is now waffling) that would permit the Saudis to expand their use of nuclear power. The appearance of influence-buying is obvious. The Trump family’s cryptocurrency speculations have also involved repeated egregious violations of the foreign emoluments clause. In sum, firms backed by the government of Abu Dhabi and headed by a member of its royal family paid half a billion dollars for a 49 percent stake in the Trump crypto company World Liberty Financial, half apparently paid before Trump’s 2025 inauguration and half after. Thereafter, an Abu Dhabi–backed group invested $2 billion in crypto company Binance by purchasing World Liberty “stablecoins,” giving World Liberty $2 billion in deposits to invest. Like Saudi Arabia and Qatar, Abu Dhabi and the other members of the United Arab Emirates are deeply economically enmeshed with the United States and heavily dependent on America for national security protection. It would seem entirely accurate to characterize these investments in Trump’s crypto wealth as literal protection payments. And not unfair to suspect that the millions flowing into Trump’s personal coffers have been repaid in the coin of American lives and treasure expended defending the Emirates in Trump’s foolish Iran war. The bottom line is that Trump has repeatedly violated the letter of the foreign emoluments clause, thereby vastly enriching himself and his family businesses, while openly directing American foreign policy to favor his foreign benefactors. There can be no question that the Framers of the American Constitution would be united in finding this conduct both shameful and undeniably impeachable. By 2023, there were an estimated 18.1 million people living with one of these two disorders. What’s worse is that the number is likely to be much higher than that once we factor in other eating disorders. And, disturbingly, those in the field are already starting to see GLP-1 weight-loss drugs exacerbating the issue. Cheri Levinson is a clinical psychologist at the University of Louisville in Kentucky who has specialized in eating disorders for almost 20 years. She says she’s now seeing more patients with more severe symptoms than at any other time in her career, and that many of them are misusing GLP-1s. “This is the worst I’ve seen things,” she says. Eating disorders are mental health conditions in which people’s eating behaviors impact their health. They include anorexia nervosa (defined by a fear of weight gain), bulimia nervosa (in which people binge and purge), binge eating disorder, and others. It’s thought that some people have a genetic predisposition to certain eating disorders, but that some external factor is what triggers their onset. That might be stress, or social pressure to be skinny. Often, simply restricting food intake can trigger an eating disorder, says Levinson. Eating disorders—along with the pressure to look thin—were already prevalent in the 1990s (the heroin chic era springs to mind). To find out how things might have changed since then, Ruhai Bai at the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University in China and his colleagues pulled together data from studies and surveys conducted on populations around the world. The team used statistical models to fill in any data gaps. That research allowed the researchers to estimate the annual incidence of two eating disorders—anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa—for each of 204 countries and territories between 1990 and 2023. They also estimated the impact those disorders had on global health by calculating their disability-adjusted life years (one DALY represents a year of healthy life lost). The findings, published in JAMA Psychiatry on Wednesday, are unsettling. Between 1990 and 2023, there was a 71% increase in the incidence of those two eating disorders, the team found. Those conditions had a huge impact on global health, too. In 2023, they “cost the world the equivalent of about 3.83 million years of healthy life,” says Bai. Bai and his colleagues expect these figures to continue to rise. In 2023, an estimated 18.1 million people had either anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa; by 2050, the team predict, the two conditions could affect 21.8 million people. It gets worse. Those two conditions represent less than half of all eating disorders, says Levinson, who was not involved in Bai’s study. The true impact of these disorders is “probably three times the rate that they’re reporting,” she says. Bai and his colleagues didn’t explore the reasons for this rise (although Bai points out that it can’t be entirely explained by population growth). So how did we get here? Social media may have played a role. “Social media comparisons, influencers, challenges, diet trends can all be precipitating … events for an eating disorder,” says Cynthia Bulik, a clinical psychologist at the University of North Carolina, who was not involved in the new study. Bulik has been studying and treating eating disorders since the early 1980s. “Between 1990 and 2023 there has clearly been an upsurge of those types of environmental exposures,” she says. Many social media users are exposed to filtered and modified images of skinny influencers who may have undergone cosmetic surgery or extreme dieting and who are promoting “thinspiration” or “fitspiration” content. But users with eating disorders appear to be more likely to engage with this content—and to be fed more of it as a result. When researchers at the University of Melbourne investigated this phenomenon a couple of years ago, they found that the algorithms of TikTok users with eating disorders delivered 4343% more “toxic eating disorder videos” than those of users who didn’t have the disorders. They also got more content focused on dieting or exercising. Many were fed over 1000 problematic videos per month. Bulik describes social media as “quicksand” for people with, or at risk of, eating disorders. The covid-19 pandemic—and the social isolation that accompanied lockdowns—also appears to have contributed to the rise in eating disorders. Research conducted in 2021 found that the number of people hospitalized with eating disorders in the US doubled between 2018 and 2020. Researchers don’t yet have good data on how trends in eating disorders might have progressed since 2023. But anecdotally, things seem to be getting even worse. “There are more eating disorders, more severe eating disorders… and we’re seeing earlier ages of onset,” says Levinson. “We’re seeing eight- and nine-year-olds with eating disorders.” “From what I’m hearing and seeing, I am not going to be surprised if that number isn’t at least doubling again,” she adds. Levinson particularly worries about the impact of GLP-1 weight-loss drugs, which have exploded in popularity in recent years. A Gallup survey conducted earlier this year suggested that 15% of Americans had used one of these drugs for weight loss. And they’re becoming more accessible—drug companies are working on new formulations that are both easier to take and more effective, and many people can buy versions of these drugs from compound pharmacies or dubious online sources. In a small study published earlier this year, Levinson and her colleagues found that 30% of people with eating disorders had used GLP-1 drugs. The drugs might benefit some of those people. But 10% appeared to have misused the drugs. And Levinson thinks that figure is also an underestimate. Levinson says she and her colleagues have seen children develop eating disorders after being prescribed GLP-1s for medical reasons. They’ve seen others—even children as young as 11—buy versions of these drugs over TikTok. “We’re very concerned that GLPs will lead to even faster and more extreme weight loss in people with anorexia nervosa, and that patients will come to us in even more advanced stages of illness that become difficult to reverse,” says Bulik. Both Bulik and Levinson are continuing to study the impacts of these drugs in eating disorders. In the meantime, both urge medical professionals to consider the risks GLP-1s might pose to people with those disorders. Bulik contributed to a set of research and clinical guidelines, published earlier this month, that recommends doctors screen for eating disorders before prescribing the drugs, for example. The guidelines also recommend more patient education and more robust monitoring of people who take GLP-1s. They broke out of their test environment and successfully hacked Hugging Face, a site that they believed held clues about how they’d be graded. Around the same time, another OpenAI agent accessed private files from an Australian government website. It’s a problem that’s by no means confined to OpenAI. In the months since, Anthropic, Google, and Meta have also disclosed incidents in which their agents have gone off track. And worryingly, in many cases the agents eluded the very researchers and safety teams tasked with monitoring them. AI agents are taking unauthorized actions, and the companies building and running them are mostly relying on luck and hindsight to mitigate any harmful impacts. They must do more to ensure safety. That’s why EU regulators and US agencies are rightfully calling for agent developers and deployers to monitor agents in real time. But these rules assume that companies can see what their agents are doing and why—when in reality, they lack sufficient visibility. As agents roll out across critical sectors, from finance to government to medicine, we must be able to monitor them in real time to prevent wide-ranging and potentially devastating consequences.  And while safety depends on monitoring, monitoring requires observability: the capacity to capture what the system did, process it into signals, and present those to humans or automated oversight systems. In other words, we need to know what the agent did and why it did it. And as our recent research shows, current agent frameworks—the software that runs agents— do not reliably generate the crucial observability records we need to ensure that agents are deployed safely. Agents present new kinds of problems that generative AI alone does not. They inherit the unpredictability of the LLMs they are built on, but where a generative AI system produces a single output for a human to act on, an agent performs longer tasks—it plans, picks tools, spawns sub-agents, and takes actions. So an agent’s course can take shape in a multitude of different ways, and no tests before launch can fully anticipate the risks. This makes observing the agents while they work even more challenging, and even more important. With colleagues from academia, civil society, and industry, we studied the four most widely used agent frameworks to see what signals they actually emit. We found major gaps that made it hard to achieve key safety goals, like noticing policy violations, unauthorized actions, and even human oversight failures. Key observability requirements were missing—like Agent IDs that persist over time, and records about which humans approved agent tasks. This made it hard to tell which agents did what or assess whether they were taking unauthorized actions. The good news is that these gaps, once identified, can be closed. Doing so starts with capturing two types of records: those that reveal what happened in an agent’s run and how the agent made its decisions. The “what happened” records identify the agent (while preserving privacy) and log how humans intervened and whether agent permissions or “memory” shifted, while the “decision” records show an agent’s reasoning and levels of confidence in the actions it’s taken. Take, for example, agents working to process a bank’s mortgage loans. The system examines an application and recommends denying it, and then a human loan officer signs off on the decision. In a later audit, or perhaps even in real time, a bank or regulator needs to be able to reconstruct which agent made the call, which officer approved it, and how long they took. And the bank must also analyze records of how the agent arrived at its refusal decision in order to ensure it did not use off-limits information, like details about the neighborhood the applicant is from. Recording “what happened” and “how the agent decided” require different approaches, but both are feasible. The current lack of “what happened” records is mostly a software plumbing problem that can be addressed through OpenTelemetry, an open observability standard maintained by engineers across the industry and by the agent frameworks adopting it. OpenTelemetry has launched a working group drafting shared formats for tracing agents, but they have yet to be finalized. The group should urgently incorporate standards for maintaining persistent agent identity and for recording human interventions and changes to agents’ permission or memory. And it should be done before these systems can scale, all the while attending to the privacy implications of agent ID that links to real people. Preserving “how agents decided” records is more challenging. The issue raises open research questions for model providers and researchers: Does the step-by-step reasoning a model displays, known as chain of thought, reflect how the model actually made a decision? And do we have reliable ways to measure how confident the agent is in that decision? These questions need answers before anyone sets standards for how such observability records are used. No single organization or sector can close the observability gaps on their own, but together, they can support the infrastructure required to prevent dangerous incidents and support safety. AI developers building agent frameworks, like OpenAI, Anthropic, CrewAI, and LangChain, must integrate telemetry signals recording an agent’s persistent identity, any human interventions, and any changes to an agent’s permissions and memory. Meanwhile, enterprises adopting these agent frameworks have an influential role to play; enterprises in regulated industries, like banks and hospitals, can put pressure on the agent developers to supply these missing observability records where standards do not yet exist. Enterprises must also define policies, permissions, and roles that set out what agents can safely do, since telemetry alone cannot assess whether agents broke specific rules. Finally, regulators and AI standards institutes must specify the observability records required for high-stakes AI deployments while leaving the technical details to industry. We have already seen initial momentum here: NIST’s agent standards initiative in the US is working on agent identity, one of the most salient gaps we found in our research. No bank would let a thousand unbadged employees loose on customer accounts and hope to piece together what happened when something goes wrong. Yet that’s how we’re deploying AI agents. Making their behavior observable is the first step toward fixing this. And by building the regulatory and policy ecosystem that makes it possible to act on what we see, we can hold organizations accountable. Until then, every task we hand to an agent hands over our agency, too. Climate change itself is increasingly fueling climate change. Rising concentrations of greenhouse gases are driving up global temperatures, setting off dangerous feedback loops that we have not fully accounted for in our climate models—or our emissions-reduction goals. Scientists have long warned that warming would propel further warming in various ways, but a growing number of observations and studies are at last providing a sharper sense of just how powerful these cascading effects could be. And, by extension, they’re showing how much faster the planet may warm in this century. A study in Environmental Research Letters in September, synthesizing the results of previous papers, found that the additional carbon dioxide and methane released from thawing permafrost, intensifying wildfires, hotter lakes and reservoirs, and wetter, warmer wetlands alone could drive global average temperatures up 0.2 °C to 0.4 °C this century. That would come on top of the 2.6°C of warming above preindustrial levels that modelers are already expecting through 2100. The additional heat could subject many of the world’s cities to monthslong heat waves and expose hundreds of millions of people to flooding. None of the models that inform the major UN climate reports, which assess how much the planet will likely warm under various emissions scenarios, account for all these feedback effects. Many of them don’t incorporate any. That leaves a huge blind spot in our understanding of how much the world could actually warm in the coming decades—and how rapidly nations will need to slash emissions to stay within critical boundaries. A separate analysis published in One Earth in March, which examined what the feedback problem means for our international climate goals, concluded that the world may be much further off track than is widely believed. It found that just three regional sources of warming-driven emissions—thawing permafrost, increasing fires in boreal forests, and warming tropical wetlands—may shorten by as much as 25% the time before the planet consistently breaches the 1.5 °C and 2 °C warming thresholds. Our climate models and the UN’s reports clearly need to be updated to better represent these effects. Nations must also step up their efforts to reduce emissions to reflect this improving understanding of the dangers we face, says Sam Abernethy, the lead author of the paper and a climate scientist at Spark Climate Solutions, a San Francisco–based nonprofit research group. At least some of this is starting to happen. Spark kick-started an initiative, called the Warming-Induced Greenhouse Gas Emissions Model Intercomparison Project, through which more than 20 international teams are running the same set of analyses on different models to develop greater understanding of feedback effects’ potential power. Researchers involved hope that the UN will incorporate those results into its next major climate assessment, due out in 2029. That report will update estimates of the remaining emissions that can be released before the world crosses key temperature thresholds. But what is already clear is that the planet is rapidly warming and that nations haven’t done nearly enough to cut fossil-fuel pollution. And even if we did manage to halt it, warming-driven emissions could continue. So a number of the research groups involved in the recent studies have started to explore what nations could do to get feedback emissions under control. Scientists at the Woodwell Climate Research Center in Massachusetts are, for example, investigating the possibility of preventing fires or rapidly detecting them and putting them out when they threaten to burn the vegetation and soil that help insulate particularly carbon-rich wedges of permafrost (a subject I explored at length in a recent feature story). In addition, the Environmental Defense Fund’s Climate Innovation Initiative has backed lab research on the potential of adding sulfate, iron, or other compounds to wetlands to counteract fast-rising methane emissions. Several startups are also exploring possibilities. They’re looking into suppressing the lightning strikes that are igniting more and more fires in high latitudes, placing synthetic mats above permafrost to prevent it from melting, and deploying autonomous drones to quickly suppress potentially high-emissions fires before they burn out of control. Investigating all these ideas is worthwhile.
