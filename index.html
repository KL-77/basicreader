In the future, you won’t be able to use Reddit without logging in. Reddit CEO Steve Huffman confirmed the impending change in a letter (PDF) to shareholders today about Q3 performance. He said that Reddit is shifting “to a logged-in-only experience,” adding: Moving to primarily logged-in will ensure our users have the best Reddit experience possible, and can find the content and communities most relevant to them. It is better for our business because we will have more signals for ads targeting, and being logged-in leads to higher engagement generally. It also allows us to protect access to Reddit’s extremely valuable corpus of data, and helps us better adapt to emerging regulations around the world. Huffman also said that the login requirement will help Reddit better retain new users. Reddit will start testing the login requirement in “select,” non-US markets in Q4. The company will use those tests to decide what a logged-in-only Reddit will look like “globally,” per Huffman. “Pretty much all of the major ad-based platforms have gone through this transition. We are excited and moving quickly,” the CEO added, noting Reddit’s intent to become a “direct daily habit for every Internet user.” The impending requirement comes as Reddit search referral traffic “was down meaningfully in Q3,” Huffman wrote. The largest hit came from logged-out and web traffic, and the overall trend has continued into Q4. “During October, we have seen that search traffic has dropped by more than 1 million referrals on average per week. If this rate of decline continues, current trends would suggest that total DAUqs [or daily active unique users] could fall in Q4, by a low-double-digit percentage from our current base of 130 million DAUqs,” Huffman wrote. Reddit’s average number of DAUqs increased 12 percent year over year (YoY) in Q3. Huffman showed interest in Reddit becoming less dependent on search referrals. “Logged-in and app DAUq are our most valuable users, and they are our primary focus,” he wrote. Reddit has been testing a login requirement on desktop since at least August, per user reports. The login requirement is one of many changes that Reddit has made over the past couple of years aimed at growing its user base of its website and mobile apps that also make it easier to track user activity. Earlier this year, Reddit began testing a requirement that people use one of its mobile apps to access its mobile website. It plans on expanding testing. “Currently only about 13 percent of eligible mobile web users are seeing the app conversion prompt, so this has room to scale,” Huffman said. As of July, Reddit requires users to be logged in to use Old.Reddit.com. More recently, it blocked access to Old Reddit for people who haven’t logged in within the previous six months. Reddit also effectively shuttered most third-party apps by making its API expensive in 2024. Combined, the changes should make it significantly easier for Reddit to understand who uses Reddit and why, which it says will help manage things like AI scraping and evolving search habits. The changes should also help Reddit understand ad effectiveness and gain additional insight into user activity, while making it more appealing to advertisers and investors. Reddit reported a net income of $283 million (a 74 percent YoY increase) off of $895 million in revenue (a 53 percent YoY increase) in Q3. Microsoft is beginning the rollout of a Windows 11 preview update that finally addresses a persistent power user complaint with the operating system: the right-click menu that obliterated years of muscle memory on command locations and hid others behind an additional click. The timing is notable, as the announcement comes alongside Microsoft’s quarterly financial report, which showed a continued decline in the business of selling Windows licenses for new PCs. The preview update, KB5127936, is cumulative for Windows 11 versions 26H2 and 25H2. The update will be a gradual rollout before eventually moving on to a normal rollout for all Windows 11 devices once it reaches general availability. The highlight of the new update should calm annoyed users who have spent years with custom scripts and services in an attempt to shape Windows the way they would prefer it. Users will get a “customize menu” button that controls what commands appear when they right-click their mouse. Users can toggle built-in commands such as Send To or Create Shortcut on or off, rearrange what appears on the main menu, and control which third-party extensions show up in either the main or submenus. The gripe with the right-click menu had been that Microsoft hid familiar command locations behind a top row of icons while moving other familiar commands under the “Show more options” submenu. Now, Windows users can customize the whole thing around their habits. The new preview also highlights a new Windows feature called Cloud Rebuild, a recovery option that can reinstall Windows 11 directly from the cloud, even when an existing Windows 11 fails to boot. Cloud Rebuild will pull the Windows 11 image and needed system drivers directly from Windows Update. Users will not need a custom recovery image or external media to reinstall Windows. There is an important distinction to note, however. Microsoft classifies Cloud Rebuild as a recovery option, but it behaves more like a fresh Windows install than a repair, as existing applications and files will not be included. Those worried about data security can sanitize entire system drives prior to reinstalling. For a full list of improvements coming to Windows 11, including changes to Xbox mode and settings, you can view the official Microsoft release announcement. Microsoft’s latest quarterly report, released this week, paints a less encouraging picture of its Windows OEM business. “Windows OEM revenue was below expectations and decreased 27% and 28% in constant currency, driven by the Windows 10 end of support demand in the prior year, inventory drawdowns by OEM and channel partners and weaker PC market demand,” said CFO Amy Hood, according to a transcript of the call. The broader Windows OEM and devices category, which includes Microsoft’s own Surface lineup, fell 22 percent, per the earnings release. Hood went on to state that “we expect these trends to continue through the rest of the fiscal year.” Recent hardware efforts like the Surface Laptop Ultra and broader RTX Spark platform arrived too late in the quarter to register in the results, and Microsoft forecast further drops before any possible boost from new hardware can show in its numbers. The news for Microsoft isn’t negative all around, though, as its Azure platform saw a 48 percent jump, and Microsoft Cloud revenue was also up 30 percent, year over year—now roughly 70 percent of the company’s $92.3 billion total quarterly revenue.

President Donald Trump has declared the imports of foreign-made factory robots to be a national security threat—ordering new US negotiations with trading partners while holding out the possibility of imposing new tariffs on such robot imports in the future. Issued on October 28, Trump’s presidential proclamation represents heightened government scrutiny of foreign-made industrial robots that could complicate the manufacturing operations of major US corporations that rely on such robots. Those companies include Detroit automakers General Motors and Ford, electric vehicle manufacturer Tesla, and aerospace and defense giants like Boeing and Lockheed Martin. Trump’s proclamation describes industrial robots and machinery as being crucial for producing US military weapon systems along with representing a “driving force” behind all critical infrastructure sectors within the US economy. The proclamation cites an investigation under US Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick as having found that US imports of such robots “pose[] risks to the United States industrial base and threatens the long-term competitiveness of the United States in a sector that is increasingly vital to national security.” The Department of Commerce investigation also supposedly found “unique security and safety risks” that may introduce “significant cybersecurity vulnerabilities that can result in severe consequences to the United States.” Such wording echoes justifications used by Trump’s Federal Communications Commission when it banned new models of foreign-made robots, such as robotic vacuums, four-legged robot dogs, and humanoid robots, in July 2026. But that FCC action had specifically exempted industrial robot arms and other fixed or stationary robots commonly deployed in automotive factories and logistics warehouses. Now Trump’s directive for the United States and trading partners “to adjust imports of robotics, industrial machinery, and their components” aims to strike new agreements on the issue within 180 days of the proclamation’s date. If no agreements have been reached by then, the proclamation warns that the president may take additional actions, like imposing “tariffs, duties, quotas, and fees” on such imports. Any tariffs or other fees would, of course, be paid by the US companies importing such foreign-made robots. That would likely increase the cost of US manufacturing operations at a time when the United States has become the second largest market in using industrial robots, with the International Federation of Robotics reporting nearly 38,500 new US installations of industrial robots in 2025. Major US automotive and aerospace companies often buy large robotic arms for factory floor work from the “big four” robotics makers—FANUC, ABB, Yaskawa, and KUKA—that are headquartered in Japan, Switzerland, and Germany. No US company currently offers comparable heavy-duty hardware for large industrial robotic arms, although the foreign robotics makers have been expanding their US engineering and assembly facilities. Even Amazon, the company that has arguably deployed more robot workers than any other in the world, still uses some foreign-made robots, like Japan’s FANUC robotic arms, despite having significantly ramped up its own in-house robot production and developed certain specialized robotic arms. Trump’s own proclamation acknowledges that US robotics companies do not produce enough industrial robots to “meet national security needs,” and that the United States “relies on foreign imports for most types of robotics and industrial machinery and for their components.” If Trump eventually slaps tariffs on US imports of foreign-made industrial robots, that would at least fall short of restricting imports outright. Although as Intel recently showed, major US companies can still get waivers from the Trump administration to continue using foreign-made robots even when a nominal ban is in place.

Turning a solar power system into its own backup

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-29

Visit Original Website
Rather than directly converting photons into electricity, concentrated solar energy uses mirrors to turn sunlight into heat, which can then be used to drive traditional generating turbines. Up to about a decade ago, there was a case to be made that concentrated solar energy had a bright future, as improvements in heat storage had turned it into a way to generate power around the clock. It wasn’t to be. While concentrated solar costs fell enough to compete with nuclear power, the price of photovoltaics plunged, making solar panels the cheapest source of power in most locations. Currently, if you have a good site to generate solar power, then the economics of PV panels are hard to beat. That has left us needing to build a mixture of other facilities—typically batteries and natural gas facilities—to handle overnight needs or weather-driven shortfalls. But a small company is promising that it can help avoid the need to build a separate facility. Its version of concentrated solar is designed so that a fossil-fueled backup system shares most of the generating hardware with the solar side. Concentrated solar, also called solar thermal, is pretty simple in principle. Most of the site is covered with heliostats, devices that incorporate mirrors and a motorized tracking system that can keep their focus on a single point as the Sun moves across the sky. The point of focus is in a tower, where sunlight heats a working fluid. The hot fluid can then be used to power a traditional steam turbine, or its heat can be stored and used to generate power overnight. There are many potential variations, however: different working fluids and methods of heat storage, trough-shaped mirrors that eliminate the need for a tower, and so on. All of those variants are meant to reduce some of the issues inherent to solar thermal: lots of moving parts, potentially corrosive fluids, and so on. Those issues and the lack of standardization have helped ensure that the costs of these plants haven’t come close to keeping pace with photovoltaics. Without any incentive to generate renewable power overnight, there hasn’t been any way for concentrated solar to compete. Construction of new plants has largely stopped, and a number of sources have simply stopped tracking the economics of it. So it was a bit of a surprise to hear that India had contracted with a US-based startup called 247Solar to build and operate a small concentrated solar plant. While the plant will be extremely small (1 megawatt) and mix in photovoltaics, the company had a unique angle: The same hardware that is handling the solar thermal output can simply be run using fossil fuels if there is a significant shortfall in renewable generation. “Our systems are their own backup,” said 247Solar’s Mark von Keszycki. “During times of low solar resource—long duration, bad weather, whatever the case may be—they’re able to burn a variety of fuels.” To understand how this works, it’s best to step back and understand the entire system being built, which includes details about how the 247Solar team hopes to avoid some of the problems faced by other solar thermal systems and provide a great deal of flexibility in terms of how its systems get put to use. Like other concentrated solar systems, 247Solar’s will be using a field of heliostats. But the receiver—the hardware that they focus the sunlight on—is distinctive, in that it works with ambient-pressure air. (It’s an updated version of hardware developed by a German company.) Air exits this hardware at approximately 970° C. That air can potentially be used directly for industrial process heat or sent on to the hardware that generates electricity. But in most cases, it will be directed to heat storage. Rather than dealing with molten salts and potential phase transition, 247Solar heats up ceramic pellets roughly the size of a golf ball. Depending on the details of the storage configuration, von Keszycki says that the heat from the storage could be used to generate power for anywhere from nine to 20 hours after the Sun sets. Hours will obviously be shorter if some of the heat is used to generate power during the day, but 247Solar plans to pair its system with photovoltaic installations for daytime generation. Wherever the hot air comes from, it will be sent to a heat exchanger. This is designed as a drop-in replacement for a combustion unit on a traditional turbine to heat up compressed air to drive generation. “The heat exchanger does the same job as the combustor did,” 247Solar’s Bruce Anderson told Ars. “It’s in the same place in the turbine assembly as the combustor was: after the compression stage and before the expander.” The initial installation will pair two 200 kW generators; aside from the heat exchanger, everything else is part of a standard off-the-shelf package. This is the point where the system’s flexibility comes in: It doesn’t care how the air in the exchanger gets heated. So, any of a variety of fuels can be burned to provide the equivalent heat. von Keszycki suggested that adding the capability to the system to burn fossil fuels tacks on less than 10 percent of its total cost. The air coming out of the heat exchanger is still quite hot, in the neighborhood of 250° C, and von Keszycki called it “a separate usable resource” that could be directed to a variety of applications, including industrial processes and absorption cooling. Again, if intermediate temperatures are needed, it could potentially be mixed with some hot air coming directly from the storage medium, albeit at the cost of generation. The first installation will be small, with a pair of 200 kW generators, and so will mostly serve to validate the system’s engineering—Anderson said the biggest worries are “unknown unknowns.” But the system is designed to be modular, and the company is already working through the engineering of a 3 megawatt facility that combines multiple units that share a single tower that holds multiple receivers and heat storage. The installation will also give the company a sense of how the system actually works out financially. On a typical grid, there are few incentives for providing around-the-clock renewable energy. And the cost of ensuring that there is enough generation to back up solar plants is often absorbed by other parties. The ability to generate fossil-fuel-based backup power without having to build a dedicated facility can definitely save someone money, but markets aren’t always set up so that it’s easy to extract those savings. So, it’s not surprising that 247Solar has other markets in mind. It’s especially excited about remote mining sites, which typically require diesel to be shipped in, often at considerable expense. The 247Solar system would avoid that expense, while potentially providing process heat that could be useful for some of the ore processing. And powering mining operations without emissions would work well for companies that are required to account for the carbon emissions of their supply chain. Still, there might be a space for this sort of facility on the grid, if it does successfully avoid the need for dedicated fossil fuel plants that only ever run for a small fraction of the night—provided the price and incentives line up.

The U.S. expected 17,000 disputes a year when it passed this medical bills law. It gets over 2 million instead

Imagine you get hit by a car. An ambulance brings you to the nearest hospital. Your private health insurance covers emergency care, but the hospital isn’t in your plan’s network. Before 2022, if you received emergency care from an out-of-network provider or hospital, you could get hit with an extra, often huge bill on top of what you owe as a copay, coinsurance or deductible. A law called the No Surprises Act, which took effect in 2022, was designed to protect people with private health insurance from just these situations. It limits your costs to what you would have paid if the ambulance had brought you to an in-network hospital or you were treated by an in-network provider instead. As a health policy researcher and a former federal employee who helped draft the rules to implement the No Surprises Act, I applaud the law’s success in preventing patients from being hit with surprise medical bills in most cases. However, a small number of out-of-network providers are using the law to extract enormous payments from health plans. This behavior is starting to drive up the cost of coverage for both employers and individuals. In my view, if left unaddressed, these increases will only get steeper. The No Surprises Act was designed to address a troublesome feature of the U.S. healthcare system: It is notoriously difficult to predict how much your healthcare will cost. This is especially true when someone receives out-of-network care. Before the law went into effect, roughly 20% of inpatient emergency department visits resulted in a surprise bill from an out-of-network provider. Surprise bills are common for other types of care too. One study found roughly 40% of air ambulance rides resulted in a surprise bill, costing an average of almost US$20,000. The threat of these bills exacted an enormous emotional and financial toll. Roughly two-thirds of Americans reported being very or somewhat worried about unexpected medical bills, according to a 2020 poll conducted by KFF, a national health policy nonprofit. The No Surprises Act protects Americans from certain instances of a practice called balance billing, in which an out-of-network hospital or doctor asks you to pay the difference between what they billed for a service and what your insurance agreed to pay for it. When a provider joins your plan’s network, they agree to accept a specific payment for a given service or procedure. They agree to charge you only for cost sharing, such as a copayment, coinsurance or deductible, and they aren’t allowed to balance bill you. However, plans can’t prevent out-of-network providers or facilities from balance billing. On top of that, your own plan may also charge you a higher copayment, coinsurance or deductible when you go out of network. That’s why out-of-network care can be much more expensive than in-network care. The No Surprises Act protects consumers from these extra costs in cases where they have little or no control over whether they see an in- or out-of-network doctor. The law prohibits balance billing and requires cost sharing to be at approximately in-network levels in three cases where surprise medical bills were common: If you receive out-of-network emergency care at a hospital or independent freestanding emergency facility. In a medical emergency, you’re not expected to figure out whether the closest hospital is in-network. You can’t be balance billed. If an out-of-network provider treats you during a visit to an in-network hospital or ambulatory surgical center. For example, if an out-of-network anesthesiologist cares for you during a surgery at an in-network hospital, that anesthesiologist cannot balance bill you. If you receive out-of-network air ambulance services. The No Surprises Act doesn’t prevent big, unexpected medical bills in all cases. For example, you can still get balance billed for ground ambulance services or if you see an out-of-network doctor outside of a hospital or ambulatory surgical center. The No Surprises Act is working as promised for patients – as of September 2026, it has prevented approximately 50 million surprise medical bills. But there’s a downside: Employers and unions are raising the alarm that the No Surprises Act is increasing the cost of providing health insurance. What’s driving up costs is an arbitration process run amok. Rather than specifying how much insurance plans should pay out-of-network providers, the law allows providers who are dissatisfied with the plan’s payment to bring disputes to arbitration. Each side submits an offer. The arbitrator must select one party’s offer as the out-of-network payment. To choose an offer, the arbitrator considers information submitted by both parties. Information can vary by case. But the law requires the arbitrator to always consider an amount that is basically the middle amount the health plan pays in-network providers for the same service. Health policy experts like me anticipated most disputes would be settled close to this amount. Over time, experts expected this would put downward pressure on the cost of healthcare and result in payments to both in- and out-of-network providers going down. The Congressional Budget Office predicted these lower payments would reduce premiums for consumers by 1% and reduce federal deficits by $17 billion. As a federal employee, I worked on rules that would have required the arbitrators to treat the middle amount as a strong benchmark. I expected those rules to achieve savings like those predicted by the Congressional Budget Office. But lawsuits from providers blocked those rules from going into effect. Without those rules, arbitrators are selecting offers that are far above what plans would pay in-network. The winning offers are also much larger than what Medicare would pay for the same service. Providers are winning the vast majority – roughly 85% – of these disputes. They are also using arbitration more than expected. The federal government estimated there would be 17,000 disputes annually. Last year, there were more than 2 million disputes, many brought by providers with private equity investment. And the volume of disputes has grown every year. Frequent and big provider wins at arbitration will not put downward pressure on healthcare prices. They will likely do the opposite. When a provider wins an arbitration dispute, an individual patient’s cost sharing doesn’t change. It is capped at what the in-network amount would have been. However, consumers as a group are the ones who ultimately will pay for the cost of arbitration. Researchers estimate the arbitration process alone cost health plans $22.4 billion in the law’s first four years. This doesn’t include the increases in in-network rates that are likely to occur if arbitration trends continue on pace. I’m concerned that to pay for this, insurance companies will increase rates. Employers will need to budget more to pay for health insurance or increase the amount their employees pay. We’re already seeing the costs of arbitration affect the price of health insurance. The New York state employee plan reported that the No Surprises Act was a key driver behind the plan’s 10% rate hike for 2027. A Texas city reported the No Surprises Act caused its employee plan to exceed its 2026 budget by $40 million. Right now, only a small portion of providers use arbitration. If volume continues to grow and providers continue to win big, the cost of arbitration will grow as well – and so too will costs for consumers. With healthcare costs a huge concern for Americans and a major issue in the 2026 midterm elections, the No Surprises Act is under scrutiny. Congress appears interested in addressing this threat to affordability. In September 2026, the Senate Health, Education, Labor and Pensions Committee hosted a roundtable to discuss options to fix the law. In October, U.S. Rep. Frank Pallone, a New Jersey Democrat, introduced legislation to scuttle the arbitration system entirely. Absent action by Congress, I fear that the protection the No Surprises Act offers Americans will come at a cost that ultimately limits consumers’ access to health insurance. Research on political polarization points to how people’s preferred news outlets and social media use create information silos, environments in which people primarily encounter views consistent with their own, further cementing those views. But this research often omits an important source of political information: popular culture, from influencers and podcasts to comedy, television and movies. Politics are intertwined with popular entertainment media. The current president is a former reality television star who was reelected in 2024 with the help of comedy podcasters like Joe Rogan. Blockbuster films such as “The Odyssey” spark controversy and turn into political footballs, while late-night talk show hosts must skirt the Trump administration’s regulatory ire with their politically oriented humor. While we know a lot about the news-consumption habits of Republicans and Democrats, there is less data about the other places that people encounter political ideas. This is particularly true for younger people, who are increasingly likely to get political content from social media influencers and popular culture sources. Political consultant Sarah Longwell of The Bulwark recently told The New York Times’ Ezra Klein that many people in her focus groups get political information from an “algorithm-driven information environment” populated by influencers, comedians and sports personalities. She noted that the “overlap between politics and culture” creates a “vibe” that shapes many people’s political decision-making. As co-founders and research affiliates of Colorado State University’s Center for Democracy, Art, and Popular Culture, we conducted a survey that documents the pop culture preferences of Americans ages 18-49. We wanted to know whether the siloing that has been observed in Americans’ news environments is replicated when younger Americans are consuming entertainment media that often contains political content. That matters, because popular influencers and entertainers who cross partisan boundaries have an increased ability to shape the political “vibe” discussed by Longwell. And an important finding from our survey is that many of the favorite creators and outlets ranked in the survey feature men and promote masculinity. Pop culture is the diverse collection of cultural productions consumed by members of a society: films, television series, video games, art, music, comedy and the like. We asked 741 Americans to name their top influencers, podcasts, comedians, musicians, video games, films and television series. We examined results by age and political party. Respondents first identified which pop culture outlets they engaged with and then shared their favorites in each content category. The answers were diffuse, but trends are evident in the top three listed by people across political affiliations. Both the Democratic and Republican parties have turned to influencers to reach voters – particularly young voters – who “don’t consume traditional political news.” In our survey’s lists of favorite influencers, three creators who rose to fame staging viral stunts, interviewing celebrities and playing video games on YouTube and the live streaming platform Twitch dominated the list: MrBeast was No. 1 for Democrats, Republicans and independents. Republicans and independents ranked Kai Cenat in their top three, while IShowSpeed was No. 2 for Democrats and Republicans. A similar pattern of the same names populating each list is evident in people’s favorite podcasts. “The Joe Rogan Experience” and “Call Her Daddy” were in all groups’ top three, while Republicans and independents also ranked the “Tucker Carlson Show” and “This Past Weekend with Theo Von.” Joe Rogan and Theo Von are comedians turned podcasters, whereas Tucker Carlson rose to fame as a right-wing pundit. “Call Her Daddy” is an outlier on our list insofar as it is hosted by a woman, Alex Cooper, and is known for frank discussions of sex and relationships. The website Campaigns & Elections reported that in 2024 half of all registered voters surveyed who listened to at least one podcast per month said that podcasts had some influence on their vote. The site reported that in the 2024 presidential election, those podcast listeners favored conservatives. Our survey helps to explain why. Rogan, popular with all three groups, has long voiced libertarian ideas and platformed right-wing politicians. Von occupies a similar space, and Carlson is conservative. Both are favorites not just of Republicans but also independents. Political scientists and media scholars have demonstrated how comedy can influence politics. Our survey found considerable overlap among political partisans’ favorite comedians. Dave Chappelle, creator of the boundary-breaking Comedy Central series “Chappelle’s Show,” topped the list for Democrats and Republicans and was the second favorite of independents. Bill Burr, known for comedy that is both cocky and aggrieved, was in the top three for Republicans and Democrats, while Shane Gillis, whose comedy The New York Times says “fits into the evolving meaning of conservatism,” was second and third for independents and Republicans. Video games are another shared pop culture venue where Americans of different political affiliations are encountering one another as they play, via discussions with other players on Twitch and by participating in the comments sections of gameplay livestreams. Researchers have demonstrated that young people, in particular, are exposed to diverse political views when engaging with one another in “online participatory activities that are non-political in nature.” For example, one study examined how a viral trend of “building Trump’s wall” while playing Fortnite spurred political discussions both inside the game and in comments on gameplay videos and social media posts. U.S. Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez demonstrated the political power of the medium in 2020 when she livestreamed herself playing the game Among Us, an online, multiplayer deduction game similar to the popular party games Mafia and Werewolf. Ocasio-Cortez playing the game with U.S. Rep. Ilhan Omar and prominent gamers was billed as a get-out-the-vote effort and earned 4.5 million views. In our survey of video games favored by people of different political affiliations, Call of Duty, Fortnite and Sims appeared on all three lists. Our data suggests that these pop culture spaces are thus not partisan silos. But that doesn’t mean they are free of particular ideologies. Some of the most popular creators – Rogan, Von, Carlson and Gillis – appeal to, or helped construct, the manosphere, a loose collection of online communities that promote masculinity and often reflect misogynistic attitudes. Burr’s comedy is not politically conservative. But The New York Times said that it “gives voice to the aggrieved confusion of the white heterosexual guy adrift.” Chappelle is known for his satirical critiques of racism. But in his more recent Netflix stand-up specials, he voiced anti-trans sentiments that Mother Jones called “indistinguishable from, and grist for, the material of conservative streamers and right-wing edgelords.” Our survey did not ask respondents why they preferred these influencers, podcasts, comedians and video games. Academic research has shown that pop culture audiences don’t passively accept every message a creator delivers. Nevertheless, the dominance of male creators and the prevalence of misogyny and transphobia in their humor and commentary is significant. Whether convened online or in person, the groups that gather via popular culture do not fit into the partisan silos that have formed around news and social media outlets. That makes the gendered nature of these lists even more significant, because the manosphere vibes are not relegated to one side of the political spectrum. Today, Ahn argues, the lion’s share of global venture capital is concentrated in the U.S. and North Asia. Venture capital is a derivative of other money flows: only “a fraction of the fraction of the fraction” of the money that enters a market ever reaches startups, he explains. Companies need to post stellar results to win more VC money—and Ahn fears that the region’s startups aren’t delivering. “A lot of the Southeast Asian companies that have gone public or are about to go public, they’re not showing those results at all,” he says. “If you build something that’s of quality in Southeast Asia, you just get a lot less oxygen than if you build the same thing in the U.S.” Take Grab, perhaps the golden child of Southeast Asia’s tech sector. A report by Lightspeed Venture Partners found that outcomes in Southeast Asia are significantly less capital-efficient—generating only half the value per dollar invested as compared to markets like China. “To succeed as a venture capitalist in this region, you’re going to have to transform yourself into an entity that not only does equity investments, but also looks seriously at other levers like mezzanine or tranche financing, debt and credit, that can help your company grow more productively,” Ahn explains. Ahn’s pessimism on Southeast Asia is a stark contrast to his record. He started Rakuten Ventures in 2013 as one of its founding managing partners, and says his team exclusively focused on Southeast Asian investments. (Rakuten Ventures later changed its name to Rakuten Capital.) “We were lucky enough to catch onto the macro trend of people becoming interested in Southeast Asia’s growth and potential,” he recalls. “Investors like Sequoia Capital were all moving into the region, and we were able to invest in some really great companies.” Southeast Asian startups which Rakuten Capital has backed include Indonesian tech firm GoTo, and Singapore-founded cashback platform ShopBack and hotel platform RedDoorz. “A lot of the investments we made on the first and second generation, they’ve done very well for themselves and exited themselves out,” Ahn says. The midterm elections are now underway across the country and as voters decide who should hold the levers of power in Washington, all eyes are on President Donald Trump. He’s spent the last decade baselessly questioning the integrity of U.S. elections—despite plenty of evidence finding they are safe and secure—with things coming to a violent head on Jan. 6, 2021. This year, as more and more polling is suggesting Democrats will sweep Republicans, even in solidly red districts, it raises the question: Will the president accept the results of critical House and Senate races, or won’t he? “I will, if they’re honest elections,” Trump ominously responded when asked this very question on Tuesday. Since Trump began his second term, he’s been laying the groundwork to try to nationalize U.S. elections and go after “voter fraud” through executive action and lawsuits against states, though most of these efforts have been struck down in court. Yet, the Justice Department has not given up. In fact, in recent weeks the agency filed at least two more dubious election lawsuits against California and Alaska, plus a series of cases against immigrants for alleged noncitizen voting. The legal challenges suggest the administration is planning ahead this time around, planting lawsuits that might create a foothold in the courts for the GOP to seize on if the midterms don’t end up going their way. To Charles Stewart III, a political scientist and professor at MIT who has spent decades studying American congressional politics, elections, and political development, the current situation created by Trump is a test of America’s democracy. “Not primarily because of fundamental shortcomings in American election administration, but because political actors associated with the Administration will attempt to delegitimize or overturn its work,” he wrote in a newly published paper, “The Day After: Threats and Guardrails in the 2026 Election.” Stewart believes that Trump’s efforts to thwart this year’s midterms should be viewed in three distinct phases: Before Election Day, during the vote counting and certification process, and when the new Congress convenes on Jan. 3, 2027. In practice, this looks like Trump beginning to seed baseless claims that losing Republicans can make after Election Day: mail ballots are suspect, voter rolls are contaminated with noncitizens, slow ballot counts are evidence of fraud, and any Democratic victories in close races shouldn’t be accepted. After Election Day, the lawsuits begin, harkening back to the days and weeks following Trump’s 2020 loss to Joe Biden, and finally things might culminate at the Capitol, where lawmakers could be pressured not to certify election winners. I spoke with Stewart to understand how this framework can help us see important red flags in the hours immediately following Election Day, plus the chances that Trump incites yet another angry mob to take over the Capitol. And is there any silver lining to hold on to? Here’s our conversation, lightly edited and condensed for clarity. Shirin Ali: In your paper, you drill down on the president’s efforts to undermine the midterms into three flashpoints: Before Election Day, during the vote counting and certification process, and when the new Congress convenes. Can you explain what each of these are? Charles Stewart III: The ideas behind those flashpoints come from what we’ve seen the Trump administration working on in the second term: two executive orders, activities at the Justice Department and Homeland Security, actions that are beginning to nibble around the edges of the election administration process. Particularly before Election Day, putting pressures on mail voting, highlighted by the executive order from this past March that basically directed the U.S. Postal Service to create a new series of procedures for handling mail voting, marrying that together with directives to DHS to create these lists of citizens. The second flashpoint has to do with pressures on election administration, especially election administrators and the institutions themselves. There, I think the pressures go beyond even what the administration has been doing. Certainly, a lot of the activity of the administration has been focused on the possibility that if election officials don’t do what the administration wants, they will not only bring civil complaints against them, but they may very well bring criminal complaints against them. Then after Election Day, that’s where we’re thinking about getting the final count in. We shift into the formal process of canvassing the vote, certifying it, and making sure that we’ve named the winners. I’m thinking broadly about a potential replay of what happened after the 2020 election, where the Trump campaign lodged a number of lawsuits in battleground states like Wisconsin and Pennsylvania. I’m still anticipating, even more now, that coming true. The final flashpoint is what happens when the 120th Congress convenes on Jan. 3, 2027, particularly the House. And there the question is whether there will be efforts to bar the seating of senators or members of the House who’ve been elected, specifically Democrats who would be the pivotal members of the two chambers that would flip control of the chamber to the Democrats. There’s another obvious parallel with 2020, when there were efforts to deny the certification of the presidential election. How do the Justice Department’s lawsuits against California and Alaska fit into this? Those are kind of jumping the gun on the timeline that I had suspected, because they’re really almost preempting post-election challenges and making them pre-election challenges. About a week ago I was saying, “Well, they’ve filed this lawsuit against California, it’s getting kind of close to the election. If there’s just California, it’s kind of a weak rule in terms of applying formal pressure on the states.” Then came Alaska, and this one makes me wonder what else the DOJ is going to do in the next week to bring early lawsuits against states and kind of do a shot across the bow. The California lawsuit is the sort of thing that we that we’ve long expected the Trump administration to do, and in fact in the first administration, there were rumors that they were trying to put together a national voter database and merge it with immigration records to begin to go after states for having noncitizens on the rolls. This has been a long dream of the administration and people associated with the administration, and it’s taken them basically a decade in order to act on it. It doesn’t look like they’ve done a particularly sophisticated job, both in terms of the actual job of database matching or on the legal side. The Alaska case seems to be the most frivolous election challenge that I can imagine, and in fact it’s kind of orthogonal. It’s unrelated to the things I had anticipated, because the lawsuit they’re bringing is the sort of lawsuit that you would expect a candidate to bring. Really, it’s up to Sen. Sullivan to bring these challenges under Alaska law and then potentially to bring a contest to the U.S. Senate. I only have to believe that the Alaska case is the classic case of pleasing an audience of one; Donald Trump wanted it and he got it. There’s just no way in the world this is going to go forward. It’s sort of like going after James Comey or going after the members of Congress who reminded service members they didn’t have to follow illegal orders. It’s really performative, to please the boss, and hard to take seriously, except the degree to which it’s predictive of what we’re likely to see after the election: the federal government putting the thumb on the scales of candidate-oriented post-election litigation. Next week, immediately after Election Day is over, what are you going to be watching for? I think there will be two things. One will be legitimate, and the other will be illegitimate. The legitimate thing is that we may very well see more losing candidates contest their elections and begin taking legal actions to try to save evidence, within the normal contest laws. Some of it may very well be performative to garner support from the administration or local political actors, but those are the ill-fated sorts of things that there’s a legal framework for doing. Plus there’s a legal framework for judges throwing these cases out, right? And that’s the way it should happen. What I think we need to be prepared for, and I don’t think it’s going to be widespread, are candidates supported by the administration going into court to attempt to impound voting machines and ballots and to remove them from where they’re normally housed. Basically, a repeat of the Fulton County event a year ago where the FBI removed ballots. Those are the things I think we should anticipate, and I think that the Department of Justice may very well take matters into their own hands that are quasi-independent of the candidates themselves. For instance, the Alaska lawsuit was just something that the DOJ did because the president wanted it. That’s a disturbing thing that I think we need to be prepared for after the election because we’ve already seen it happen before the election. That is the DOJ just going into places they don’t like and filing lawsuits. If you think about Jan. 6, 2021, in Congress and leading up to that, there was a whole lot of noise in the political system about whether the vice president could refuse to accept electors and whether Congress could impose their own view on what state electors they should accept, and all those sorts of things. In retrospect, traditional constitutional election lawyers had it right all along in terms of the law. The correct person was elected president, but the political ramifications of that whole episode and Jan. 6 itself were just catastrophic for American politics and certainly transformative in many ways. The law held, the right guy was elected president, but antidemocratic elements were energized and we had a riot at the Capitol. A social movement has arisen in the country that makes millions of dollars based on stoking a view that elections in America are illegitimate. It’s an extreme view, and a minority view even within the Republican Party at that level of extremity, but it’s really been politically consequential over the last six years. I think we could be in an Act 2 of that, and that’s what I’m getting at here. Imagine that this was a decade ago and we’re coming into a Congress where the Democrats were going to take over the chamber and have about a 20-seat lead. You wouldn’t have imagined anything major to come out of any contest, any seat. There might have been a couple of contests and it might have been highly politicized, but they would have been handled in the regular order. Imagine right now if Democrats have that same margin of success. I think we have to assume there will be at least a few members of the House on the Republican side who will make a lot of hay around refusing their being seated, refusing to allow them to vote for the speaker, pressuring the clerk not to accept their certificates of election, those sorts of things. All of those would be illegitimate. The rules of the House are very clear about the clerk of the House accepting the properly given certificates of election for members of the House for the purpose of the roll-call vote at the beginning and electing the speaker. There is a very prescribed way of handling contests in the House of Representatives, and there is no legitimate, legal way for the current Congress to be the judge of those elections. Now, one of the things I think will stoke the political fires, though, is the investigations into any contest that any member would want to bring in the 119th Congress. There will be pressures in the current Congress for House Speaker Mike Johnson to highly politicize those investigations, use them as opportunities to shine a spotlight on California and other states that are not cooperating with the administration over noncitizens on the rolls, for instance. Or on states with high levels of mail voting. It’s going to unleash this political theater in hopes of blowing up regular procedures that are very clear, much more clear than the Electoral Count Act was in 2020, into something that looks like a rejection of this election. And the closer it is, the higher the stakes and the higher the temperature. Despite having serious concerns about what the Trump administration will do in the event Republicans lose control of Congress, you’re optimistic that the 2026 midterms will be resilient enough to withstand a Trump bashing. Why? It starts with the decentralized nature of election administration. When I’ve been asked about whether the Trump administration is going to be successful in overthrowing this election, I’ve generally responded with two words: Feckless—they’ve not been very good at what they’ve done; and capacity—there are just not enough lawyers left in the Department of Justice, or enough immigration agents, there’s not enough of anything to take over the American electoral system. If it’s a matter of mobilizing the federal government, there are more local election officials running elections than there’s the capacity of the Trump administration to go after enough of them. That’s decentralization. The second thing is that we continue to see evidence that the facts-based institutions of our electoral system continue to hold. That includes state and local election officials who are nothing if not careful about following the law. And there are the county boards of elections; we’ve seen general election boards stand by the canvassed results. There have been several states that have passed laws over the last few years reinforcing the notion that the certifications of elections at the local level are ministerial and that the proper place for conducting contests, if something is seen to be amiss, is in the court system, not in the election boards. Finally, I think we continue to see the courts holding to what the law is, holding to the precedents that have been established in the past about what do you do when someone contests the results of an election. There’s established law there, and trial courts have, on the whole, held, and when they haven’t, they’ve been admonished or overturned on appeal. It would be nice if we didn’t have to say, “Well, at the end of the day, we’re relying on the Alaska Supreme Court.” It would be much better if it didn’t have to get that far, but we do still have a robust and independent judiciary that has been willing to stand up to nonsense. Less than half an hour into the first debate between the two candidates for New Hampshire’s open Senate seat, the moderator put his foot in his mouth. “Do you think that’s important, Chris Sununu?” George Epstein, of the Conway Daily Sun, tried to ask one candidate, before immediately course-correcting: “Excuse me—Chris Sununu is definitely the wrong name to use.” It was a mistake that said more about the race than any of the candidates’ answers. Yes, John Sununu, Republican, is running against Chris Pappas, Democrat. But Epstein’s error wasn’t an unfortunate mashup of the two candidates’ names: It was a reference to John’s younger—and significantly more popular—brother, Chris Sununu, also the state’s most recent former governor. (John Sununu has served on New Hampshire’s behalf in both the U.S. House and Senate, but he’d been out of office—and out of politics—for almost 20 years, until he joined the race this year.) If you’ve read anything about the New Hampshire Senate race, you might have heard that this is the one where the Republican is trying to ride his brother’s coattails to flipping a seat. This is reflected in his campaign’s perhaps oversimplified yard signs, the majority of which carry only two words “Sununu, Senator,” prompting plenty of residents to wonder if he’s deliberately skipping listing his first name. Otherwise, on paper, the New Hampshire Senate race has been a bit of a sleeper, despite the fact that it’s a competitive contest in an every-seat-counts struggle for control of Congress’ upper chamber. There are plenty of reasons for that: The September primaries were extremely boring compared to other Senate primaries that demanded national headlines. And unlike other primaries that seemed to point toward where the Democratic Party might be heading in 2028, Pappas’ win hardly felt like a bellwether. He did face a challenger from the left, but Karishma Manzur never put up numbers that made her a real threat, and Pappas beat her handily. Sununu did the same with his further-right challenger, Scott Brown, the former senator who had previously (and briefly) represented Massachusetts. That brought Pappas and Sununu to face off against one another in a Senate race that feels almost like it could be happening back in 2012, with classic establishment candidates from each party promising Granite Staters that they’re the ones who will have New Hampshire’s back in Washington. By many measures, it should be Pappas’ race. New Hampshire may be purple, but it’s reliably voted Democrat for president every year since 2000, and the delegation it sends to D.C. has been blue over the past several cycles too. Despite a bit of panic earlier this fall (“New Hampshire Democrats Sound an Alarm: We’re Losing” blared a New York Times headline in September), Pappas has pulled ahead in polling in recent weeks. But I have become particularly interested in New Hampshire’s Senate race, in large part because I live here—I moved a couple of weeks after Trump retook the presidency, soon enough that my town hall was still displaying the 2024 vote count, when 800 residents went for Kamala Harris and 792 went for Donald Trump. And I have come to think of this race not as a sleeper, but as the flip side of the coin to whatever happens with Abdul El-Sayed’s bid for Michigan’s seat or James Talarico’s impressive attempt to flip Texas. This is a race of establishment candidates in a moment when both the Republican establishment and the Democratic establishment are beleaguered. I wanted to find out what the establishment version of either political party even looks like these days, and whether the most boring Democrat in a midterms year of stars might have something to teach the rest of his party. I also needed to understand whether anyone actually votes a split ticket in our wildly polarized times. Given my own progressive tendencies, I came away surprised by how much close observation of the contest changed my mind. It’s not that this race feels like it’s happening specifically in 2012, it’s just that it has a retro feeling about it, particularly thanks to the candidates themselves. Jason Barabas, a government professor and director of the Rockefeller Center for Public Policy at Dartmouth College, put it succinctly: “You have the first open Senate race since 2010, but you have two incumbents squaring off.” Chris Pappas served in the House of Representatives for four terms before the Dems nominated him for Senate this year, and Sununu, a former congressman and senator, comes from the state’s biggest political dynasty. (It’s not just John Sununu’s brother Chris—the boys’ father, also John, also served as governor of the state, and as chief of staff for George H. W. Bush, with a memorable legacy.) “If his name wasn’t Sununu, I don’t think he’d be in the position he’s in,” Nicholas Germana, a government professor at Keene State University, told me. Germana knows what he’s talking about not only because he studies government (primarily German politics, not American ones), but because, like so many New Hampshirites, he serves in the state government himself. New Hampshire’s House of Representatives has 400 members (though apparently no one sits in seat No. 13), making it the second biggest legislative body in the country, just after the U.S. House, and one of the largest governing bodies in the world. This is particularly silly when you consider the small size of the state—with under 1.5 million residents, it’s split into just two congressional districts. But it’s also a huge source of pride and a cause of political engagement here. While reporting this story, multiple state reps reminded me that New Hampshire was the first state to declare independence from England (six months before the country did), and even if the state is losing its first-in-the-nation primary status (Iowa was always a caucus), being demoted from second to third (South Carolina and Nevada are moving up for Democrats) in this upcoming cycle, New Hampshire will still take its task extremely seriously. Is it people’s political involvement that has given the state its purple nature, with a push and pull back and forth between the parties? Not really. Independents outnumber registered Republicans and Democrats. For years now, the state government has been red—and currently, it’s a united Republican governorship, Senate, and House—while the state votes blue for president and sends a full blue delegation to D.C. Which means that people are splitting their tickets: red for the state, blue for the country. “That’s something we talk about all the time—marvel at, frankly—is how much ticket splitting there is,” Germana told me. That’s partly because you have a much higher likelihood of actually knowing your House representative, which might get you to vote across party lines. But it’s also because there’s some cultural state pride in “balancing things out,” even in these times. New Hampshire had one of the highest rates of ticket splitters in the 2022 midterms, Barabas told me, and this year, 13 percent of voters have already declared a preference for splitting their ticket. (The likely way this will lean is to send Pappas to Washington while the current governor, Kelly Ayotte, stays in power.) But the times are making it harder and harder to do that. These midterms are seeing an active effort from Democrats across the state as they respond to some of the more extreme moves made by the united Republican state government. “Republicans in the state had been able to maintain a brand identity that is different than the national Republican Party—rooted in that traditional view of what a Yankee Republican used to be,” Germana told me. But as the Republican agenda has lurched right, that distance has evaporated. In recent sessions, the GOP here has passed culture-war legislation like banning trans athletes from sports. They’ve also taken more aggressive steps to throttle public schools, including voting in a school voucher program that has activated plenty of independent voters. These days, there’s “no daylight between the Republican brand in the state and the Republican Party nationally,” Germana told me, though it’s worth keeping in mind that Germana is not just an observer—he’s technically a member of the opposition party. All of this makes Sununu’s bid to flip a Senate seat a tough effort, and that’s before you take into account his weaknesses as a candidate. While Sununu says he’s not at all trying to fool anyone into thinking he’s Chris, multiple people told me they only realized which brother was running when the primary came around in September. (Their reaction was not positive—Chris had a lot of cross-party appeal thanks to being “a phenomenal retail politician,” Germana said, which John lacks.) John Sununu’s been out of politics since he lost to Jeanne Shaheen in 2008, and it’s Shaheen he’s looking to replace now. While his absence from politics affords him a certain distance from Trump, when he lost his Senate seat, Sununu resorted to a classic move for the establishment and politicians of both parties: He went into lobbying, something the Pappas campaign has made hay of in both ads and debates. John Sununu was a Never-Trump Republican who supported John Kasich in 2016 and Nikki Haley in 2024. And yet, he finds himself in a difficult position this race, as D.C. Republicans personally recruited him to run, after failing to get his brother to do so. It feels a bit like he’s trying to run this race as if he could be New Hampshire’s very own Susan Collins, a Republican, sure, but a reasonable one who would hold his party to account. The problem is, Donald Trump endorsed him during his primary race, and here, like everywhere, Trump’s popularity is plummeting. Recent polling found that the number of Granite Staters who approve of the president was the lowest ever—just 19 percent said they approved of Trump in September of this year (compared to the 41 percent who approved of Trump in 2018, during his first in-office midterms). People are mad about tariffs and the Iran war just like they are everywhere—but New Hampshirites get to be extra mad because we share a border with Canada and are heading into winter, where fuel costs will matter more. All in all, Collins and Sununu seem to be the last individuals trying to claim that it’s possible to be something of a New England Republican—conservative on fiscal issues while being socially liberal—as Trump’s presence and his overwhelming grip on the Republican Party make the viability of that brand more and more difficult. But even Collins is struggling with this message this cycle, and Sununu is not campaigning in the relentless way she is in neighboring Maine. “Sununu is running a low-key campaign with almost no public rallies, few appearances from Republican surrogates, and limited engagement with national media,” the Dispatch reported recently, and he dropped out of what was supposed to be the final debate. I myself tried to engage with his campaign in multiple ways—attempting to RSVP to a meet-and-greet I was told was closed to the press, emailing the campaign, and even eventually texting his campaign spokesman—all to no avail. The main action seems to be in the form of the millions of dollars pouring into the state, where the GOP does have a clear advantage. (In early October, Republicans had spent $39 million compared to Dems’ $23 million, though both campaigns said they have about $14 million on hand for the final few weeks.) The latest ad features Sununu trying to distance himself from Trump, repeating his line that he’s “never met the man.” Pappas is doing the exact opposite of Sununu. He’s been crisscrossing the state (well, driving up and down—you can’t really drive across in New Hampshire) with bigwig Democrats who are happy to add a stop here in anticipation of 2028. Raphael Warnock, Pete Buttigieg, and Gavin Newsom all came through, and late this month, Pappas was joined by Kamala Harris herself. There are many reasons for that, including Trump’s astonishing willingness to break norms (see: setting tariffs, something Congress is supposed to have control over). Chris Pappas is running to legislate. In 2026, that’s pretty boring. We urgently need to monitor AI agents’ actions in real time

MIT Technology Review | 2026-10-29

Visit Original Website
This summer, hundreds of OpenAI agents tried to cheat on a cybersecurity test. They broke out of their test environment and successfully hacked Hugging Face, a site that they believed held clues about how they’d be graded. Around the same time, another OpenAI agent accessed private files from an Australian government website. It’s a problem that’s by no means confined to OpenAI. In the months since, Anthropic, Google, and Meta have also disclosed incidents in which their agents have gone off track. And worryingly, in many cases the agents eluded the very researchers and safety teams tasked with monitoring them. AI agents are taking unauthorized actions, and the companies building and running them are mostly relying on luck and hindsight to mitigate any harmful impacts. They must do more to ensure safety. That’s why EU regulators and US agencies are rightfully calling for agent developers and deployers to monitor agents in real time. But these rules assume that companies can see what their agents are doing and why—when in reality, they lack sufficient visibility. As agents roll out across critical sectors, from finance to government to medicine, we must be able to monitor them in real time to prevent wide-ranging and potentially devastating consequences.  And while safety depends on monitoring, monitoring requires observability: the capacity to capture what the system did, process it into signals, and present those to humans or automated oversight systems. In other words, we need to know what the agent did and why it did it. And as our recent research shows, current agent frameworks—the software that runs agents— do not reliably generate the crucial observability records we need to ensure that agents are deployed safely. Agents present new kinds of problems that generative AI alone does not. They inherit the unpredictability of the LLMs they are built on, but where a generative AI system produces a single output for a human to act on, an agent performs longer tasks—it plans, picks tools, spawns sub-agents, and takes actions. So an agent’s course can take shape in a multitude of different ways, and no tests before launch can fully anticipate the risks. This makes observing the agents while they work even more challenging, and even more important. With colleagues from academia, civil society, and industry, we studied the four most widely used agent frameworks to see what signals they actually emit. We found major gaps that made it hard to achieve key safety goals, like noticing policy violations, unauthorized actions, and even human oversight failures. Key observability requirements were missing—like Agent IDs that persist over time, and records about which humans approved agent tasks. This made it hard to tell which agents did what or assess whether they were taking unauthorized actions. The good news is that these gaps, once identified, can be closed. Doing so starts with capturing two types of records: those that reveal what happened in an agent’s run and how the agent made its decisions. The “what happened” records identify the agent (while preserving privacy) and log how humans intervened and whether agent permissions or “memory” shifted, while the “decision” records show an agent’s reasoning and levels of confidence in the actions it’s taken. Take, for example, agents working to process a bank’s mortgage loans. The system examines an application and recommends denying it, and then a human loan officer signs off on the decision. In a later audit, or perhaps even in real time, a bank or regulator needs to be able to reconstruct which agent made the call, which officer approved it, and how long they took. And the bank must also analyze records of how the agent arrived at its refusal decision in order to ensure it did not use off-limits information, like details about the neighborhood the applicant is from. Recording “what happened” and “how the agent decided” require different approaches, but both are feasible. The current lack of “what happened” records is mostly a software plumbing problem that can be addressed through OpenTelemetry, an open observability standard maintained by engineers across the industry and by the agent frameworks adopting it. OpenTelemetry has launched a working group drafting shared formats for tracing agents, but they have yet to be finalized. The group should urgently incorporate standards for maintaining persistent agent identity and for recording human interventions and changes to agents’ permission or memory. And it should be done before these systems can scale, all the while attending to the privacy implications of agent ID that links to real people. Preserving “how agents decided” records is more challenging. The issue raises open research questions for model providers and researchers: Does the step-by-step reasoning a model displays, known as chain of thought, reflect how the model actually made a decision? And do we have reliable ways to measure how confident the agent is in that decision? These questions need answers before anyone sets standards for how such observability records are used. No single organization or sector can close the observability gaps on their own, but together, they can support the infrastructure required to prevent dangerous incidents and support safety. AI developers building agent frameworks, like OpenAI, Anthropic, CrewAI, and LangChain, must integrate telemetry signals recording an agent’s persistent identity, any human interventions, and any changes to an agent’s permissions and memory. Meanwhile, enterprises adopting these agent frameworks have an influential role to play; enterprises in regulated industries, like banks and hospitals, can put pressure on the agent developers to supply these missing observability records where standards do not yet exist. Enterprises must also define policies, permissions, and roles that set out what agents can safely do, since telemetry alone cannot assess whether agents broke specific rules. Finally, regulators and AI standards institutes must specify the observability records required for high-stakes AI deployments while leaving the technical details to industry. We have already seen initial momentum here: NIST’s agent standards initiative in the US is working on agent identity, one of the most salient gaps we found in our research. No bank would let a thousand unbadged employees loose on customer accounts and hope to piece together what happened when something goes wrong. Yet that’s how we’re deploying AI agents. Making their behavior observable is the first step toward fixing this. And by building the regulatory and policy ecosystem that makes it possible to act on what we see, we can hold organizations accountable. Until then, every task we hand to an agent hands over our agency, too. Our climate models have a big, dangerous blind spot

MIT Technology Review | 2026-10-29

Visit Original Website
Climate change itself is increasingly fueling climate change. Rising concentrations of greenhouse gases are driving up global temperatures, setting off dangerous feedback loops that we have not fully accounted for in our climate models—or our emissions-reduction goals. Scientists have long warned that warming would propel further warming in various ways, but a growing number of observations and studies are at last providing a sharper sense of just how powerful these cascading effects could be. And, by extension, they’re showing how much faster the planet may warm in this century. A study in Environmental Research Letters in September, synthesizing the results of previous papers, found that the additional carbon dioxide and methane released from thawing permafrost, intensifying wildfires, hotter lakes and reservoirs, and wetter, warmer wetlands alone could drive global average temperatures up 0.2 °C to 0.4 °C this century. That would come on top of the 2.6°C of warming above preindustrial levels that modelers are already expecting through 2100. The additional heat could subject many of the world’s cities to monthslong heat waves and expose hundreds of millions of people to flooding. None of the models that inform the major UN climate reports, which assess how much the planet will likely warm under various emissions scenarios, account for all these feedback effects. Many of them don’t incorporate any. That leaves a huge blind spot in our understanding of how much the world could actually warm in the coming decades—and how rapidly nations will need to slash emissions to stay within critical boundaries. A separate analysis published in One Earth in March, which examined what the feedback problem means for our international climate goals, concluded that the world may be much further off track than is widely believed. It found that just three regional sources of warming-driven emissions—thawing permafrost, increasing fires in boreal forests, and warming tropical wetlands—may shorten by as much as 25% the time before the planet consistently breaches the 1.5 °C and 2 °C warming thresholds. Our climate models and the UN’s reports clearly need to be updated to better represent these effects. Nations must also step up their efforts to reduce emissions to reflect this improving understanding of the dangers we face, says Sam Abernethy, the lead author of the paper and a climate scientist at Spark Climate Solutions, a San Francisco–based nonprofit research group. At least some of this is starting to happen. Spark kick-started an initiative, called the Warming-Induced Greenhouse Gas Emissions Model Intercomparison Project, through which more than 20 international teams are running the same set of analyses on different models to develop greater understanding of feedback effects’ potential power. Researchers involved hope that the UN will incorporate those results into its next major climate assessment, due out in 2029. That report will update estimates of the remaining emissions that can be released before the world crosses key temperature thresholds. But what is already clear is that the planet is rapidly warming and that nations haven’t done nearly enough to cut fossil-fuel pollution. And even if we did manage to halt it, warming-driven emissions could continue. So a number of the research groups involved in the recent studies have started to explore what nations could do to get feedback emissions under control. Scientists at the Woodwell Climate Research Center in Massachusetts are, for example, investigating the possibility of preventing fires or rapidly detecting them and putting them out when they threaten to burn the vegetation and soil that help insulate particularly carbon-rich wedges of permafrost (a subject I explored at length in a recent feature story). In addition, the Environmental Defense Fund’s Climate Innovation Initiative has backed lab research on the potential of adding sulfate, iron, or other compounds to wetlands to counteract fast-rising methane emissions. Several startups are also exploring possibilities. They’re looking into suppressing the lightning strikes that are igniting more and more fires in high latitudes, placing synthetic mats above permafrost to prevent it from melting, and deploying autonomous drones to quickly suppress potentially high-emissions fires before they burn out of control. Investigating all these ideas is worthwhile. But researchers are only in the earliest stages of figuring out how big a dent in emissions any such efforts could achieve across sprawling global ecosystems undergoing massive transformations. The surest path to reducing the effect of warming-driven warming remains the one we’ve known all along: breaking society’s addiction to fossil fuels. Living digital twins are helping industrial plants run more like living systems, and setting the stage for industries that work in sync. Most people associate industrial environments with cold steel and clockwork machinery. Functionally, though, a plant or factory is more like a living thing: It has a pulse, even a metabolism. It goes through wear and tear as it ages, and signs of deteriorating health often show up as barely perceptible irregularities. Digital twin technology has long aimed to mirror that dynamism and model anomalies that may be symptomatic of larger problems. But earlier implementations were a few beats behind physical reality; they were incapable of real-time analysis and unable to proactively address any issues detected. They focused more on simulation, observation and what-if scenario planning, rather than closed-loop execution. Rather than serving as a digital mirror of reality that can spot anomalies or suggest optimizations, a living digital twin combines a dynamic, high-fidelity digital representation of complex operations with the capacity to intervene in the physical world through operations and control systems. AI is at the center of this change. Rob McGreevy, chief product officer at AVEVA, likens newer, AI-infused digital twins to organisms that grow and evolve. These “living” versions are adaptive, evergreen models of a plant’s assets and processes. Because they keep pace with live conditions and connect systems that once ran in isolation, they provide a previously missing layer of visibility and orchestration that better reflects the organic rhythms of the facility itself. Now, agentic AI is also enabling industrial systems to move beyond diagnostics or suggestions and act of their own accord. As the technology progresses toward greater autonomy, it’s starting to develop the reflexes for a kind of industrial homeostasis, in which small, constant adjustments keep a plant or factory healthy and humming. All that said, human judgment and operator oversight still play an instrumental role by defining what healthy looks like to begin with—and flagging when AI systems are falling out of alignment. Living digital twins provide the means for human engineers to monitor what AI is doing and ensure execution is on track. Living digital twins link engineering and design information with real-time operational data through a digital thread, or a connected record of an asset that follows it through its lifecycle. A couple of notable technological shifts have helped string this thread together: Sensors (which have gotten more affordable in recent years) now stream telemetry from IoT devices and industrial control equipment, and software platforms bring that information into user-friendly interfaces. Predictive and physics-based models can also provide AI-enriched insights into how a machine or process might behave. The lenses through which operators view digital twins have evolved in turn. “You can look at the digital twin through the lens of sustainability: What are my emissions?” says McGreevy. “Or, through the lens of maintenance: Is this asset performing in a healthy way?
