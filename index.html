OpenAI models hacked a crypto wallet and breached more websites

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-16

Visit Original Website
OpenAI said internal models hacked two more websites, bypassed company-imposed browsing restrictions, and used exposed credentials to draw cryptocurrency from a wallet it couldn’t otherwise access, in the latest installment of disclosures of its agents performing unethical and likely illegal acts. The company on Thursday issued reports of four new incidents, all of which occurred in June. They come after the company revealed in July that internal test agents exploited a zero-day vulnerability in a tool designed to restrict their access to the Internet. They then broke into the network of AI tool provider Hugging Face. The agents took that illegal action to steal answers to a hacking test that weren’t available when completing the tasks on their own. The incident—which likely would have resulted in criminal charges had they been performed directly by a person—has further heightened concerns that widespread AI use could have harmful effects on an unprecedented scale. In two of the cases reported Thursday, OpenAI agents tasked with retrieving information from websites hacked them when the models ran into obstacles obtaining it directly. In one incident during reinforcement training, a model that received access denials when trying to retrieve historical hospital-bed statistics on a government statistics website “used the site’s file-download feature to retrieve server configuration and application files, including sensitive credentials,” OpenAI said. “It inspected the application code, bypassed the statistics endpoint’s authorization checks, and retrieved data that had previously been denied. The model saved the data locally.” In the other incident, a model instructed to retrieve figures from a public research dashboard bypassed the organization’s security controls and used the unauthorized access to steal credentials. It then made configuration changes on the server. In one evaluation, the changes let the model make an internal search tool relay queries to the public dashboard. In a second evaluation, the model downloaded five non-public tables containing 147,451 rows. OpenAI researchers had prompted the agents to retrieve only five dashboard figures. Even after resetting the setting, the model continued using the token to retrieve non-public data. “The evaluation ended without the model disclosing that it had guessed answers, accessed the organization’s internal systems, downloaded non-public tables, or changed the authentication configuration,” OpenAI said. “All five answers in this evaluation were marked incorrect.” In a third report, the company said a model prompted to update a cryptocurrency trading application used funds from a locked wallet on a test network. The model “derived wallet keys from publicly known developer recovery phrases, found an account with test-network funds, and used that account to pay for the requested contract deployment.” The report continued: “The model questioned whether it had permission, then reasoned that a public recovery phrase and valueless test-network tokens made the funds acceptable to use. The deployment succeeded and charged transaction fees to the account. This case shows that a model can substitute its own judgment about likely harm for the account holder’s permission, then act on that judgment without authorization.” In the last report, OpenAI said that a model bypassed restrictions researchers had placed on the types of web requests it could make. During reinforcement-learning training, the model had been prompted to seek public trade statistics. OpenAI went on: After finding a public yearbook and submitting an answer, it continued probing for another route to the statistics service. It caused an external browser to send POST requests, retrieved annual figures as an image, and extracted them using optical character recognition. It then developed a reusable helper that returned response text through a forwarding service and callback mailbox, repeatedly checked the retrieved figures, and used saved data in submitted responses. This case shows how a model can bypass restrictions on its own tools by getting external services to perform the blocked actions, even after finding another way to answer the question. The model first tried to use an external browser with permission to make GET requests on its behalf, then used JavaScript to obtain the information. The model then used an online mailbox to troubleshoot the technique. Eventually, the model settled on a different method: It asked the external browser to open a web form that sent the trade query to the statistics service by POST. When the browser displayed the response, a rendering service returned a GIF image of the page. The model then used optical character recognition to read the figures from the image. It has become common for researchers and journalists to describe incidents like these as AI “going rogue.” That’s not necessarily true. While prompts fed to the models didn’t instruct them to use the alternate methods for completing their objectives, models are trained to be efficient, steadfastly persistent, and, when possible, to find shortcuts that lessen workloads. Further, most or all of the models had disabled certain guardrails that might have prevented the actions. In this sense, the models performed precisely as OpenAI had trained them to.

US Rep proposes bill to give video game buyers "ownership," not "a license"

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-16

Visit Original Website
A new bill proposed by Rep. Ritchie Torres (D-NY) aims to establish new regulations that would ensure that “buying a video game would give the consumer ownership of the game rather than a license to play it,” including assurances that they can continue to access the games for years after they are released. The announcement names NBA 2K25 as an example of the problem the bill aims to solve; that game launched on September 6, 2025, but is scheduled to become unplayable on December 31 due to server shutdowns. The bill, which is called the Power to Players Act, directs the FTC to take action to prevent outcomes like that in the future. Torres published the following statement on the bill: When you pay for a game, you should own it. Consumers must have the right to own, the right to play, and the right to transfer the games they buy. A game that can be taken away the moment a company pulls the plug is not a purchase. It is a rental disguised as a sale. Families in the Bronx and across the country save up to buy these games, and they deserve better than a license that can vanish at any time. The Power To The Players Act ensures consumers own what they buy and can keep playing long after a company moves on. “I think there should be a simple rule: if you pay for it, you own it,” Torres told Kotaku. He called the new status quo the bill proposes a “restoration to the historical norm,” referencing how most games were experienced by players back in the ’90s. The bill comes at a time when game ownership and preservation is a hot-button issue. Some consumers have been protesting Sony’s announcement that it plans to discontinue releases of PlayStation games on physical media. In its most recent financial earnings report, Sony revealed that digital downloads account for 82 percent of full-game unit sales across PlayStation 4 and PlayStation 5 for the covered fiscal year, suggesting that less than 20 percent of games are still purchased in physical media form. But the argument is that continuing to offer physical discs is not just about satisfying a minority of players’ habits. Players hope it would aid with long-term preservation of games, which are sometimes delisted from digital marketplaces and then become inaccessible. Physical discs don’t solve the problem on their own, though. Many games sold on discs still require online servers to function, and generally, the end-user license agreements make it clear that the user is purchasing a revocable license, not actual ownership of the media, even if the files are burned onto something physical. There are also numerous examples of games where the version that shipped on the disc was meaningfully distinct from the version players ultimately know because of patches. So it makes sense that consumers and their representatives are turning to legislation like this to drive a change in industry practices. There have been activist and lobbying movements, such as Stop Killing Games, that have been advocating for change on this front, but different proposed bills around the world have suggested different kinds of solutions. Some prior proposed legislation on this issue has required developers to keep servers running indefinitely, leading to criticism that those bills’ authors don’t understand the cost. This bill appears to take those criticisms into account—but at least as worded in the press release on Ritchie Torres’ House of Representatives website, it could still face resistance. While the announcement does not say the bill requires online servers to remain online, it requires normally online-only games to offer offline modes of play so players can keep playing long after servers might be shut down. The way some online games are engineered, that may not be feasible, so this could require developers to take very different approaches to some online games’ architecture. This bill’s proposal does not guarantee it will become law; it still has a long path ahead, and most proposed legislation never even makes it to a vote. And since the full text of the bill is not yet publicly available, it’s not yet possible to assess its effectiveness or enforceability. There is another elephant in the room: Large language models and specialized coding harnesses have recently enabled a wave of decompilations and reconfigurations of both classic and contemporary video games and software. These projects are almost certainly currently illegal, but the scale of the movement is such that enforcement may be difficult. It’s possible that these new tools will change the shape of the software preservation conversation in the coming months and years.

Some Ray-Ban Meta smart glasses were recording people’s conversations without the wearer knowing. The recordings occurred without users saying the prompt phrase (“Hey, Meta”), in a bug that Meta says has been fixed. This week, some users reported online that their Ray-Ban Metas were recording without their consent. A person going by Kindly-Audience-137 on Reddit, for example, said this happened to them across three Meta Ray-Bans and that they had the “Respond without Hey Meta” setting turned off. The person said the Meta AI app “saves audio recordings and text transcripts of these conversations in my conversation history.” “I believe this deserves a serious technical investigation. Customers should not have to wonder whether their private conversations are being captured when they have not intentionally activated their glasses’ AI assistant,” the Reddit user said. Android Authority reported the discussions on Tuesday. Later that day it published a response from Meta that read: “This issue has been resolved. People can manage, review, and delete voice logs of their interactions with Meta AI via settings at any time.” Although Meta’s brief message suggests that the bug is fixed, the broader privacy problem remains. Meta hasn’t said what caused the Ray-Ban Metas to record people without user consent. Reddit user Kindly-Audience-137 said they “first noticed this behavior after a recent firmware update,” but Meta hasn’t confirmed if a faulty update caused the bug. We also don’t know how many Meta smart glasses were affected. There are a few reports online, but Android Authority said it was unable to replicate the problem before Meta said it fixed it. Ars Technica sent Meta a message asking what it’s doing to prevent future bugs like this from happening again and how many devices were affected. We’ll update this article if we hear back. Even a handful of affected devices is cause for concern, though. Before reports of this bug, privacy advocates had already expressed widespread concerns about Meta’s smart glasses, as well as those from other vendors. Beyond the fear that naturally accompanies the release of new technology that features a microphone and camera, Meta’s glasses pushed the boundaries of privacy further with their more inconspicuous look, prompting concern about how easy it could be for someone to secretly record another person. Meta has previously tried to assuage concerns by pointing to the fact that its glasses light up when recording audio or video. But the bug this week shows that even with this light activated, it’s possible for the smart glasses to record inadvertently. Meta’s brief statement does little to alleviate those worries. Meanwhile, the bug lends some credence to reports and trepidation that Ray-Ban Metas are creating recordings without subjects’ knowledge. Meta has been steadfast in its push for gadgets that put microphones and cameras in people’s homes and on their faces, while battling fears that the devices could be used for nonconsensual recordings. Contractors for Sama, a data annotation company headquartered in Kenya, say they have viewed footage that was shot by Ray-Ban Metas, seemingly without the wearer or other people in the video knowing. A report by Swedish journalists earlier this year interviewed over 30 Sama workers and pointed to a “stream of privacy-sensitive data that is fed straight into the tech giant’s systems,” per a machine translation, resulting in workers viewing private activities, like a glasses owner’s partner coming out of a bathroom naked. “Meta has that type of content in its databases. People can record themselves in the wrong way and not even know what they are recording,” the report quoted an anonymous Sama employee as saying. In the US, the report led to a lawsuit seeking class action certification [PDF] and an investigation from Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton. In August, Meta updated its smart glasses so that they won’t record if the glasses’ indicator light is covered up. Some potential privacy violations using Ray-Ban Metas, however, hinge on a bad human actor. But this most recent glitch, as well as the reports from the Sama workers, highlights the possibility for well-meaning users to inadvertently record stuff, too. The privacy shortcomings of Ray-Ban Meta and other smart glasses have raised concerns about being clandestinely recorded in public and about the creation of apps for detecting the smart glasses. The privacy concerns have also led to proposed legislation, including in Norway, which is considering a temporary ban. Glasses like Ray-Ban Metas have the potential to intentionally or unintentionally violate people’s privacy, whether through technical problems, sharing data without user consent, or poor design. Without further constraints, one of the biggest drawbacks to devices like Ray-Ban Metas will continue to be the fear of nonconsensual recording, a fear that this week’s bug turned into a reality.

Under Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the Food and Drug Administration is moving forward with plans to ease access to unproven peptide drugs that are popular among members of Kennedy’s Make America Healthy Again movement—despite FDA staff scientists finding that the drugs have not been sufficiently studied to establish safety and efficacy and may cause harms. The FDA submitted an “Interim Access Policy” on peptides for review by the White House, according to a filing notice posted on an Office of Management and Budget website. The posting does not provide information on the proposed policy. But people familiar with the proposal told The Associated Press that it would give the FDA “enforcement discretion” on certain peptide drugs. This would allow the agency to choose not to enforce current restrictions on peptides and provide an assurance to specialty pharmacies eager to start making them that they will not be targeted by the agency. The Department of Health and Human Services, which oversees the FDA, did not respond to a request for comment from Ars Technica. The proposal comes as the FDA is currently re-reviewing a dozen popular, but unproven, injectable peptide drugs. In 2023, FDA scientists determined that the peptides lacked data on safety and efficacy and posed risks. As such, they were stricken from a list of drugs that can be made by specialty pharmacies, called compounding pharmacies, which make customized formulas for drugs. Still, the dodgy drugs have grown more popular, with sellers claiming, without evidence, that the drugs can improve appearance, boost energy, and help heal injuries, among other wellness claims. They’re especially popular among the MAHA crowd; many prominent MAHA figures sell peptide products, and Kennedy, a self-proclaimed “big fan” of peptides, vowed to end “FDA’s war” on peptides upon taking office. In April, the FDA announced that it would reconsider 12 unproven peptides. In June, Kennedy appointed nine new members to a 13-member FDA advisory panel that would review the peptides. Of the new members, at least six have ties to peptide-related businesses and clinics, raising significant conflict-of-interest concerns. In a July meeting, the panel voted to recommend adding six of the first seven peptides considered back to the list of drugs compounding pharmacies can make. The recommendations overruled FDA staff scientists who maintained that the drugs still lack data on safety and efficacy and posed risks. It’s unclear if the FDA will follow through with the recommendation. The AP notes that formally adding the peptides back to the compounding list involves a federal rulemaking process that could take years and require multiple rounds of public comment. The new proposed enforcement discretion could ease access in lieu of that rulemaking or provide access during the process. Science-based advocacy group the Center for Science in the Public Interest (CSPI) is among those strongly pushing back on Kennedy’s efforts to support unproven peptides. The group sent a letter to Acting Food and Drug Administration Commissioner Kyle Diamantas this week, urging him to disregard the panel’s recommendations. “If this conflicted committee’s recommendations stand, charlatans will continue to put unsuspecting consumers at risk of serious injury by making unsupported claims about their [peptides’] purported benefits,” Pete Lurie, CSPI president and a former associate commissioner at the FDA, said in a statement.

Meta may use sweeping new commercial TOS to avoid millions in AI licensing fees

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-16

Legal experts are sounding alarms that Meta will soon update its commercial terms to require business accounts to allow Meta to train AI on their linked website content, not just their social media posts. Kicking in on October 30, the TOS update won’t allow businesses to opt out, instead suggesting that businesses stop using Meta services if they won’t agree to let Meta bots freely crawl their linked content. As Nieman Lab reported Thursday, this will affect all business accounts but is particularly shocking for news publishers who could perhaps charge Meta millions in licensing fees to continually access their articles for AI training. Currently news organizations are fighting a battle with OpenAI over whether such training without licensing is fair use, and Meta’s terms seemingly risk granting it sweeping permissions, no matter which way the court leans. “While journalism isn’t the only industry that will be affected by this update, virtually every news organization has at least one Meta business account, including Nieman Lab,” the lab said. You authorize us (or any entity acting on our behalf) to use automated software (such as web scrapers, bots, robots, spiders, crawlers, and user agents) and create test credentials to access, index, cache, analyze, collect, retrieve, or crawl the domains, web pages, or URLs (and any content available through them) that are associated with your products or services, or that are made available through your or users’ use of Meta Company Products and services, and to periodically pull updates from such domains, web pages, or URLs. Meta may use any content from such domains, web pages, or URLs to provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain the integrity of, the Meta Company Products, services, and features provided under these Commercial Terms and any other applicable supplemental terms. Notably, there is no mention of AI, but it’s inferred from a single use of the word “train” and references to automated scraping and crawling. Meta’s spokesperson told Nieman Lab that Meta has no plans to use the terms as a legal basis to ignore robots.txt instructions that block scraping on news sites. The company still allows news organizations to opt out of scraping and provides instructions on how to block crawling. A spokesperson told Ars that “there is no change to how Meta uses web crawlers; any suggestion to the contrary is false.” However, should courts affirm that AI training on non-news articles is fair use, news organizations question if Meta’s stance might change, and the terms could be used to freely grab access to content by legally overriding robots.txt instructions. Three legal experts told Nieman Lab that it’s not clear if a court would enforce the terms, but they could be “used to argue that Meta can scrape content from news sites and use that content to train the company’s AI products.” Particularly concerning, James Grimmelmann, a professor of digital and information law at Cornell Law School, told Nieman Lab that Meta could “train AI on any content from their sites” and possibly “deploy some of that content, even in competition with the publishers themselves” on Meta platforms. Additionally, Meta just began charging users to post links on its platforms, Nieman Lab noted. So, the terms seem to give Meta a path to first charge publishers for sharing links, then use that cash-for-links program to seize permission to scrape those links to train AI. Matt Topic, a media and intellectual property attorney at Loevy & Loevy, told Nieman Lab that “this is a remarkable provision to bury in [the] terms of service, given that this would give Meta access to material that would likely cost many millions of dollars in a licensing market.” Meta announced the update in a blog, where the company claimed it was simply “streamlining” its commercial terms. In a statement to Nieman Lab, a spokesperson said that “language around crawling was previously scattered across different terms of use, including terms about using generative AI in ads, and this update consolidates them in one place.” “Content owners can still manage how their content is accessed by using robots.txt,” the spokesperson said. However, Ars compared the current terms to updates, and what Meta calls “streamlining” seems to ignore that the new terms use fresh language and grant broader provisions. In Meta’s current commercial terms, there are no mentions of “test credentials” that, experts warned Nieman Lab, could allow Meta to bypass paywalls and automate scraping. “Test credentials” also are not mentioned in Meta’s Product Catalog Terms or Ad Creative Generative AI Terms, which are the other policies that are being combined with the commercial terms. For news groups parsing policies Meta discussed in its blog, this seemed like fresh language that Meta only introduced this month in Self-Service Ads Terms. Asked to clarify if “test credentials” were referred to in any prior policies, a Meta spokesperson pointed Ars to this Seller Agreement, which is not mentioned in Meta’s blog announcing the commercial terms update. That policy requires sellers who use Meta’s e-commerce products to allow Meta to “use automated software and create test credentials to retrieve information from domains, webpages or other URLs you provide,” so that Meta can optimize their listings and content. Just as the seller agreement limits permissions to a specific purpose, the self-service ad terms are limited to users who want to grant Meta permission to scrape their content in order to generate ads. In both cases, a policy of requiring “test credentials” to bypass paywalls perhaps seems less egregious when it’s tailored to a niche business purpose. Legal experts told Nieman Lab that Meta’s purported “streamlining” seems more like the antitrust concept of “tying,” where businesses force a buyer to purchase a second product as a condition of buying another. They likened it to Google’s prior practice of requiring websites to opt in to scraping for AI Overviews if they wanted their websites to be indexed for general search. (Google has since started allowing opt-outs.) Previously, the only mention of crawling or bots was in Meta’s Ad Creative Generative AI Terms, and like the new ad policy, that policy also served a limited purpose. They specify that businesses consent to scraping only in connection to ad content, not for improving all of Meta’s products: We or any entity acting on our behalf may access, index, cache, analyze, or crawl (including through use of automated software) domains, webpages or URLs provided by you for your use in connection with your ads. If you provide URLs in connection with your Ad Content, you grant us and any entity acting on our behalf the right to access, index, cache, analyze, or crawl the domains, webpages, URLs and any content available through such URLs, and to periodically pull updates from such URLs, for your use in connection with your ads or for the purpose set forth in these Terms. In Meta’s blog, the company insisted that “these changes reduce overlap between our agreements and improve the clarity of the terms that apply to your use of our products.” Nieman Lab is now looking to talk to aggrieved news publishers about the terms to find out if the update might trigger a widespread scale-back of news organizations’ participation in Meta platforms. At least one Meta user—Stephen Beale, the founder and editor of The Steampunk Explorer—who depends on Facebook for traffic, is already considering quitting the platform over the update. He told Nieman Lab that he knows that his website has already been “scraped to death,” but the TOS update seemed like “such an arrogant, in-your-face move on” Meta’s part. “It just makes me angry, and I don’t want them to get away with it,” Beale said. Vaping Strongly Linked to Lung Cancer

Visit Original Website
If you’re one of the over 100 million people around the world who regularly hits the vape, you might want to think about ways to start cutting back. According to some recent research by scientists from Seoul National University and published in Nature Medicine, the use of non-tobacco devices like vapes and e-cigarettes to help quit smoking carries a strong link with the development of lung cancer. The researchers analyzed anonymous health data from over 4.5 million Korean adults. They were split into three main groups: current smokers, short-term quitters, and long-term quitters. They were then categorized by whether they vaped daily during a 2018 screening in the Korean National Health survey, with adjustments to control for factors like age, sex, and prior health conditions. In all, scientists found that those who had turned to vaping after quitting traditional cigarettes developed lung cancer at a rate about 56 percent higher than those who quit entirely. Alarmingly, incidents of lung cancer death among vape users nearly doubled, with a lung cancer-specific death rate 91 percent higher than non-vape users. Of course, there’s an important group that falls outside the bounds of the study: anyone who got hooked on vaping without trying cigarettes first. It’d be fascinating to learn more about their fates, but the implication of the study — that vaping is turbocharging the chances of cancer among former smokers — is almost certainly cause for alarm. While it may be easy for non-smokers to roll their eyes at the findings, it’s important to note that empirical data on the relationship between vaping and lung cancer remains relatively scarce. “Although epidemiological and biological studies support the association between e-cigarettes and various respiratory disorders, evidence regarding the relationship between e-cigarettes and lung cancer risk remains limited,” the researchers wrote. It’s an observational study, meaning that it can’t establish true causality. Going forward, more research is needed to say for sure whether there’s a causal association between vaping and cancer — but the early data doesn’t look pretty. More than 5 million people around the world with age-related macular degeneration (AMD) experience atrophy in their retinas. And until now, no treatment could restore their vision. The PRIMA system, which was approved in Europe in July and is awaiting FDA clearance in the US, changes that. It combines a retinal implant with camera-­equipped eyeglasses to improve sight in patients who develop a blind spot in the middle of their vision due to advanced AMD. After training sessions, more than 80% of recipients were able to read, do crosswords, and use their phones. Developed by the California-based Science Corporation, the system takes advantage of the fact that AMD doesn’t damage all retinal circuitry; only photoreceptors—the cells that convert light into an electrical signal—die off. PRIMA’s implant mimics those cells and transmits visual information directly to healthy eye circuitry and on to the brain. “We’re leveraging all of the body’s own natural functions to process and transmit the information,” says Frank Brodie, an ophthalmologist at the University of California, San Francisco, and Science Corp.'s chief medical officer for ophthalmology. Glasses outfitted with a miniature front-facing camera capture what someone is seeing in real time. The video feed is roughly five megapixels running at 30 frames per second, which is similar to TV. The raw video is transmitted to a connected processor via a fiber-optic cable. A small, handheld processor, which can be set on a table or tucked in a pocket, converts the video feed into infrared light signals that route back to the glasses. The device also includes physical buttons that let the wearer adjust contrast, brightness, and zoom. A laser projector on the glasses faces the wearer and shines pulses of near-infrared light directly at the eye at 880 nanometers—an invisible wavelength. This signal passes through the pupil and on to the retinal implant in the back of the eye. The implant is made up of 378 pixels, each with an electrode and two photovoltaic cells, which do double duty. They convert the light into a current that healthy bipolar and ganglion cells pass on to the brain. They also act as solar cells to power the chip, eliminating the need for a battery.
