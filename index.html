PHP warns it plans to release 2 emergency patches imminently

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-21

Visit Original Website
Website admins, brace yourselves. Fixes for what are believed to be uncommonly severe vulnerabilities are scheduled to drop in a little more than 12 hours for PHP, a programming language that powers websites and Web applications everywhere. PHP maintainers said Monday that they planned to release patches for two vulnerabilities, both with severity ratings of 9.2 out of a possible 10. “All PHP users are encouraged to prepare now so they can upgrade as soon as the release is available,” the advisory stated. “We reserve the right to release earlier should details of either vulnerability become public, or should evidence of active exploitation appear in the wild.” The maintainers published the advisory following the leaking of purported details, which have yet to be confirmed. The report showed a video of an exploit tool dubbed PHP2Shell executing code on webservers running WordPress 7.1.3, MediaWiki 1.46.2, phpBB 3.3.17, Magento 2.4.9, Joomla 6.2.0, Laravel 13.36.0, Laravel 8.83.29, Symphony 8.1.9, Drupal 11.4.10, and WHMCS 9.0.9, all of which use PHP. A video depicting the tool has been reposted here. Attempts to locate the unconfirmed report were unsuccessful, but security firm watchTowr said it claimed to affect every PHP version from PHP 5, released in 2004, onward. The security firm went on to say it has confirmed that the vulnerabilities, tracked as CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, are real. Researchers from separate security firm Sansec said they “expect mass attacks to start within 15 minutes of the release.” A third security firm, Webfence, also urged admins to prepare now. Huntress has also issued an advisory. Preparation steps include identifying who is responsible for managing PHP inside an organization, taking an inventory of every app built on the language, planning a maintenance window starting at 14:00 UTC with contingency plans for earlier, making a backup of site and database files, and monitoring the PHP.net homepage for any late-breaking announcements, including an earlier release time. Some experts suggest unplugging any non-essential systems until they’re patched. As an open source scripting language for building dynamic websites and web apps, PHP has come to be a de facto building block for the Web. It runs multiple CMS apps in addition to WordPress and also powers many internal tools. One security researcher said an estimated 1.3 million Internet-connected hosts are likely affected. Vulnerabilities with the severity the advisories are describing have the potential to inflict major damage across the Internet. Admins should remain aware that the patches can be released at any moment and remain vigilant until they do.

Elon Musk said Tuesday that he would like to see the consolidation of his business empire by formally merging his two largest companies, SpaceX and Tesla. “Do I think it would be a good idea? Yes,” Musk said of a potential merger. He was speaking during a question-and-answer period as part of SpaceX’s third-quarter earnings call. Musk said, ultimately, it not his decision. “But at the end of the day the shareholders have to be supportive of this.” Musk’s talk of a merger comes after a busy year in which SpaceX already acquired Musk’s artificial intelligence firm, xAI, in February. In June, SpaceX went public with the largest initial public offering on record. At the request of shareholders of each company, Musk said both SpaceX and Tesla have formed special committees of “independent directors” to discuss the idea of a merger. “I’m a conflicted party; I can’t actually be in the discussions of the special committees,” Musk said. Those committees have not reached an agreement on the terms of a merger transaction, Musk said. “Obviously there’s no guarantee that they will, but I do think there’s a lot of merit to combining the companies as we are pretty much in the superintelligence singularity,” Musk said. “There’s lots to be gained from cooperating on developing superintelligence software, chip design, the Terafab, obviously robotics, mass to orbit with the Starmind constellation, and connectivity. So I think it would be a good idea.” It might seem like madness to merge a space business with an automotive business, but both SpaceX and Tesla are rapidly transitioning from their original markets into artificial intelligence. In its public financial documents, SpaceX has indicated that the bulk of its future revenues are likely to come from orbital data centers that will provide the backbone of computing for artificial intelligence on Earth. Tesla sees its future in sophisticated self-driving cars, robots, and industrial hardware. Both enterprises would require advanced computer chips and energy. SpaceX could provide the connectivity for Tesla hardware. Beyond these synergies, the merger would be attractive to Musk—already the world’s richest person and first trillionaire—because it would give him more control. Based on SpaceX documents filed as part of its IPO, Musk holds more than 82 percent of voting power in SpaceX. In contrast, Musk holds about 20 percent of voting power at Tesla. By merging SpaceX with Tesla, where he has less direct control, Musk could acquire a majority of shares eligible for voting at the combined company. This would depend on the nature of valuations and negotiated agreements at the time of the merger. At the time of Musk’s announcement, SpaceX and Tesla had market valuations of about $2 trillion and $1.5 trillion, respectively. The merger will face scrutiny by US regulators. Additionally, Morningstar reported earlier this year that valuation disagreements will likely be significant, especially among Tesla shareholders. Musk said Tuesday that 50 percent of all Tesla shareholders, not just those with voting shares, would have to approve of the merger. Such a vote would only come if the two special committees reached an agreement on merger terms. Some political leaders, already wary of Musk’s outsized power and influence and willingness to use both freely, may also object to a single Musk mega-corporation. It’s not definitive what the merger’s implications are for SpaceX’s traditional space business and its longtime goal of settling Mars, or for Tesla’s one-time aspirations to bring electric vehicles to the global market. Most probably, however, a combined Musk-universe would focus on artificial intelligence and the computing power needed to back those ambitions. Less Mars and cars—and more silicon.

Outrage flared again after a Rolling Stone story earlier this month claimed the CDC set aside another $1.6 million in funding in late September and planned to move forward with the trial. For now, the trial is not moving forward; Guinea-Bissau officials have not signed off on the trial. In a press conference last week, the country’s health minister, Quinhin Nantote, confirmed that the government had not yet authorized it. “Any scientific research initiative involving human beings, particularly children, ‌must ⁠strictly comply with applicable national legislation, international ethical principles, competent evaluation and approval mechanisms,” Nantote said, according to Reuters. Meta drops the word "train" from its commercial TOS after AI backlash

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-20

Visit Original Website
Hoping to quell criticism, Meta has dropped the word “train” from its “streamlined” commercial terms of service that kick in on October 30. Last week, Meta faced backlash after legal experts warned that the tech giant’s sweeping new commercial terms of service seemed to give Meta permission to endlessly scrape websites linked on business pages. Talking to Nieman Lab, experts suggested that Meta could possibly use the policy to avoid millions in licensing fees from news organizations by requiring all business users to share a wealth of AI training data simply for running a business page. A Meta spokesperson confirmed that Meta did not alert users to the changed language following backlash. This potentially only adds to confusion ahead of the policy rollout. In response to backlash, Meta’s spokesperson provided a statement to Ars, acknowledging Nieman Lab’s initial report but not its follow-up report reiterating experts’ concerns: “We’ve heard you and want to clarify: the crawling terms we consolidated only applied to our ads and business products, and we’ve clarified that in the terms themselves. It does not change how Meta uses web crawlers. As always, content owners can use robots.txt to signal their preferences. This is not about scraping news sites, and these updates don’t give us new rights that supersede those preferences.” The same experts who sounded the initial alarms were not impressed by Meta’s updates, which they told Nieman Lab do not go far enough to protect business page users from invasive scraping. Matt Topic, a media and intellectual property attorney at Loevy & Loevy, told Nieman Lab that “publishers should remain concerned.” “The revised version of the new terms still allows Meta to use that scraped content to develop any ‘business product,’” Topic said. “That doesn’t seem to be a defined term, and presumably Meta is developing AI business products. I think ‘develop’ is broad enough to include ‘train.’” Initially, Meta’s terms authorized Meta to use “any content” from websites posted on business pages “to provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain the integrity” of any Meta products, services, or features. The updated terms delete the word “train” and no longer refer to “any content.” Instead, they allow Meta to “periodically pull updates” from business users’ websites in order to “provide, develop, improve, research, and maintain advertising and business products,” as well as to help Meta “maintain the integrity and security” of its products. This scraping, Meta says, is for users’ benefit, feeding into Meta research into products to “help you create and deliver ads and other commercial content, provide users information about your products and services, and connect you with users who may be interested in your products and services,” the policy says. Following tweaks, it’s good that Meta added “more language about the permitted uses,” James Grimmelmann, a professor of digital and information law at Cornell Law School, told Nieman Lab. But “[the] ‘develop…advertising and business products’ [language] is still broad enough to drive a truck through,” he said. Ultimately, Grimmelmann agreed with Topic that “this does nothing to restrict the ‘associated with your products or services’ [language] that would let Meta scrape entire sites.” What would help—as University of Wisconsin law professor Benjamin Sobel told Nieman Lab—would be if Meta added specific language to the policy where the company agrees to respect scraping instructions in robots.txt files. Since complying with robots.txt is voluntary, Meta could possibly rely on the legally enforceable TOS omitting mentions of robots.txt to scrape sites that attempt to block scraping via robots.txt, experts suggested. When it comes to robots.txt, “you can still express the preference, and unless it’s a legally enforceable preference, it can still be ignored all the same,” Sobel said. Criticism goes beyond Nieman Lab’s reporting, as news groups globally urge regulators to intervene and block Meta’s controversial terms from kicking in. A chief executive for the United Kingdom News Media Association, Theo Bamber, accused Meta of a “shameless land grab” relying on the “worst kind of arrogant tech platform bullying.” Bamber urged UK regulators to “stand up for British creators and the business community” by “preventing Meta from setting this dreadful precedent.” Meanwhile, the European Publishers Council has already asked the European Commission to intervene. “Publishers must be able to decide whether and how their journalism is used, and negotiate fair payment,” the group’s executive director, Angela Mills Wad, said. “Burying such far-reaching claims in platform terms is no substitute for consent.” Similarly, Danielle Coffey, the president and CEO of the News/Media Alliance—which Nieman Lab noted is the largest news publishing trade association in the US—protested the terms as “outrageous and probably illegal.” In the US, Coffey urged regulators to recognize that Meta’s “decision to withhold access to its audience unless businesses allow Meta to scrape content from their websites is an appalling, bad-faith policy, and a clear abuse of the company’s market power.” “It cannot be allowed to be implemented on October 30,” Coffey said.

The way we consume energy is changing. Abu Dhabi’s integrated system has already embraced that change

Fortune | FORTUNE | 2026-10-21

Visit Original Website
Across the global energy sector, a quiet but significant shift is underway. The debate around energy usage is intensifying because the pressures on it are becoming more real: grid queues, water resource pressures, increased industrial electrification and the need for reliable power at every hour of the day to match changing demand patterns. Recent strategy resets among global energy majors BP, Shell and ExxonMobil illustrate a greater focus on delivering reliable supply and ensuring that infrastructure can meet fast-growing power demand. The International Energy Agency (IEA) expects global electricity demand to grow at an average annual rate of 3.6% between 2026 and 2030. That represents an acceleration of around 50% from the average pace of growth over the past decade. Global data center electricity consumption is already spiking, driven in particular by the rapid expansion of AI-focused facilities. That trend shows no signs of slowing down. The IEA estimates that data center electricity demand rose 17% in 2025, with AI-focused facilities seeing demand skyrocket by around 50%. AI may be digital in use, but it is deeply physical in its requirements. It needs land, energy grids, clean power, water, cooling and flexible supply. That is why the question facing governments and the energy industry is changing. As AI and other sources of electricity demand grow rapidly, the question is no longer which source of energy will grow fastest or which project will attract the most attention. The more practical question is how do we build the right energy mix in order to provide reliable, affordable and progressively lower-carbon power under increasing pressure? A solar farm or desalination plant does not, in itself, create a national advantage. It becomes an advantage when it is integrated into a diversified and synchronized ecosystem. Here in Abu Dhabi, we have been building that ecosystem for more than five decades. We have developed an energy and water architecture in which the right energy mix delivers secure, sustainable and affordable energy, working in harmony with grid planning, water desalination and reuse, and underpinned by progressive regulation and long-horizon capital. Our approach is not to choose between energy security and sustainability, but to remove that false trade-off through system design, built on four elements. The first of these is responsible hydrocarbon production. Oil and gas will remain important to global energy security for years to come, especially in sectors where alternatives need time to scale. The question for producers is therefore becoming more focused: how can these resources be produced and used more efficiently? The performance of hydrocarbons must be considered as part of a wider system that is simultaneously scaling clean electricity input and improving efficiency across the value chain. Murban crude, produced by the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC), has a carbon intensity less than half the global industry average. Meanwhile, the use of clean grid power through ADNOC’s partnership with Emirates Water and Electricity Company (EWEC) is helping to further reduce its emissions profile. The second element is delivering clean, reliable power at scale, especially for fast-growing economies. Solar can scale quickly where the resource is strong. Nuclear can provide continuous low-carbon electricity. Gas can support flexibility as demand grows. The challenge is integrating these resources into a power system that can consistently meet the needs of households, industry and critical infrastructure every hour of the day. Abu Dhabi’s experience shows how long-term planning can deliver a diverse, resilient energy mix. EWEC has met more than 80% of the emirate’s total power demand using renewable and clean energy from solar and nuclear sources. The Barakah Nuclear Energy Plant provides a large-scale clean baseload, while Masdar’s global renewable energy portfolio has reached 65GW and is set to increase to 100GW by 2030. Together, these assets show how clean power can become a core part of a balanced and secure energy mix. The third element is water. A data center needs electricity, but it also needs water for cooling. A desalination plant provides water, while also creating energy demand. Treating these as separate challenges increases cost and complexity over time.  In arid regions, it is also one of the clearest tests of whether resilience has been designed into the system from the outset. For Abu Dhabi, water security is part of the same economic platform as energy security. We have built resilience through diversified desalination, energy demand management and long-term infrastructure planning. This is increasingly important as water scarcity places pressure on communities and infrastructure, even in traditionally water-secure regions, leading to restrictions and, in some cases, constraints on power generation. Our experience shows the value of planning for these pressures before they constrain growth. As the United Nations convenes its Water Conference in Abu Dhabi in December this year, co-hosted by the UAE and Senegal, it will be a timely opportunity to reinforce the connection between water security and energy infrastructure, technology and long-term investment. The fourth element is capital with a long-term horizon. Energy systems require investment cycles measured in decades. Generation and storage, grids, and digital infrastructure all depend on capital that can look beyond short-term market conditions. They also require forward-looking regulation, agile planning and commercial foresight to move together successfully. This is becoming especially important as the AI economy expands. AI infrastructure will compete on the speed and reliability of the power and water systems behind it. The UAE-US AI Campus, with a planned 1 GW cluster in Abu Dhabi, shows how energy, water, and digital infrastructure are becoming part of the same competitiveness equation. Abu Dhabi’s Energy and Water Resilience Framework strengthens how we anticipate risk across infrastructure, governance, technology and operations, so that essential systems can continue to perform as conditions change. Such resilience is not separate from competitiveness. It is part of what gives economies the confidence and capacity to keep growing through disruption. Today, governments and investors are increasingly looking at how infrastructure can keep pace with demand in the decades to come. In that sense, Abu Dhabi’s model is relevant beyond the UAE. The opportunity now is to build on these lessons through closer cooperation across governments, industry, technology and capital in order to create energy systems that can perform reliably as demand and complexity continue to grow. Some of Formula 1’s most populat drivers are already serious about photography. Lewis Hamilton and Lando Norris have both been seen with Leica cameras, and Norris runs a separate Instagram account for his photos. That overlap goes well past the drivers, or even the intricate pieces that goes into both cars and cameras, says Mike Giannattasio, president of Leica Camera North America. “There is such synergy between people who collect cameras and people who collect cars,” he told Fortune. “If you see people who are passionate about vintage cars or racing cars, and if they have anything other than a passenger in the seat next to them, eight out of 10 times it’s going to be a Leica camera.” To Giannattasio, that made a partnership an easy call. On Tuesday, the German camera maker and Formula 1 announced a global licensing deal, Leica’s first official tie-up with the sport. It covers F1 as a whole rather than a single team and includes a special-edition camera, a gallery show in Los Angeles, and events at the United States Grand Prix in Austin this weekend. Not everyone saw the workings of a partnership right away. When Giannattasio described the deal to someone he was meeting with, the reply was, “Mike: F1 and Leica? That’s a stretch.” He disagreed, and his case comes down to how both are made. Car and camera manufacturing both involve intricately crafted pieces put together into something the owner will spend countless hours learning. Each time a shutter fires, and each time a tire squeals, involves countless pieces working together to deliver a smooth outcome. “When you look at the mechanics of a camera and you look at an M system, inside just a rangefinder, there are over 150 pieces that make the camera work,” Giannattasio said. An F1 team spends months engineering and testing for a race that can be decided within a fraction of a second. Giannattasio compared that to French photographer and artist Henri Cartier-Bresson’s idea of “the decisive moment,” the instant a photographer has to be ready for. “Both photography and racing depend so much on preparation,” he said. “While Formula 1 goes at incredible speed, and it may be really hard to stop the moment, photography helps someone who loves that craftsmanship and excellence stop the moment, so that it can be examined, remembered, celebrated.” Both brands also sell their history. F1 has raced since 1950. Leica still makes film cameras, including the MP and M-A, which have no digital parts. “You can bring an MP or an M-A into the most extreme weathers in the Antarctic, and because there’s nothing digital, the camera will work,” he said. “It won’t freeze. It won’t break.” But he said history only counts if it pushes the product forward: “It’s heritage, but it’s not heritage for heritage’s sake,” he said. “It’s heritage for innovation’s sake.” So what goes into making an F1-driver-approved carbon fiber camera? The result is the Leica Q3 43 Formula 1 Edition, which sells for $9,950. It is the first Leica camera to use carbon fiber, the material that defines modern race cars because it is light and rigid. The carbon covers the grip, the thumb rest, and the soft-release button, the spots a photographer touches most. Giannattasio said the material had to be adapted so the camera still handled the way photographers expect, and that it couldn’t stop looking like a Leica. “You would ask a little child to draw a camera,” he said. “It’s going to be a rectangular box with a little round circle. It is literally quintessential.” “We had to make a product that was quintessentially iconic, immediately recognizable as a Leica camera,” he added. “But yet we had to take the design cues and materials that are big in the world of F1.” The body is wrapped in a black covering modeled on a racing steering wheel, and the red accents on its buttons come from the same place: F1 steering wheels color-code their controls so drivers can find them quickly. Giannattasio, who grew up in Astoria, Queens, came to Leica from luxury goods, not cameras. He spent more than a decade at Montblanc, rising to president and CEO of Montblanc North America, and earlier worked on Christian Dior watches at LVMH. When he interviewed to run Leica’s North American business in 2020, he said, he was likely the only candidate with that background. “My counterparts were from the electronics or from the camera business and very large companies within that world,” he said. Once he started, staff kept testing his knowledge of aperture and ISO. “What I always said to people is, I love art, I love photography, I love culture, and I am here because I’m passionate about building this organization around those principles, not because I am the best photographer who interviewed for the role,” he said. Despite his résumé, he doesn’t think of Leica as being part of that world: “We don’t by any means consider ourselves a luxury brand. We are a premium brand, meaning we make the best optical equipment in the world.” What sets Leica apart, he said, is what it asks of the owner. With most luxury brands, money is the only way in. A Leica, he said, also takes time and intellect. “You need time in order to develop this relationship with your camera,” he said. “While we don’t position ourselves as a luxury brand, what we do in today’s world is quite luxurious.” He sees that same pull behind a renewed interest in analog products, and he ties it to technology moving faster around us. “In a world that continues to advance with artificial intelligence, I think [there is a] need to slow down to celebrate and be in the moment,” he said. “Whether it’s film, whether it’s books, whether it’s a writing instrument, there’s a real resurgence for things that are real and deliberate.” Catherina covers markets, the economy, energy, tech, and AI. © 2026 Fortune Media IP Limited. Just over a decade after Elizabeth Holmes threatened him with lawsuits and tried to kill his reporting, author John Carreyrou has become a perhaps unlikely fan of “You Can See Everything,” a new documentary exploring the disgraced founder’s home life as she prepares for prison—in fact, he’s seen it twice. Carreyrou is the author of the 2018 book “Bad Blood,” in which he reports on Theranos’ collapse and Holmes’ downfall in a captivating narrative that topped the New York Times’ bestseller list. The new documentary, in which comedian Nathan Fielder lived in the guest house of the home Holmes shared with her partner Billy Evans and her two children for 34 days before she went to prison, has thrust Holmes, and indirectly, Carreyrou, back into the spotlight, while also earning rave reviews and an estimated $15 million during its first weekend—the second-biggest opening for a documentary ever. Carreyrou, for his part, told the New York Times’ “Hard Fork” podcast that he saw the film twice, the first time being during a virtually private screening with his wife at the Manhattan offices of A24, the studio behind the film. He said he enjoyed the film even more the second time he watched it. “I guess, over two viewings, I was really won over by the artistry of the way they filmed this thing, but also the structure, the amount of meta-thinking that went into it, how unusual it is,” he said on the podcast. In a post on X, Carreyrou went even further, saying the documentary deserved best picture. But he warned it doesn’t cover the full story. “The film is a great piece of art, but let’s not confuse that art with the underlying facts,” he said in a statement to Fortune. Some people have told Carreyrou that Holmes is “unknowable and everything is ambiguous,” he wrote in a post on X. He said what these people are missing are the details of what he experienced during his reporting. This includes legal threats that Theranos’ lawyers leveled against him as well as against two of his sources, Tyler Shultz and Erika Cheung. “She went after Tyler Shultz and Erika Cheung with everything she had. I was threatened with litigation on a daily basis for months on end. It was a ruthless cover-up of a breathtaking fraud, and she was at the controls,” he wrote in the post. At the center of that fraud was Holmes’ promise that Theranos could perform hundreds of medical tests with just a few drops of blood from a finger prick, which she sold as a way to make testing more accessible and cheaper. Yet, the company’s machines really could only perform a limited number of tests and often produced unreliable results. Some tests also required much more than the small portion of blood Holmes said they needed or had to be done with conventional methods, rather than with Theranos’ product. Holmes also misled investors, falsely stating that Theranos had been validated by numerous large pharmaceutical companies and telling investors the company would bring in $1 billion in revenue in 2015, while knowing that the actual revenue figure would be negligible, according to the Justice Department. Further details of the campaign to stop Carreyrou’s reporting are revealed in his book, including Holmes’ attempt to kill his original story by appealing directly to the Wall Street Journal’s billionaire owner, Rupert Murdoch, who was also a Theranos investor. Zuckerberg spent years building on Apple’s terms. His $100-billion-plus AI bet is about owning everything

Mark Zuckerberg says he knew he was taking an enormous risk when he green-lit Meta’s AI build-out—a commitment he pegged at as much as $200 billion—before there was any proof it would work. “We’re going to write this check for, like, I don’t know, like $100 billion, $200 billion,” Zuckerberg said on a recent episode of Cheeky Pint, the podcast hosted by Stripe cofounder John Collison. “I’m like, ‘Alex, are you sure this is going to work?’” Alexandr Wang, Meta’s chief AI officer, appeared alongside Zuckerberg and said decisions of that size get made “long before there’s proof points around whether or not even the thing is working.” He added: “It’s like the opposite of ‘lean startup.’” The episode was released Oct. 12, the same day Meta launched Muse Wonder, the first frontier-class model from the superintelligence lab Wang leads. Meta has guided for as much as $145 billion in capital expenditures this year, much of it on AI infrastructure. Much of that money is going toward what Zuckerberg describes as a “full-stack” operation. “Meta is a full-stack tech company,” he said. “We build our own data centers; we build our own chips; we build our own low-level infrastructure.” Zuckerberg’s push to own the full stack may stem from his nearly two-decade rivalry with Apple and Google, which control the world’s dominant smartphone platforms. Facebook’s iPhone app launched in July 2008, followed by an Android app in September 2009. Its mobile years that followed were marked by friction with the platform owners, some of it self-inflicted. Zuckerberg repeatedly sought work-arounds to the limits those platforms imposed. In 2013, Facebook pushed updates to its Android app outside the Google Play store, prompting Google to ban the practice. In 2019, Apple shut down a Facebook “Research” app that paid users, including teens, up to $20 a month for sweeping access to their phone data. The biggest blow came in 2021, when Apple began letting iPhone users block apps from tracking them—a change Meta later said would cost it about $10 billion in 2022 alone. Zuckerberg has said that experience shapes his approach to AI. “One of my formative experiences has been building our services constrained by what Apple will let us build on their platforms,” he wrote in a 2024 post on Meta’s website. Speaking with Collison, Zuckerberg said owning the stack is “less about wanting to own a platform for its own sake. It’s about being able to build the best things.” Unlike Amazon, Microsoft, and Google, Meta doesn’t run a cloud business that rents its data centers to outside customers, so it shoulders those costs itself. Zuckerberg said a one-gigawatt AI data center costs $50 billion to $60 billion to build and roughly $15 billion a year to operate. Meta’s Hyperion campus in Louisiana is designed to scale to five gigawatts. That could change. In July, Bloomberg reported that Meta is building a cloud business to sell AI computing power and is also weighing selling raw computing capacity. Weeks later, Zuckerberg confirmed the cloud plans on Meta’s earnings call. On Cheeky Pint, he framed all that compute as a hedge. “If … the cost is whatever, $15 billion a gigawatt, we could get, like, a lot more for that,” Zuckerberg said. “So I think that you, in order for it not to work out, it’s not just that we have to fail, AI overall has to fail.” Meta’s full-stack ambitions don’t stop at data centers. Zuckerberg and Wang want Muse Wonder and the more powerful Muse Wonder Full to drive Muse, Meta’s personal AI agent, which can act on people’s behalf. “The best personal agent should feel like a team of experts in your corner,” Wang said. “Your legal team, and your finance team, and your medical team.” Wang said he uses Muse to “source increasingly obscure teas from increasingly obscure vendors of tea.” Zuckerberg said he set up an Instacart account specifically for Muse, which orders ingredients for weekend baking projects with his 3-year-old daughter. He went from using Instacart maybe twice a year to “once or twice a week.” That vision of full-fledged agentic commerce could, paradoxically, disrupt Meta’s main profit engine. Advertising made up about 98% of Meta’s revenue last quarter, and eMarketer projects the company’s worldwide ad revenue will reach $243.46 billion this year, surpassing Google’s for the first time. But if AI agents compare prices, check delivery dates, read reviews, and ultimately make purchase decisions, who are the ads for? Collison raised a version of that question on the podcast, suggesting that a business that “only succeeds by overcharging consumers” would rationally want to keep agents out. Zuckerberg countered that few businesses have tried to block agents and that those that do are likely to lose out. “We have way more businesses that are calling us asking to integrate deeply,” he said. He argued agents could grow commerce overall: “Is the total addressable market for all of commerce just increasing dramatically?” Zuckerberg also sketched how Meta could make money beyond ads. “Subscriptions is going to be a big one,” he said. Muse is free to use, with paid plans at $20 and $100 a month for heavier users. On commerce, he floated taking “a small cut of what you are saving or making for people.” And on Meta’s July earnings call, he pitched business agents as working “just like the ad system”: “We will get paid when we deliver results for those businesses.” What Zuckerberg hasn’t addressed directly, on the podcast or the earnings call, is whether agents will eat into the ad impressions that generate nearly all of Meta’s revenue today. For now, Wall Street isn’t pressing the point. It’s official: Tesla and SpaceX are in talks. Independent board members at the two Elon Musk-led companies have formed special committees to hash over a possible merger, Musk told investors during SpaceX’s third-quarter earnings call Tuesday. “These special committees consist of independent directors, and because I’m a conflicted party, I can’t actually be in the discussions of the special committees,” said Musk, who was asked about a potential combination during the call. “This is definitely the kind of question where I feel like I’m tap dancing through a minefield.” Tesla stock rose just over 1% in after hours trading, while SpaceX rose above 5% following the Q&A with Wall Street analysts featuring Musk, SpaceX president and COO Gwynne Shotwell, and chief financial officer Bret Johnsen. Musk said the committees haven’t reached any kind of an agreement on the terms of a potential transaction, and there’s no guarantee that they will. He noted that “some people out there assume that a SpaceX and Tesla merger is a certainty, and I think that would be overstating it.” Musk also has to walk a fine line as a conflicted party. As the CEO of both companies, he owns a roughly 20% stake in Tesla and 45% of SpaceX—and wields control over about 82% of the voting power at the latter. “It’s an odd kabuki dance where two groups of people are talking and I can’t be in the conversation,” said Musk. At the same time, Musk has made clear he believes a tie-up would be a fine idea. He cited collaboration on AI software, chip design, robotics, and connectivity, plus the planned Terafab chip plant, which is a colossal manufacturing complex being developed by SpaceX and Tesla jointly. The committees were formed “at the request of shareholders,” said Musk and on the Tesla side it would need support from a majority of all outstanding shares, which Musk described as “quite a high hurdle.” Tesla filed its annual shareholder proxy report on Monday, and it doesn’t include a merger proposal for shareholders to vote on. Rumors of a tie-up, however, have swirled for months, even before SpaceX’s blockbuster IPO in June. At Tuesday’s closing prices, a combined Tesla-SpaceX would have a total market value of about $3.5 trillion, with SpaceX at about $2 trillion and Tesla at nearly $1.5 trillion. A formal deal price wouldn’t come until much further down the road, if at all. Musk would own about a third of the combined entity. The companies are already financially entangled, including a $2 billion investment by Tesla in SpaceX, Tesla’s Monday proxy statement confirmed. There are also multiple areas of overlap between the Tesla and SpaceX boards. Ira Ehrenpreis, founder of venture capital firm DBL Partners, chairs SpaceX’s compensation and nominating committee, which is a single committee at SpaceX, as well as the two separate committees at Tesla. And Tesla board member James Murdoch, who has been a director at the electric-vehicle maker since 2017, testified in a previous legal challenge over Musk’s pay package at Tesla that he had previously invested in SpaceX. The disclosure from Musk came as SpaceX reported its first operating profit since its June IPO. Revenues for the third quarter fiscal 2027 year rose 161% to $14.58 billion, well above the $12.86 billion analysts expected, according to Visible Alpha consensus from S&P Global Market Intelligence. Operating revenue clocked in at $2.79 billion, apnd net income was $4.4 billion. Revenue from SpaceX’s AI business, which was renamed “Super Intelligence,” nearly tripled from the second quarter to $7.56 billion, surpassing Starlink’s $5.25 billion for the first time since the company went public. In his usual fashion, Musk offered up aggressively optimistic thoughts about the future of SpaceX, claiming SpaceX’s breakneck buildout pace implies annual recurring revenue of $500 billion to $1 trillion by the end of 2027. Meanwhile, Johnsen reaffirmed a target of $100 billion in annualized revenue by the end of this year. At its third-quarter pace, SpaceX is bringing in about $58 billion a year. Hitting the $100 billion figure would take about $8.3 billion of revenue in December, roughly 70% more than its average month last quarter. Musk’s range would require about $42 billion to $83 billion a month by December 2027. Tesla did not immediately respond to a request for comment. Steven Bartlett started his podcast with just a laptop and a sock pulled over a $100 microphone. Before there were guests, it was just Bartlett, reading from his diary at 2 a.m. and trying to make sense of what he had written that week. He eventually began interviewing other people about theirs. Now, The Diary of a CEO is one of the world’s biggest podcasts and is on track to overtake Joe Rogan as the most-subscribed podcast on YouTube. The show has grown into a business Bartlett forecasts will generate about $60 million in revenue this year. But growing it has also meant turning down money—including a deal that would have required more advertising and taking the show off YouTube. He described the proposed arrangement as a trade: Accept a large payment, put 12 advertisements in the podcast, and have little say over those ads. “One of the central reasons why I turned down such an offer is because that is four times more ads than I have to put in now,” Bartlett told Fortune’s Ellie Austin on Fortune Daily. Working with what he calls “fragmentation vultures”—companies seeking exclusive podcast distribution deals—can mean sacrificing revenue or control over the relationship with an audience, Bartlett argued. Instead, he sees the relationship with his audience as worth protecting, even when a lucrative offer like that comes along. The business he aims to preserve already makes money beyond advertising through products, subscriptions, and events. Bartlett, who dropped out of college to pursue entrepreneurship, launched The Diary of a CEO in 2017 by turning his own diary entries into podcast episodes. He later began interviewing other people about their own diaries, finding it more intellectually stimulating to learn about their own lives than to read about his own. Those conversations explored his guests’ motivations, insecurities, and ambitions, eventually broadening the show’s scope to subjects ranging from neuroscience and psychology to performance and sports. And with it, the business has expanded too. When asked where the show’s projected $60 million comes from, Bartlett pointed to brand partnerships, products his business owns and manufactures, subscriptions, memberships, and events. On top of that, he runs an investment fund. “It’s a wide range of things and again, this goes to the point of diversification,” he said. Steven.com, Bartlett’s holding company, houses the podcast and his other creator businesses. It also includes other creators, helping them build media brands and develop products and ventures. Bartlett forecasts about $90 million in revenue for his broader media company this year, including the projected $60 million from The Diary of a CEO. He also put next year’s holding-company forecast at about $160 million. But he’s not the only person to see nine-figure valuations as a result of the podcasting business: Call Her Daddy podcast host Alex Cooper’s holding company Unwell raised investment at a $500 million valuation in August. Bartlett sees that evolution as part of a wider shift of how creators work with brands. Early in his career, he said companies treated influencers like billboards: Pay them, and they would post something. Then came the creative-director titles, equity arrangements, and opportunities to cofound businesses. That is most evident with Sydney Sweeney, who became an equity partner and creative director at sports prediction market Novig, shaping its advertising rather than just simply appearing in it. He believes the next stage involves creator businesses making acquisitions and operating more like large holding companies. “You’re no longer an influencer, you’re no longer an equity partner, a creative director,” he said. “You are an owner or cofounder.” Distribution has become a point of negotiation across podcasting, perhaps most famously in 2024, when Spotify ended Rogan’s exclusivity and allowed his show to stream on rival platforms. Bartlett prefers fewer partnerships, and only with businesses that matter to him personally. About 70% of his podcast partners, he said, are companies whose founders he approached himself. Choosing sponsors is only one part of maintaining audience trust—what guests say on the podcast matters too. On Fortune Daily, he was questioned about a 2024 BBC investigation into health claims made on his show. He said his team had contracted a full-time medical PhD to review health episodes, adding context on screen and removing material that could not adequately be contextualized. Now, an independent review team fact-checks every episode—not just those focused on health—before it is published. When asked whether an unsupported scientific statement would be removed from the audio, he said the decision depends on its framing: A definitive claim would be treated differently from a guest discussing a hypothesis based on early research. But despite his growing influence, Bartlett doesn’t consider himself a journalist. He described his interviews as an exercise in pursuing his own curiosity, whether he’s sitting across from Kamala Harris or JD Vance. Viewers will always have questions they wish he had asked, he said, but he believes his approach needs to remain anchored in that curiosity. “If you’re not anchored to, like, your own curiosity, you’re going to be a plastic bag in the wind,” he said. The gentle, accessible workout offers a modified take on the ancient movement and meditation practice, usually from a seated position. No one’s asked to climb down onto the floor or independently hold themselves upright in tricky standing postures—which makes it ideal for those with limited strength or balance. But experts say chair yoga shouldn’t be pigeonholed, because anyone can benefit. “It is a practice that meets you where you are,” says Divya Balakrishnan, a yoga instructor in New York City. “You’re still using your strength and flexibility, just in other ways.” A yoga teacher named Lakshmi Voelker first began adapting some yoga poses to a chair in 1982 to make the practice more accessible for a student with arthritis. Since then, numerous instructors have found ways to use a chair for adapted versions of a surprising number of traditional yoga movements, like lunging warrior poses and standing-to-lying sun salutations. While students typically crouch, sit, and lie down on a mat in traditional yoga classes, the chair, in a way, brings the floor closer. Some chair yoga classes are done entirely seated, moving through modified variations of classic yoga sequences without ever asking participants to get up. Other classes transition between sitting and standing while holding onto the back for a little extra help with balance, or doing poses like downward dog with hands on the seat instead of reaching to the floor. In a sense, the chair is simply another prop. “Just like you would use a strap or blocks to increase the accessible range of motion or bring different awareness to the body, the chair can be used in a similar way,” says Jessica Pate, a Boston-based yoga teacher. Experienced yogis also use a chair for athletic feats that wouldn’t be possible on a mat alone. In particular, the Iyengar style of yoga uses a yoga chair (designed with an open back and a completely flat seat) for advanced postures like an upside-down inversion where the yogi’s shoulders rest on two chairs while their head hangs in between. “There's an infinite number of ways that you can use the chair,” says Jules Mitchell, a Las Vegas–based yoga educator. Research on the physical benefits of seated chair yoga has found that practitioners tend to  improve their functional fitness and experience a decrease in how much pain interferes with their daily life. Much of that research has focused on older adults, who generally have a lower baseline of fitness than younger, healthy adults. It’s unlikely that people who are in peak shape would see as much benefit. “You're not challenging your muscles or your balance as much as traditional yoga,” says Justin Smith, an assistant professor at Wichita State University who recently co-authored a case series on the benefits of chair yoga. But that doesn’t mean it’s a waste of time for more athletic folks. For experienced yogis, doing yoga from a chair can offer new ways to explore familiar movements, says Pate, who incorporates a chair into many of her regular yoga classes. By sitting, the body’s weight gets distributed differently, which makes the muscles engage in new ways and can bring a fresh awareness to the body. “For example, with Warrior 2, having the chair underneath [my] pelvis, I'm able to feel where my sit bones are,” she says. This can help people better recognize if their hips are twisted or tilted. Although most people assume that sitting makes everything easier, that isn’t always the case. Sometimes the challenge just changes: Pate says she actually feels her outer hips working harder in Warrior 2 on a chair than they do when she’s standing in the pose because she’s forced into a more neutral alignment, and bigger muscles can’t take over. Sitting on a chair can also sometimes help people go deeper into poses. Say you’re trying to target your glutes with a Figure 4 pose, where one ankle rests on the opposite knee as you bend forward. When you’re seated, you’re able to focus more on the stretch because you’re not struggling to balance, Balakrishnan says. “If I was doing a standing Figure 4, half of my attention is going to be on the standing foot and keeping myself upright, so I may not sit as deep,” she says. The same is true for breathing. Pate says that the extra support of the chair makes it easier to focus on breathing deeply and intentionally—something that many students forget to do once poses get challenging. One group that might particularly benefit from chair yoga is people who are stuck sitting in front of a computer all day. “For anyone who works at a desk, it is a lifesaver,” Balakrishnan says. A simple seated yoga routine can relieve tight, achy joints, and multiple studies have found that chair yoga can significantly reduce stress. Balakrishnan regularly takes yoga breaks right from her chair, doing neck rolls, spinal twists, or anything else her body is craving to bring some movement and mindfulness back to her day—without feeling like she has to stop what she’s doing and carve out time for a full yoga session. “You don't have that mental barrier of, ‘Okay, let me get out of my chair, go roll out my yoga mat, go get my blocks,’” she says. “It requires less of you to begin the practice.” There’s also a simpler reason to do seated chair yoga: It just feels good. The low-key movement can help stretch out tense muscles and get some blood flowing without feeling like you have to do a workout that impresses anyone. Seated chair yoga may not build Olympic athlete–level strength and agility. Interruptions don't mean GPU failure. They simply mean something crashed, failed, or timed out, forcing the training run to stop and roll back to the last known good checkpoint. Meta claims in the year since it's been able to cut down the number of interruptions by 99 percent, to about 11 per day per 8,000 GPUs. Meta did this because a single failed GPU would take out an entire rack and interrupt the training run. Now, when a GPU fails, one of the spares can slot in and take its place. While this saves time — Meta doesn't have to drop the entire rack from the cluster or wait for someone to swap the faulty component to start training again — a failed GPU would still result in an interruption, albeit a shorter one. To mitigate this, Meta took advantage of the fact that large scale training jobs usually use data parallelism, where multiple copies of the model are resident within the cluster. "When a fault can be isolated to a data-parallel model replica, we reduce the impact of a hardware failure by allowing a single faulty replica to be taken offline, while the remaining cluster repeats that step and continues productive training," Meta explained. "The compromised slice simply restarts on available spare hosts and resynchronizes, converting a potential total system halt into a minor, temporary reduction in aggregate throughput."
