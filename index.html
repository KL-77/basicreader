Seagate's new hybrid drive raises some old questions

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-10

Visit Original Website
Seagate doesn’t think hybrid drives are done just yet. On Tuesday, the company announced the launch of the new FireCuda Forge line of storage drives. The new drives combine the high capacity of a traditional plattered HDD with the speed and responsiveness of a user-supplied solid-state drive (SSD). The new hybrid storage solution is Seagate’s first to be powered and managed by the new Alkemi intelligent storage platform. The FireCuda Forge is available right now through Seagate’s web store, and comes in 8TB ($699) or 20TB ($899) HDD configurations—neither of which includes the required SSD. Users will have to bring their own SSD, with some specific restrictions. The FireCuda Forge is compatible with M.2 2280 NVMe drives that are PCIe Gen3 or higher. The maximum supported NVMe capacity is 1TB, which means that’s as much data as you’ll be able to dedicate to storage on the “fast side” of the hybrid setup. The NVMe will require minimum speeds of 2,400MB/s read and 2,000MB/s write, and a unit with DRAM cache is recommended. Seagate claims 2,500MB/s read and 2,000MB/s write speeds for the combined system, along with USB4 and support for Windows 11 and macOS 26+. Credit: Seagate Credit: Seagate After dipping to historical lows earlier in the decade, storage pricing per TB has exploded amid the AI boom. According to data collected by Tom’s Hardware, some solid-state drives now cost two to three times their historic lows, and it’s not just SSDs. Mechanical drives haven’t been immune either. According to PCServerParts, a Seagate Exos 24TB drive that sold for $298.99 in the summer of 2025 was priced at $699.99 by August 2026. Hybrid drives had largely fallen out of fashion since their heyday in the early to mid 2010s. The promise then was similar to what Seagate is offering now. A large HDD is combined with a smaller SSD and handed to the user as a single storage pool. Apple introduced its own hybrid solution back in 2012 with the Mac mini’s Fusion Drive. When Ars opened up a unit, the Fusion Drive system was found to write in 128KB tiered blocks. Information was generally written to the SSD first, and was then migrated to the HDD as needed. If the files were frequently used, they might find a more permanent home on the SSD side of things. As solid-state storage continued getting cheaper, the hybrid approach was largely abandoned. Fast-forward to our current storage cost predicament in 2026, and Seagate’s line of thinking that everything old is new again starts to hold water. All storage costs have exploded, but the 2012 math still works today. NAND flash is still fast, but expensive, and traditional mechanical drives are at least comparatively cheaper per TB for higher capacity storage. The problem with Apple’s Fusion solution was its fragility. If one of the two drives failed, that was likely it. Recovery depended on both drives coming together as a single volume. If one of those two disks experienced a physical failure, recovery became difficult since the remaining working drive depended on the now broken drive’s data to rebuild the full system picture. According to Seagate’s press release, the Alkemi intelligent storage platform takes a different approach. Where Apple’s Fusion Drives used tiering managed by macOS, Alkemi manages the storage pool directly from the enclosure. Seagate says, “Alkemi learns and adapts over time to keep high-demand content fast and accessible on a user’s SSD, while the HDD provides space for growing libraries. Behind the scenes, Alkemi manages data placement, giving users the simplicity of a single storage drive.” The product data sheet PDF from the FireCuda Forge website describes the Alkemi system as a two-tier storage system, but doesn’t elaborate any further on whether the system uses SSD caching, automated tiering, a combination of the two, or something else entirely. The announcement doesn’t address how the Alkemi platform manages the data between the two drives. The old Intel Smart Response Technology kept failure risks low by mirroring data from the mechanical HDD to the faster SSD, with the HDD retaining the authoritative copy. The optional write-back mode could still hold new data on the SSD, though. Apple’s Fusion Drive instead tiered data in 128KB blocks across the storage pool, storing data on both drives. The distinction matters, and is probably why Ars asked the same question after Apple’s hybrid announcement 14 years ago. Seagate’s announcement speaks of “intelligent data tiering” without further explanation. The data system promises that your most accessed files will be quickest to access via intelligent storage management, but stops short of explaining whether one drive going down means failure for the whole system. There’s also no mention of how the Alkemi system distributes data on first boot. Does all the new written data go directly to the SSD until the 1TB cap is crossed, and then the system starts determining which files are important enough to remain on the speedier drive? Seagate seems to be keeping things mum for the moment, but as the drives have already launched for sale, there should be answers to all of these questions coming soon. Seagate hasn’t published technical documentation regarding how the Alkemi system handles drive failure or repair. Until it does, the new FireCuda Forge drives offer the same appeal as Apple’s old Fusion Drives: SSD speed with HDD capacity, along with the same questions Ars was asking about hybrid storage years ago.

The US military is taking a new approach to prop up commercial launch companies as SpaceX looks to wind down its Falcon rocket program, suggesting defense officials see the workhorse rocket’s retirement as a potential national security risk. The Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital has twice in the last two weeks announced it was sending cash to two of SpaceX’s competitors in the launch business. First, on October 26, the office announced a $150 million conditional loan commitment to Stoke Space. Military officials revealed a second $150 million Pentagon loan Tuesday for Rocket Lab. Stoke Space and Rocket Lab are the first two launch companies to receive significant support from the Office of Strategic Capital (OSC), an organization established in 2022 to infuse companies developing “vital” national security technologies with capital. OSC has been on a loan spree in recent weeks, committing hundreds of millions of dollars to companies specializing in batteries, drones, and semiconductors. In the space sector, the office also provided a $150 million loan to LeoLabs, a commercial satellite tracking company, for production and deployment of radar units. SpaceX, the Pentagon’s leading launch provider, has stopped taking commercial orders for Falcon 9 launches after 2028 as the company shifts its focus to the next-generation, super-heavy-lift Starship rocket. Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy launches for government customers, including NASA and the US Space Force, are expected to continue a while longer. The Space Force’s launch contract with SpaceX allows the military to place Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy orders through 2029, with the actual launches extending through 2033. Then, if Starship is fulfilling its promise, SpaceX plans to retire Falcon for good. Starship may eventually be capable of launching national security payloads, but SpaceX’s near-term priority is using the rocket’s massive cargo capacity for its own Starlink Internet satellites and Starmind orbital data centers. Military space officials have held to a policy of assured access to space since the 1990s, with the Pentagon paying to support at least two US rocket programs capable of launching national security satellites. The support previously came in the form of direct subsidies, development funding, and launch contracts at premium prices to give the government technical and financial insight into their launch providers, schedule priority on their launch manifests, and, in some cases, to simply keep them in business. The Space Force’s mix of launch providers today consists of SpaceX’s Falcon family and United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan rocket, which has been grounded twice in the last two years by problems with its solid rocket boosters. ULA’s woes with Vulcan forced the Space Force to shift several national security missions to SpaceX. Other launch providers aim to compete for their own slices of the growing pie of Space Force launch contracts. Space Systems Command, which oversees launch procurement for the Space Force, now forecasts the military will require up to 249 medium-to-heavy-lift launches through the early 2030s from its roster of launch contractors, more than double the command’s expectation just two years ago. Alongside SpaceX and ULA, Space Systems Command has tapped Blue Origin, Rocket Lab, Stoke Space, Relativity Space, and Impulse Space for eligibility to compete for some of those national security launches. Blue Origin is the only company that has flown a rocket that could meet the Space Force’s requirements. Rocket Lab, Stoke Space, and Relativity Space are developing their own medium- and heavy-lift launch vehicles, while Impulse Space is working on in-space propulsion. The loans from the Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital aim to get new medium-class rockets from Stoke Space and Rocket Lab ready to meet the military’s needs. Stoke Space’s loan is tailored to support the buildout of the company’s second launch pad at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, Florida. The second launch site would support flights of Stoke Space’s Nova Block 2 rocket, an upgraded version of the company’s fully reusable Nova Pathfinder, which is set to debut sometime next year. “By supporting the construction of additional launch infrastructure, this commitment would enhance the overall capacity and resilience of the commercial space industrial base,” said Jordan Sebold, senior managing director of investments at OSC. The loan to Rocket Lab will support the company’s efforts to increase the flight rate of its partially reusable Neutron rocket, which Rocket Lab says is also scheduled to make its first launch next year. “The proposed loan would support faster vehicle delivery and processing and allow Rocket Lab to expand its fleet of maritime vessels with additional equipment for Neutron recovery and transport,” OSC said in a statement. “It would also enable manufacturing upgrades to increase the production of composite structures and engines, and support infrastructure that would reduce turnaround time between launches once the reusable rocket makes its operational debut.” Both conditional loans are subject to “various financial, legal, technical, investment, due diligence, and other requirements,” OSC said. Adam Spice, Rocket Lab’s chief financial officer, said Monday the Pentagon loan is on “very attractive terms” and “underscores the importance” of the military’s appetite for launch capacity. “We always take it when we can get it,” Spice said in a quarterly earnings call. “It gives us additional capital to scale key parts of our business, Neutron in particular … I think this is a helpful indicator, a very positive indicator, about where everyone sees the demand constraint and how it can be unlocked, and capital is certainly a part of that.” Rocket Lab also unveiled new pricing for the Neutron rocket—an increase from $55 million to $70 million per launch—in response to what the company said was a surge in bookings for the still-unproven launch vehicle. Rocket Lab said it now has a backlog of 117 launches on contract, including orders for Neutron missions and the company’s smaller Electron rocket. “Launch is extremely constrained, and that is exactly the situation we predicted,” said Peter Beck, Rocket Lab’s founder and CEO.

Grok Build, the vibe-coding agent from SpaceXSI, is leaking data—some of it proprietary business information and other sensitive secrets—from thousands of its users, including the very same Elon Musk-owned company that offers the platform, new research shows. The information is revealed through links allowing Grok Build users to share their apps with others. Grok takes steps to randomize the URLs so only people who know the exact URL can access the data stored there. A design decision by developers of the platform, however, completely torpedoes that objective and makes it possible for anyone to enumerate thousands of live Grok Build projects. Except in rare cases, clicking those links opens the app and reveals all data associated with it. Research from security firm Claroc shows that the exposed data includes things like internal business plans, ad proposals, medical exam results, galley checklists from a celebrity cruise line, and employee names and ID numbers. Ironically, some of the sensitive data comes from two-dozen apps built by SpaceXSI (the name of the now-merged SpaceX and xAI) itself. A small sampling of the documents revealed in the SpaceXSI apps—Claroc found 24 of them—include an onboarding plan, marked confidential, for a new member of SpaceXSI’s enterprise sales team, a sales territory map of 594 accounts, internal system addresses for sales team tools, and advertising proposals. “We were shocked,” Claroc said in a Tuesday post. “The company that makes Grok had its own internal material open to anyone holding the link, and we had never been given it. Had the sales team bought a tool for this, it would have sat behind company sign-in, open only to the team. Built with AI, it went live open to anyone with the link. Claroc notified SpaceXSI of the privacy spill on Sunday. When no one responded, a Claroc official took to X to tag both Musk and Grok Manager Matt Palmer about the threat. At the time this post went live almost all data remained available with one notable exception: the contents from the 24 SpaceXSI apps. All of them were exposing the information on Monday evening. SpaceXSI began removing the links within a few hours of Ars sending an email with questions for this article. At the time this post went live, links for all 24 apps returned an “app not found” error. Claroc found sensitive data belonging to other Grok Build users, some working for Fortune 500 companies and a defense contractor. Some of the affected organizations and data revealed includes: Asaf Aprozper, the co-founder and CTO of Claroc, said he’s in the process of notifying companies. After speaking to the CISO of the luxury cruise company, it “pulled in their whole security team as soon as we shared the findings and are already working to contain the Grok exposure.” Link sharing for Grok Build is intended to streamline the process of developing and deploying apps. A user can input speech or text and have a working app ready for internal testing within minutes. The links are designed to give developers a way to get the app in front of authorized users quickly. The very left of the URLs contain two or more “dice words,” meaning randomly generated phrases such as “failing chassis traders,” although users can modify them. The rightmost portion of the link is grok.me. The URL for an X ads campaign plan was: https://cinder-crane-sand-frost.grok.me. The scheme is intended to prevent links from being easily guessable. When done correctly, a phrase comprising three dice words has roughly 38 bits of entropy, amounting to billions of possible combinations. The idea—sometimes called capability-based access control—is that possession of the URL serves as authorization to the resources behind it. Google has long used this technique to make URLs easy to share but hard to guess. A URL share on that platform might look like: https://docs.google.com/document/d/1BxiMVs0XRA5nFMdKvBdBZjgmUUqptlbs74OgvE2upms/edit There’s a fatal flaw in the way SpaceXSI has implemented the mechanism. Instead of being to the right of the domain—as is the case with Google links—the randomness for Grok Build links is positioned to the left of it. That allows the links to be easily enumerated using passive DNS services. These services monitor central DNS lookup servers. When a server lookup requests the IP address of a previously never-before-seen URL, the transaction is captured in a publicly searchable database. Had Grok followed Google’s approach, the links would have been invisible to passive DNS searchers. Muse, the Meta agent that CEO Mark Zuckerberg said was built from the ground up for security, recently suspended a link-sharing feature after it too was found leaking sensitive data. Aprozper said a passive DNS search returned 43,601 links ending in grok.me. Of them, more than 35,000, or nearly 95 percent, were public, meaning that clicking on the link opened the corresponding app and all data it stored. He said that tests he conducted over the past week showed that, by default, Grok sets links to “Anyone with the link.” Users have the option to change the setting to “invited users” or “only you.” A representative with Cursor, the AI coding service SpaceXSI recently acquired, said in an email that Grok Build “apps are private by default and nothing goes public unless the creator chooses to share it.” The representative said that competing coding agents from ChatGPT, Lovable, and Bolt also give users the ability to generate shareable links. Aprozper’s tests contradict the representative’s statement. In all cases, Grok Build required users to change anyone with the link to one of the other two options. The Cursor statement regarding other AI coding platforms is correct. Aprozper said that app builders, including Lovable, Bolt, and Replit, “also give each app its own web address, so the same DNS records expose them, too. However, Grok is where we found by far the most sensitive company data.” That means that people using these other platforms are also at risk of exposing sensitive data through shared links. Interestingly, AI platforms that follow the correct sharing link practice include Claude’s published artifacts (claude.ai/public/artifacts/), ChatGPT’s shared conversations (chatgpt.com/share/), and Gemini (gemini.google.com/share/). For all three, the secret randomness is in the path, so DNS never sees it. The representative didn’t respond to follow-up questions, including if SpaceXSI intentionally exposed the proprietary internal information, why the company removed the data after Ars asked about it, and if it planned to notify customers who also exposed similar types of information. Anyone using shareable links with Grok Build should choose the “invite others” option unless they intend to make an app widely available.

Our modern world runs on math, from social media algorithms, package delivery, and weather prediction to medical imaging and machine learning. The rise of computers helped fuel its dominance, enabling us to work with increasingly large numbers that would otherwise be beyond the capacity of our finite human brains. But with the rise of large language models, AI chatbots, and, soon, quantum computing, so-called “big math” is beginning to outstrip its creators entirely. What that means, and how we might adapt, is the focus of a new book, Big Math: The Hidden Codes That Run Our World, by applied mathematicians Steven Strogatz and Alex Townsend. Strogatz is perhaps best known in his field for his co-authorship of a seminal 1998 paper with Duncan Watts on the collective dynamics of small-world networks. He has also studied chaos theory as applied to physics, the geometry of supercoiled DNA, and the biological collective oscillators, such as swarms of synchronously flashing fireflies and crickets that chirp in unison. In 2005, Strogatz extended his study of synchronicity to the crowds that caused the wobbling of London’s Millennium Bridge. An award-winning communicator, Strogatz wrote three series of columns on mathematics for The New York Times in 2010, 2012, and 2025 and hosted a podcast for Quanta magazine from 2020–2021. He has written four popular books: Sync (2003), The Calculus of Friendship (2009), The Joy of X (2012), and the New York Times bestseller Infinite Powers (2019). This time around, Strogatz has found a well-matched co-author in Townsend, a younger colleague recently hired at Cornell who studies numerical analysis and deep learning. As senior faculty, Strogatz was assigned to mentor Townsend, and the two men began regularly lunching together in Risley Hall. Strogatz was intrigued by the popular book potential of Townsend’s course on the top 20 algorithms of the 20th century and suggested co-authorship. That concept slowly morphed into a book about big matrix calculations, and then into what became Big Math. “There are so many books about pure math, as if computers didn’t exist,” Strogatz told Ars. “Alex and I are both very applied mathematicians. We thought there should be a book on computers and math, especially the class of really big problems with billions of variables or parameters that you just can’t approach in your head. A lot of the math we’ve had for centuries perfectly scales up to this computer moment. What the abacus did for arithmetic, the fangcheng (“rectangular array”) board did for systems of linear equations. It was a matrix you could manipulate with your hand and bamboo sticks. So this externalizing of math outside of our heads into our tools is a 2,000-year-old idea.” “The mathematician in each of us will always want to know more,” Strogatz and Townsend wrote. “The human being must insist that knowledge serves something larger than itself. That might be the work of our century: letting our power grow without losing our soul. Big Math keeps getting bigger. So must we.” Ars caught up with Strogatz to learn more. Ars Technica: Let’s start by defining “big math,” because it’s such a broad umbrella term. Steven Strogatz: Operationally, big math is “big” relative to human cognitive capacity. So it can be surprisingly small and yet big. We also wanted to expand it to things that people would think of as computational engineering—in the building of the Burj Khalifa, that’d be civil engineering or design architecture. It’s there in medical imaging. It’s in getting us to the Moon. The lunar module was controlled by a tiny computer that only had to think about 20 or 30 variables at a time to steer the LEM down to the Moon’s surface. But it’s not something a person could do in their head. Big Math is not simply big in the ordinary sense of big numbers. There’s an undercurrent in the book about the importance of linearity and sparsity. When problems are linear, they can be scaled up to immense size, and they stay tractable. If they’re non-linear, you don’t have any hope of dealing with them with pure math. You can do them with computers. But for the people who want rigorous theorems, we’re in terra incognita when we deal with anything that’s non-linear and big. Small math can be very hard. Historically, we had small math problems with one or two variables, arithmetic, algebra that you learn in high school. Even to this day, a lot of pure math is relatively small. It doesn’t concern itself with enormous numbers of variables simultaneously because it’s totally intractable. Cryptography can be small, but it’s so complicated with its non-linearities and other algebraic difficulties. So big is one way to be difficult, but there are a lot of other ways. Nowadays, trillions of parameters are typical in the biggest of the large language models. But infinity is not big from this point. Infinity, which you would think of would be the biggest of big math, is not because calculus fits perfectly nicely in a human head. Infinity turns out to be very tractable in a way that a billion is not. Ars: One of your real-world examples of big math is particularly relevant at the moment: mathematics as it relates to the gerrymandering issue. Strogatz: It’s an interesting question of, what’s a fair map and what’s a reasonable redistricting? The legal system gives us certain criteria, but they are vague. The districts should not be too snakey-looking, not look like salamanders, for instance. The term in the law is ‘compact.’ They should be reasonably compact. But what does that mean mathematically? The districts are also supposed to be contiguous; you don’t want to have separate islands and call that one district. It’s supposed to respect natural boundaries like rivers or other landscape features. Also, each district is supposed to have roughly the same population. So how to make maps that respect all of the criteria? It turns out there are a lot of ways to make them. Using past voting information, you can stack the deck. That’s what gerrymandering is all about, trying to redistrict in favor of whatever party is in power. There has been a big debate about whether certain maps violate people’s rights. The principle should be one person, one vote. If you do things that dilute somebody’s power so that their votes are wasted, so to speak, that’s traditionally thought to be unfair or maybe even illegal. The Supreme Court has heard all kinds of cases, but the court is very resistant to the use of math. I quoted [Chief Justice] John Roberts talking about the efficiency gap as one measure of gerrymandering. He referred to it by its Greek letters and said something like, “The average person on the street is just going to think this is gobbledygook, that this is not a criterion that the law should pay any attention to.” Nevertheless, Moon Duchin and her colleagues showed this very down-to-earth way of generating a big ensemble of reasonable maps that satisfy all the legal criteria so that you have a baseline, you could do the control experiment. What the math does is give you a principled way of generating maps at random that respect the criteria that the law requires, but otherwise are just a random sample of reasonable maps. How often would a map produce the result that the gerrymander intended? If you can show that it’s an extreme outlier, that’s a measure of something. Does it prove malicious intent? Not really. It does prove it’s not typical. There was an Alabama voting rights case where Roberts actually voted with the plaintiffs, who said that there was real harm being done to Black voters in Alabama with one of Alabama’s maps. The state was claiming there’s no way we could get a reasonable map to have two districts with Black majority voters. Moon Duchin showed that was wrong. You could generate thousands of maps that would do that. So it just wasn’t true what the state had claimed. After the book was copyedited and ready to go to press, a Supreme Court decision was made that, in fact, you can partisan gerrymander as much as you want. It doesn’t violate the Voting Rights Act. We wrote a more optimistic story. Ars: How has math changed over time from a philosophical standpoint? Strogatz: I think a crisp way to encapsulate it is the role of the computer. For a long time, a computer was a servant to mathematicians and engineers and scientists. It was no more philosophically threatening than a microscope or a telescope. It could extend our senses. It’s a chisel, it’s an anvil, it’s a tool. It helped us do calculations faster; things that would be too hard for people to do at scale you could do on a machine. We had mechanical calculators. They became electronic computers in the 1950s with the invention of the transistor and got much faster to give us today’s supercomputers. We program them; they do exactly what we tell them. So they’re servants. The first philosophically big change is in the 1950s, when computers start to be used as more than servants; they become scouts. You could send the computer out into unknown, unexplored territory in the mathematical landscape. The clearest example was work by Enrico Fermi and his colleagues (Pasta, Ulam, and Tsingou) that we wrote about in the chapter on simulation. Mary Tsingou was the one who did the calculations in what was traditionally called the Fermi-Pasta-Ulam numerical experiment. What distinguished it from earlier uses of computers is that Fermi and colleagues had no idea what the answer was going to be. It’s one thing to use a computer to calculate the power released by an atomic bomb blast or to calculate an artillery shell’s trajectory. We didn’t know the exact numerical values, but we knew what was happening. With this experiment, they were trying to simulate a simplified model of how atoms could, in the jargon, formalize as chemical bonds jiggle. You want to see the decay to entropy. How do systems degenerate to greater and greater disorder if they’re just left to their own devices? Fermi thought that the non-linear aspect of chemical bonds—meaning if you’d stretch a chemical bond by twice as much, it doesn’t pull back exactly twice as hard, so it’s not strictly proportional—might account for the so-called second law of thermodynamics, the decay to entropy. Pencil and paper math was not up to the job because you couldn’t calculate these non-linear interactions far enough to see the decay into entropy. So they ran it in the computer, and a shocking thing happened: It didn’t decay. It showed a strange echo. You started all the energy in one of the modes of vibration of this system, and then it started to degenerate. But after a while, 98 percent of the energy came back into the original mode. That was the echo that they called a recurrence phenomenon. It led us to a whole new branch of math that we call soliton theory. It was an early indication that once you walk into the nonlinear jungle, human intuition breaks down. We couldn’t walk there, but we sent our scout, this computer probe, into the wilderness of the mind, and it was the only thing capable of doing the calculations and reporting back to us what it found. Ars: The rapid rise of AI in mathematics has alarmed many in the field, particularly in light of OpenAI’s release last month of 722 AI-generated math papers, dubbed the “mathocalypse.” Technically, AI is also a new tool, but it seems fundamentally different. Strogatz: So from servant to scout was a new thing, and computing was our partner. At first, it was all very thrilling. It was like having a new Ferrari or something. You could race ahead and then discover things. That’s what Fermi and Ulam said at the time. That was truly amazing. They didn’t know what to expect. The computer was now teaching us by showing us things, but we could still keep up. We could develop these new theories like soliton theory, so we could rationalize what it was showing us. What’s different now is that it’s going from servant to scout to oracle, where it’s showing us things that we don’t understand and it’s not articulate enough to explain itself. It seems to be right. A clear example would be something like Google Translate, where it’s fantastic at translating one language into another, but it has not discovered new principles of linguistics, or if it has, it can’t tell us what they are. Likewise, protein folding with AlphaFold that won the Nobel Prize in chemistry a couple of years ago for the people who built it. We now can solve a thousand times more proteins than we had before, but we have not done it by learning new physics or chemistry. It just recognized patterns, but it can’t tell us how it’s doing it. So that’s where we are, and it’s going to get worse. Ars: In your book, you ask if insight still matters, citing Stephen Wolfram as an example of someone who might answer that question in the negative. Strogatz: Wolfram’s position is tricky. He pushes computational irreducibility. I love these two words, perspicuous and perspicacious. Systems can be perspicuous; you can see into them. Science has, for thousands of years, concerned itself with systems that are perspicuous, meaning you can get insight into them because you’re so perspicacious. But the question from Wolfram would be, have we been the famous drunk that’s looking under the lamppost for his keys because it’s dark everywhere else? You wouldn’t have any hope of finding them except under the lamppost. So is it that we’ve artificially restricted ourself to systems that are transparent enough that we can develop science that our insight illuminates? I think Wolfram would claim that most of nature is computationally irreducible in the sense that there is no simple insightful theory into what’s going on. The best you can do is just run a simulation and watch what happens, similar to just letting nature unfold and watch what happens in reality. In the jargon people use nowadays, you shouldn’t expect a compressed description, a simplified model that will account for what you’re seeing. We have seen those. Newton could explain the motion of the planets from a few simple principles and that worked, but that’s, I think in Wolfram’s take, artificially simple. Most of science is not going to end up being like that. We don’t know if Wolfram is really right about that. Wolfram, I think, is demonstrably right that many computer simulations are computationally irreducible, but nature might not be. Ars: If you can’t see into the models, and they can’t explain what they’re doing, how do you know they’re right? Strogatz: Let’s stipulate that we don’t understand them, and that they are not at the present able to explain themselves to us. Let’s put a little asterisk next to that part, though, because it could be that they’ll become very good teachers. Or maybe we don’t understand Google Translate or AlphaFold right now, but we will in 10 years. I do believe that there will be systems that are truly opaque because of human limits, that we just won’t be fast enough or smart enough or have enough memory. AIs will show us something that we just won’t be able to understand even with their help. What happens then? Then I think math starts to be more like science or medicine, or engineering. At the FDA, for a new drug to be approved, it has to be shown to be safe by doing a lot of controlled studies and randomized trials. There’s a whole methodology for opacity that people in medicine have worked out. But the doctors do not require knowledge of the mechanism of action. You don’t have to know how aspirin works. You don’t have to know how electroconvulsive therapy works for people with intractable depression. Even if we don’t understand, we can still live with these technologies or drugs by these methods. You don’t have to have first principles understanding. If the AI is an oracle, you can still check it by seeing how often it is right. Strogatz: It is a loss. I don’t want to pooh-pooh the severity of the—to use a fancy philosophical word—epistemic loss. What we have prided ourselves on in pure math for so long is that we’re the only subject that has absolute proof. We have certainty. We’re as close to certainty as anything that humanity can do. We can write a proof. Euclid’s proofs are still correct today, even though he wrote them in 300 BC. There’s nothing in the science or medicine of 300 BC that is still accepted, but all of the math is still true. So there is something eternal and very special about math. Even though we’re just primates, somehow we’re tapping into something really deep. And so to say, “Well, science and engineering are the models for math,” would be a big sacrifice to the purest of pure mathematicians. They don’t want to give up the certainty that we’ve always been so proud of. Ars: There’s a sadness among many mathematicians, not just for the epistemic questions, but also for the impact on why we do math in the first place. You write about that “aha!” moment when you’re struggling with a problem and suddenly you see the pattern. Are we losing that? Strogatz: There’s so many things that mathematicians are in grief about, those of us who feel grief. I have been through the whole sequence of the five stages of grief. I’m joking with you a little. But the young students don’t think this is funny. I’m an old person at the end of my career, so it’s one thing for me to be philosophical about it. But imagine being 25 years old and you love this subject and you’ve dreamt about being a mathematician, and now people are saying the subject is over. There are even people gloating about it. Some of the people in the tech community are very cruel about what’s happening to math. We talked about the epistemic aspect, but there’s also the culture of productive struggle. The pure math people like Terry Tao have been emphasizing understanding. That’s our idea of insight, that when you try hard to understand something, even if you don’t solve it, you often are so resourceful in thinking of strategies or other simplified versions of the problem. You learn a lot on the journey. You also have all this companionship and human joy of being with other people who love the thing you love. This is not restricted to math. The people who love art or weaving—my colleagues in creative writing are bereft over what their students are experiencing now, handing in AI [generated work]. They want writing with soul, with human individuality. Ars: That is the opposite of what AI does. I guess I worry that it will do the same thing to math. There is a beauty to math, an aesthetic element. If all you’re focused on is getting the answers and ignore the journey, those are qualitative losses. Mathematicians have individual voices, too. Strogatz: Totally. There are people whose style is very visible in the questions they ask, in the approach they take to them, in the way they explain themselves. I have cried on camera for several interviews over the past few months because there are lots of things maybe being lost. But I want to put an underline on the fact that we don’t really know. For example, what if AI gets really good at teaching us? I find when I interact with it, that it can be a very effective explainer. And why do we have to feel sad? Maybe we can understand math better than ever with the help of this machine. We may not get to make the greatest discoveries because the machine will probably be making them, but we can still make little discoveries. I never made a great discovery. I’m not at the level of a Fields medalist, but I can still enjoy math and so can people way below me. There are recreational mathematicians who take pleasure in discovery, whatever they figure out on their own. Just like when a person solves a puzzle, it’s not like a jigsaw puzzle or Sudoku is important. You just have the pleasure of thinking. There are people who love the workout part of it. There are people who like the competition. We shouldn’t pretend. There are people who are thrilled by the idea of being the first to make a big discovery. That’s animating for some folks, and it will be hard for them. Strogatz: So far the AIs are not good at asking questions. I think they could be trained to ask good questions. A graduate student is often not good at asking questions when they start because in college you’re not taught to ask questions, you’re taught to give answers. The hardest thing in training a new PhD or postdoc is helping them figure out how to ask questions. I think we could do that with AIs, too. There is a feeling of loss, but I don’t think we should shortchange the feeling of excitement. There’s so many practical, important benefits that could happen in energy technology and climate and medicine, theoretical physics, pure math. I mean, we’re making astonishing discoveries through this machine. So for a lot of people, it’s a very joyful time. It’s very confusing. That’s the only honest assessment. We end the book with Faust. Usually people hear “Faustian bargain” as, “You have sold your soul to the devil, now you’re going to pay.” Goethe’s version is more ambiguous: that if we keep striving to build something bigger than ourselves, maybe we can be redeemed. But it’s still a very dangerous game that we have chosen to play.

‘Stay, with less’: Why Nvidia’s board may rein in Jensen Huang rather than fire him

Fortune | FORTUNE | 2026-11-11

Jensen Huang has acknowledged an affair with Alice Huang, a former Megaspeed executive suspected of being a Chinese spy. On Sunday, the same day it opened an independent investigation, Nvidia’s board said it had confidence in its CEO. Investors were less sure. Nvidia shares fell as much as 8.8% on Monday and closed down about 4.3%, wiping roughly $190 billion off the market value of a company that ranked as the most valuable on the 2026 Fortune 500. For most CEOs, that sounds like the kind of week that ends with a resignation letter. But at Nvidia, even removing the CEO could be complicated. “In most cases of scandal at major corporations, the CEO is either replaced or put on leave while investigations are carried out and appropriate responses decided,” Jo-Ellen Pozner, an associate professor of management at Santa Clara University’s Leavey School of Business, told Fortune. Nvidia’s board faces two problems. It may be reluctant to force out the man who built the company, and even if it wanted to, there is no obvious successor. “I don’t expect a ‘stay or go’ decision. It will be more like a ‘stay, with less,’” Jason Schloetzer, an associate professor of accounting at Georgetown University’s McDonough School of Business, told Fortune. Schloetzer said the board could appoint a clear second-in-command, communicate more openly about succession planning, and reshuffle board seats to signal stronger oversight. Huang would remain CEO, but under closer supervision At a typical large corporation, the board oversees the CEO and succession planning, while layers of senior management provide potential replacements. Nvidia operates differently. Huang has built an unusually flat management structure, with dozens of direct reports and few layers of hierarchy. Pozner calls it “an extremely unusual organizational structure.” It has kept Huang at the center of the company since its founding in 1993. “I also imagine that he did it in part to prevent politics and political factions within the organization,” Pozner said. That model can work well at a small organization, she explained, but Nvidia has outgrown it. “There needs to be disagreements and productive conflict and things like that,” she added. Over time, she said, the structure “had the effect of suppressing the emergence of potential successors, probably diminishing conflict and consolidating power in the hands of Jensen Huang.” Nvidia has not publicly identified a successor. Pozner said promoting an insider would be risky because investors, regulators and business partners would be looking for credibility and stability. That leaves bringing in an outsider, which carries risks of its own. Finding a replacement is only half the problem. The other half is whether Nvidia’s board would be willing to remove Huang at all. Nvidia has no board chair, a position typically responsible for leading the board and overseeing management. Instead, Huang works with lead independent director Stephen Neal to set board meeting agendas, while management prepares the information presented to directors, according to the company’s filings. Neal can also convene meetings of independent directors without Huang. Of Nvidia’s 11 board members, three, including Huang, have served continuously since the company’s founding in 1993, while another joined in 1997. A fifth director, Mark Stevens, served from 1993 to 2006 before returning in 2008, according to the company’s 2026 proxy statement. Although Nvidia classifies all directors other than Huang as independent, Pozner questioned whether their longstanding relationships and the company’s governance structure could affect their willingness to hold him accountable. “They’ve become billionaires because of their affiliation and ownership of this company. And just working together with the same CEO since the inception of the organization makes it impossible for them to act as outsiders,” she said. The board’s Sunday statement said a committee of independent directors, with help from outside counsel, would oversee the investigation. It said it had expressed confidence in Huang before that work was completed and that the board had no reason to believe national security or Nvidia’s secrets had been compromised. “It is unclear that a captive board like this—which in some ways is not unlike Tesla’s—has the heart to question, censure, or sanction Huang, and it is doubtful that it has taken succession planning very seriously,” Pozner said. The controversy has also produced something rare in Washington: bipartisan agreement. On Monday, Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D., Mass.) and Sen. Jim Banks (R., Ind.) issued a joint statement calling for answers from Nvidia and tighter restrictions on advanced AI chip exports to China. Nvidia and Huang are also being sued in a proposed shareholder class action that alleges they misled investors about Huang’s ties to Alice Huang. And President Trump said the FBI was looking into the nature of the relationship. For now, Nvidia is standing behind its CEO. A company spokesperson told Axios that Huang was committed to continuing as CEO without interruption. Some investors are equally reluctant to see him go. Michael Monaghan, a partner at Founder ETFs, told Axios that his firm would sell its Nvidia holdings if Huang departed, calling him in some ways “irreplaceable.” “We don’t really believe there is such a thing as succession in these massive founder-led companies,” Monaghan said. Prediction markets lean the same way: Polymarket traders put the odds of Huang resigning or being removed by year-end at 16%, Fortune reported. Keeping Huang doesn’t necessarily mean business as usual. A failure to be candid with the board, however, could still cost Huang his job. “Boards remove CEOs over candor issues,” Schloetzer said. “What did he tell the directors, and when? Can he be trusted with board communications going forward?” If Huang does leave, Pozner questions whether Nvidia’s current board is equipped to choose his successor. “I think it was very short-sighted and is exacerbating the danger that Nvidia is in,” she said. She suggested that several long-serving directors could accept some accountability for a governance failure and step down. Replacing them with experienced directors from outside Silicon Valley, she said, would create a board capable of making the tough call on a successor. Alison Roman’s tomato sauce costs $12.99 a jar—and it keeps selling out

Alison Roman built one of the most loyal audiences in food media with best-selling cookbooks, recipes that went viral and a widely read newsletter. Now she’s selling her own tomato sauce, at $12.99 a jar, to that same audience. It’s a bet that those readers know what other options are available, and would opt for that special fennel taste, or the shallot-anchovy blend she’s known for. The jars will be sold to shoppers in independent stores nationwide, at a price that’s above what many see as a premium sauce product: Rao’s. Campbell’s bought parent company Sovos Brands in 2023 as part of a $2.7 billion deal, and the sauce sells for about $8. For Roman, she’s open about the price. “If anything, I think it’s a deterrent,” Roman told Fortune. “But I also think it’s a different product.” Roman relaunched the sauce as Roman’s on October 21 and began selling it to stores through Faire, its exclusive wholesale partner, which boasted $750 million in annualized revenue last quarter alone and is eyeing an IPO within the next year. Roman’s is one of about 100,000 brands selling to some 500,000 retailers on Faire. The sauce started last fall as A Very Good Tomato Sauce, sold online and at First Bloom, the grocery store Roman opened in the Catskills. The brand is now in more than 100 stores across 30 states, and Roman is funding it with money from her other businesses rather than through venture capital. When she first launched it last year, Roman said fans kept asking why her name wasn’t on the label. “It felt kind of tacky,” Roman said, since everyone knows she isn’t making the sauce herself. But even then, people were already calling it Alison Roman’s sauce. “Unless I named it something completely different that had nothing to do with me, I think people were probably always going to associate it with me and my name,” she said. “And so I kind of doubled down on that.” That said, having a cool name helps. “If my name were something else, I don’t know that I would feel so bullish on that being my brand name,” she joked. Roman isn’t the first to have built a brand out of her work, as plenty of big names have built businesses out of their personal brands. Take Shaquille O’Neal’s former agent, Leonard Armato, who told Fortune last month that O’Neal “owned his own brand,” and that the job was finding companies to build value in it. For Roman, the businesses are a way to reach more people than she can on her own. “I can only write so many books. I can only do so many videos or write so many recipes,” she said. She keeps her writing and recipe development to herself, but the businesses bring in designers, operations staff—and, of course, the people stirring the sauce. “When I’m writing a book, it’s just me and my laptop, and I can’t scale that,” she said. “But I can open a store. I can put sauce in 100 stores around the country with other people.” What she’s offering, she said, is the trust she built over years of recipes that work. “If I’m gonna put my name on tomato sauce, or ask you to buy it, I need to make sure it’s really, really good,” she said. “My reputation is really important to me.” She isn’t sure the same path exists for newcomers in the food-encer space, those who didn’t write New York Times recipes and books and build her brand. “If I were to try to make it today, could I make it? I don’t know.” Those who have followed Roman understand the logic—she, and her fans, are those who are willing to spend a few hours making a nice Sunday sauce, just as they are happy to whip up a quick sauce out of somehow always-way-too-quickly rotting tomatoes. Anything that removes a deterrent from cooking, Roman said, is a plus in her book. “The goal is someone just sees it on the shelf. They’re intrigued because, oh, spicy fennel or anchovy shallot,” she said. “Or they’re curious why it is expensive.” So that’s why the jar is priced accordingly. It’s for people who know they have other alternatives, but if paying for something they believe is higher quality makes them more likely to cook at home than order out, so be it. Roman explained what goes into the sauce: The garlic is sliced, not chopped, and the sauce is cooked in small batches in kettles. “There’s like a guy stirring,” she said. “And that means labor.” She hopes to lower the price as the business grows, but for now, as demand far outruns supply, she said it’s up to the consumer. She compared it to olive oil, which can cost anywhere from $4.99 to $29.99, if not more. “It’s up to the consumer to decide what it is that you want to spend your money on, and if it’s worth it,” she said. And she’s right: The first batch sold out online within 24 hours, and the sauce ran out again after its latest launch. Roman said it was more than she expected to sell but the company is working to speed up production, from ordering jars to getting sauce to the warehouse. In addition to Faire and her upstate First Bloom store, she’s opening a second store near her Brooklyn Heights apartment in December. Inside Dan Schulman’s war on the old Verizon

Fortune | FORTUNE | 2026-11-11

Visit Original Website
The former PayPal CEO left a Montana ranch to shake up one of America’s most profitable companies. At his first companywide meeting after becoming Verizon’s CEO in October 2025, Dan Schulman dispensed with the usual corporate pleasantries. Speaking without notes or slides, he delivered a message that employees of the telecommunications giant weren’t used to hearing from the top: Verizon had surrendered market share to AT&T, -T-Mobile, and lower-cost providers, and its sprawling bureaucracy had become a problem. The company was losing badly. And, he warned, layoffs were coming. It was a stark introduction to a new leader who, until recently, had been watching the deterioration unfold from a seat on Verizon’s board. Then 67, he had left an idyllic retirement in Montana to roll up his sleeves and fix it. Schulman had at first been reluctant to take the top job, turning down the offer twice. But he also felt a sense of responsibility to its customers, shareholders, and employees. Should the board have acted sooner? “Yes, we probably should have,” he concedes. Ultimately, his wife persuaded him that he could make a difference. “I came in with a little bit of a bad attitude,” he says, but adds that now he’s “absolutely loving it.” He also came in determined to dismantle the bureaucracy that had made even simple decisions needlessly complicated. The company, he says, had become “a big lumbering giant that had fallen behind.” In some cases, approving a decision required 35 signatures. Verizon’s competitive troubles were mounting, too. In 2024, T-Mobile added a net 3.1 million postpaid phone lines and AT&T added 1.65 million; Verizon added less than 900,000. In the first three quarters of 2025, Verizon’s consumer business lost a net 414,000 postpaid phone lines, including 356,000 in the first quarter alone. Verizon remains the largest U.S. wireless carrier, generating billions in profits. But as its peers have narrowed the gap in network quality, the advantage that once let Verizon command a premium is becoming a baseline expectation for any major wireless carrier. As if Verizon’s existing challenges weren’t daunting enough, Elon Musk’s company, SpaceX, purchased an $8 billion portfolio of low-band cellular spectrum licenses in October, a move that lays the groundwork to compete more directly with traditional wireless giants. For years, Verizon summed up its pitch to American consumers in a familiar TV refrain: “Can you hear me now? Good.” The commercials featured a bespectacled Verizon employee, played by actor Paul Marcarelli, roaming the country and testing his service in remote locations. The catchphrase became a cultural touchstone, helping cement Verizon’s reputation for network reliability. But over time, that advantage became harder to monetize as network coverage became less of a differentiator and consumers increasingly judged carriers on the overall customer experience, from their monthly bills to the deals available when they needed a new phone. “Customers don’t want to pay for a great network; that’s the expectation,” says Kevin Zavaglia, Verizon’s chief commercial officer for sales and service. (Adding insult to injury, Marcarelli resurfaced in 2016 as pitchman for rival Sprint, this time touting its network.) Just over a year into Schulman’s tenure, Verizon is showing early signs of a turnaround. In the third quarter, it gained a net 406,000 phone lines, compared with just 44,000 a year earlier, its strongest third-quarter showing in five years. It also added 137,000 customer accounts, its best quarterly performance in more than a decade, even as the company slashed spending on promotional offers by 41%. The results offer a promising snapshot, but Schulman is attempting something far more ambitious than a rebound in subscriber growth: He is trying to dismantle decades of ingrained habits at one of America’s largest companies, forcing his near-93,300 employees to think differently, take risks, and move faster. The greatest danger, Schulman says, is inertia as AI and new competitors threaten to reshape the wireless business. One tendency he encountered at Verizon was managers setting goals they were confident they could meet, then treating hitting those targets as success. Schulman recalls a manager proudly presenting a plan to raise a sales closing rate from 15% to 25%. “So, are you saying at the end of that, we are losing 75% of the time?” Schulman asked incredulously. He’s pushing executives to set more ambitious goals, even if that means missing some targets, rather than consistently meeting easy ones. “Anytime the pace of change external is greater than the pace of change internal,” he says, “you’re falling behind.” Tony Skiadas, Verizon’s chief financial officer, described how the company’s pricing strategy had become a costly cycle. Verizon had raised prices, contributing to customer defections, then spent heavily on promotions to win customers back. “We’ve seen that movie before over the years, and we know how it ends,” he says. Roxanne Austin, a Verizon director, recalls watching the company finish third among the major wireless carriers for 20 consecutive quarters, losing customers and market share while management kept prioritizing network infrastructure over customers’ changing needs. “I’m like, ‘Guys, we’ve got to pay attention. Something’s happening,’ ” she remembers thinking. “Our playbook didn’t change for five years.” (Hans Vestberg, Verizon’s CEO from 2018 to 2025, declined to comment.) Austin likens the scale and urgency of Schulman’s turnaround task to “turning an ocean liner at the speed of a race car.” And given the complexity of telecommunications, she adds, he must somehow “land that ocean liner on the moon, too.” After stepping down as PayPal’s CEO in 2023 after nearly a decade at the helm, Schulman had settled happily into retirement on a Montana ranch. He rode horses daily, helped herd the couple’s roughly 900 head of cattle, and spent time with his children. Schulman, who was originally from New Jersey but had worn cowboy boots since eighth grade, finally had the lifestyle to match his footwear. “I did not miss the CEO job at all,” he says. Now, back on an executive’s grueling schedule, Schulman wants to make every hour count. The mantra for the company’s new era under Schulman is familiar corporate gospel: Put customers first. But living up to it will require fundamental shifts at Verizon, including sacrificing some short-term revenue to rebuild customer trust. One of Schulman’s early decisions was to cancel a planned price increase. Verizon is also looking to compete more aggressively for customers with deals like the new Simplicity offering, which lowers the starting monthly price for a single line from $65 to as low as $30. And to reduce churn, it is offering incentives for customers to bundle cell phone service with home internet, including its Fios service. Schulman made steep cost cuts, including the promised layoffs: A month after his appointment Verizon cut roughly 13,000 jobs, or about 13% of the workforce. Verizon had conducted smaller rounds of layoffs before, but Schulman wanted to send a stronger signal. “I really needed to show that this was different,” he says. “That I was very serious about the change that was going to happen.” Schulman has set uncompromising expectations. He demands intensity, long hours, and a willingness to make difficult decisions. He expects managers to know their businesses inside out and answer questions on the spot. In meetings, when executives suggest taking a discussion offline, he challenges them: “Why would we take it offline? Like, we’re all here.” Schulman readily describes himself as competitive and exacting of his employees, and he accepts that some may find his leadership style too demanding and leave. “And I’m okay with it, one way or another,” he says. As he puts it, “Heaven and hell are the same place. It’s just heaven for some and hell for others.” The growing demand for infrastructure to support artificial intelligence offers new opportunities for Verizon, particularly in providing the high-capacity fiber networks connecting data centers. Verizon has signed agreements with Google, Amazon Web Services, and others worth billions, Schulman says. AI is also helping improve systems within Verizon—for example, tools that help customer service employees quickly find information about plans, products, and policies. Zavaglia says AI has cut about six minutes from the in-store phone buying process. As for Musk’s threat to challenge the incumbents in the mobile business, Schulman says he takes SpaceX’s ambitions “quite seriously.” But he estimates that building a network to rival Verizon’s would could take four to six years and cost some $300 billion. SpaceX also has other businesses competing for investment and attention, he points out. And even if Musk commits the resources, Schulman argues, he could end up with a network much like Verizon’s, without a significant cost advantage. “Frankly, at that point, I welcome the opportunity to compete against them,” Schulman said during Verizon’s quarterly earnings call in October. “It’s very hard to throw a punch against something that doesn’t exist. Families kept lining up at a military monument on the National Mall, so it stayed for Veterans Day

If you visited the Lincoln Memorial this summer or fall, you likely came across a different kind of memorial that normally isn’t there. This year, to celebrate the country’s 250th anniversary, the insurance and banking company USAA, which serves military members, veterans, and their families, built two giant American flags displaying the names of over 41 million military service members. Dubbed the National Military Monument, the installation was supposed to last less than a month, but the company says families just kept coming—and so it’s sticking around for Veterans Day after the company sought an extension to keep up with the overwhelming number of visitors. And it turns out that some of the people lining up at the Lincoln Memorial Plaza aren’t there for ol’ Honest Abe. They’ve come for the pair of double-sided digital displays, each about 18 feet tall and over 50 feet wide. Up close, names of service members scroll across the screens by era. From afar, the displays resemble American flags waving in the wind. George Washington’s name is listed first, the Unknown Soldier, last. USAA, which funded the monument after becoming an official America 250 partner earlier this year, declined to disclose the total cost. Visitors can search for a relative or even themselves at interactive kiosks, and USAA staff are available on-site to help bring up individual names on the monument’s screens. “It’s very uplifting to be able to stand here and to know that their sacrifice was not in vain,” said a visitor in a USAA video announcing the monument. The goal is to honor everyone who has served since the Revolutionary War: Veterans who came home, those killed in war, and military personnel who are still serving today. That adds up to roughly 41 million searchable names so far, though not everyone is there yet, because the nation’s military records have gaps of their own. On weekends, the wait has sometimes topped three hours, USAA’s lead communications director Christian Bove told Fortune. Visitors in the USAA announcement video described traveling from as far as Puerto Rico. Families can still visit through Veterans Day on Nov. 11, when the monument’s current permit ends. A USAA spokesperson said the company plans to announce what comes next for the monument later this week. Some families traveled from afar and were pleasantly surprised to find the name of someone they love on the screen, long waits be damned. Bove himself recalled one such moment during his first visit in October after the monument was built. Sitting on a bench in front of the Lincoln Memorial, he overheard a teenage boy calling his grandfather to say he had found his name and was about to send him a photograph. “It was so cool to hear the excitement in his voice and know he was able to share that moment with his grandfather,” Bove said. It’s not just the grandkids having fun, but the grandparents too. Delbert “Ray” Houlette, a 95-year-old Marine who served in the Korean War, was shown a photograph of his name on the monument after a friend of a FOX5 reporter helped arrange it. “I’ve been a Marine since 17 and I’m still a Marine at 95,” Houlette told the Las Vegas station. Building the monument took several months of planning, research, and construction, Bove said. Assembling the names meant working around gaps in the historical record. No complete, publicly available list of everyone who has served exists, so USAA drew from military and public records—including World War II Army enlistment records and prisoner-of-war lists—as well as veterans service organizations and USAA members who opted in. Some records were lost decades ago. A 1973 fire at the National Personnel Records Center destroyed roughly 16 million to 18 million military personnel files. Northern Virginia Magazine reported that those losses complicated the monument’s research. Privacy protections account for other omissions. USAA says it requires verified consent to include currently serving members and many former service members whose records are not yet public. Families can use the monument’s website to search and submit missing names and additional records for inclusion. Because the monument stands on public parkland, USAA secured a special-use art installation permit from the National Park Service, Bove said. It opened July 28 and was originally due to close Aug. 23. USAA sought an extension because of the visitor response, and the park service approved it. The installation is free to visit from 10 a.m. to 10 p.m. daily through Nov. 12, and the online database will remain searchable after it closes. “We knew we wanted to celebrate and honor all 41 million of our veterans and service members, and what better place to do that than on our National Mall,” Bove said. The metals industry has experienced transformational moments before: the Bessemer process, flash smelting, electric arc furnaces, and modern continuous casting all changed what was possible. Yet, for more than a century, most extraction processes rely on techniques and pathways that would be familiar to metallurgists from generations ago. Processes have become larger and control systems smarter, but the fundamental science behind separating metals from ores has changed less than many realize. Today’s materials challenges are different from those of the 20th century, but why are critical metals still produced using largely the same scientific foundations? EvoMaterials, a technology platform developed by Outokumpu—the global leader in sustainable stainless steel and the producer of ferrochrome with 67% lower carbon footprint than the industry average—is exploring that question by rethinking how metals can be extracted: “We decided to go beyond the current constraints and look fundamentally at what’s the best way to do this with the benefit of the scientific engineering knowledge and hindsight that we have today,” says Caspar Stinn, technical director for extractive metallurgy at Outokumpu. “We’re looking at how we can reimagine metals production.” As economies expand and the world becomes increasingly electrified, data and scientific models point to rising demand for many minerals, driven by technologies ranging from data centers to aerospace, water treatment, energy systems, and defence. According to Antoine Allanore, professor of metallurgy at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) this coincides with many Western economies having reduced their domestic processing capacity for the very metals those industries depend on. Metallurgy is now strategically important, shaped not only by cost and availability but also by carbon footprint, geographic origin and supply-chain resilience. That reality is particularly relevant for chromium and other specialty metals used in high-temperature and extreme environments. Chromium-based materials play a critical role in aerospace systems, power generation, corrosion-resistant infrastructure and defense technologies, where materials must perform under demanding conditions. For decades, metallurgical innovation largely focused on improving yields, energy efficiency and process control. The platform originates from research into sulfidation-based processing technologies, initially pursued to reduce carbon emissions associated with ferrochrome production. Laboratory work revealed broader potential. Lab-scale trials demonstrated that these alternative process chemistries could potentially do more than lower emissions. They could recover valuable metals from lower grade resources and industrial side streams while fundamentally altering how separation processes are performed. The opportunity is to rethink how mineral resources are used and how much value can be extracted from them, bringing in greater resource efficiency and stronger industrial competitiveness. Historically, that separation has been achieved using carbon- and energy-intensive approaches. The new method creates a different chemical environment that enables more efficient extraction with fewer losses and fewer unwanted by-products, recovering critical materials from resources considered waste. EvoMaterials sits at the intersection of several scientific disciplines, a combination that strongly resonates with Stinn, who completed his PhD in Materials Science and Engineering at MIT, where his research focused on low-carbon metals processing and recycling technologies. Modern materials innovation draws on a growing mix of chemistry, materials science, physics, computational modeling, and engineering. Allanore compares EvoMaterials to platform technologies in other industries, where a single scientific breakthrough can enable multiple downstream applications. Once a new extraction pathway has been mastered for one material system, the same scientific principles may be adapted to entirely different metals and mineral resources. But spending a few days in Data Center Alley left me wondering if it’s really the proxy that Lehane describes. I came away convinced that the data center backlash is more about electricity than Big Tech admits. The industry can improve its image, but it can’t escape the physical consequences of its enormous appetite for electricity. Here’s why. There are two ways to power the sort of data center that AI companies want: connecting to the grid, or generating electricity onsite. In Virginia’s Loudoun County, where data centers are ubiquitous but have been built at a steadier pace than the breakneck expansion now underway elsewhere in the country, most are connected to the existing grid. One facility I went inside, operated by Equinix, has massive diesel generators onsite for backup but gets its power from the local utility, Dominion Energy. Much of Dominion’s electricity is generated by natural gas (which is cleaner than coal or oil, but worse than renewables). The average data center operating in this part of Virginia today has a power capacity of 54 megawatts. That’s more than the power draw of the Denver International Airport. Elena Schlossberg, a prominent activist against data centers in Virginia, told me she’s “always banging my head against the wall” when the industry paints the fight as a culture war. “It was always about power,” she says—that is, electricity. Some Virginia protesters appear more animated by a broader anti-tech populism than Schlossberg, to be fair. But it’s the particular consequences of drawing so much power from the grid that have done the most to shape how the backlash has played out. Recent protests have led to new rules from the state that data centers must pay fully for the cost of new transmission lines to the grid and make sure they’re not spread among everyone else. Data center developers told me they’re preparing for new rules that would cut them off from grid power during peak demand. Schlossberg also worries about the downstream effects of a strained grid—for example, that data centers will run more diesel generators or that Dominion Energy will expand fossil fuel inputs rather than renewables to meet demand. The overwhelming majority of AI data centers being built elsewhere in the country are not designed this way. The scale desired is so massive that companies are setting up in rural areas where there is virtually no grid infrastructure to connect to. Building there means bringing your own power (so-called “behind the meter” power), in the form of natural-gas-powered turbines. Companies also build diesel generators for backup. Anthropic, for example, has reportedly agreed to pay $3.4 billion for gas turbines in what’s described as the largest power generation purchase by an AI company. Even if grids were nearby, they’d be unlikely to serve the amount of power demanded. The smallest data centers attractive for AI training are 300 megawatts. It’s a lot of power for one industrial site, but not unprecedented: One data center developer told me he’s eyeing a former steel mill already equipped to draw that much electricity from the grid. But the ideal facilities are more like 6,000 megawatts. That’s the estimated draw of the city of Chicago, which is powered by more than 20 power plants within the city alone (and dozens farther out). The notion of data center companies’ bringing their own power might seem attractive at first, because they wouldn’t be straining the grid. But generating electricity at this scale inevitably affects surrounding communities, and when public utilities build infrastructure of this magnitude, communities have traditionally expected a say in where it goes. Now, private companies are undertaking projects on a similar scale. Natural-gas turbines of any scale release air pollutants. When diesel generators are used, it’s even worse. And in the unprecedented rush to get these facilities operating, companies have become horrible neighbors (recall that Elon Musk violated air quality laws when he set up dozens of turbines to power a data center in Memphis, according to the Environmental Protection Agency). Big Tech’s tendency to chalk the backlash up to a culture war obfuscates one simple fact: There’s only so pleasant you can make a small city of computers to live around. Sure, companies can pursue better deals that raise tax revenues, hire local workers, and get more community involvement, as the protests are leading them to do. But if a small town is asked to welcome a Walt Disney World, an international airport, a steel plant, and the power plants to run them all, should we be surprised by its trepidation? Again, that’s not to say this is the sole reason for opposition to data centers. Fears of job losses, existential risks, and concentrated corporate power all fuel the backlash, as does misinformation (most facilities use far less water than many people think). But as one developer recently told me, industry insiders see their ambitions as constrained less by public opposition than by a lack of electricity. That should tell us something. Companies might also try their chances abroad; Google, in fact, announced the day after the elections that it will spend $15 billion on data center projects in Malaysia. Product design workflows were built for an era when shipping code was the bottleneck. Now that engineering is faster, design has become the constraint. Product designer Vitalii Khomenko argues for a new workflow where designers ship faster by combining systems thinking with AI-assisted iteration, and the handoff happens earlier rather than after extensive polish. With what he counts as more than 250 client engagements across agency and embedded in-house roles, Vitalii Khomenko has seen product teams from the inside. Here he makes the case for a new kind of design workflow built for the AI era, and explains why the window to adapt is narrower than most founders think. For the last decade, every startup conversation about speed eventually became a conversation about engineering. Hiring more developers, cutting scope, choosing a simpler stack, it was almost always a dev-side problem. Vitalii Khomenko, Principal Designer at Orizon Design and founder of Fluente LLC, argues that’s no longer true. And he has a specific view on what teams should do instead. Khomenko’s view comes from the shape of his experience. He has worked both as an external specialist brought in to deliver and as an embedded designer connecting product, brand and execution, which he says shows him where friction actually originates inside product teams, and where it doesn’t. His work spans early-stage startups finding their first visual language and established brands managing complex, multi-disciplinary design systems. Across that range, certain patterns repeat. And the pattern that started becoming impossible to ignore this year pointed him toward a conclusion that is reshaping how he thinks a modern design workflow should actually be structured. The bottleneck didn’t disappear. It moved. And the workflow needs to move with it. When shipping code was slow, the existing design workflow made sense. Multiple rounds of revision, user testing, iteration, all of that fit naturally into the window while engineering caught up. That window is now gone, and most design workflows were never updated to reflect it. “Founders are asking design teams to move at the same speed as an AI-assisted dev pipeline, and design doesn’t compress the same way code does,” Khomenko says. The result is a workflow that was built around a constraint that no longer exists, applied to a pipeline that has fundamentally changed around it. The forced choice between polish and speed that product teams are now navigating isn’t new as a concept. What’s new is how stark it has become, and how directly it traces back to a workflow problem rather than a people problem. “Because development is cheap and fast now, there’s constant pressure to ship the next thing immediately,” he explains. “But the products that actually retain users are still the ones where the details were considered carefully. Fewer revision rounds isn’t a process improvement if it means shipping avoidable friction.” Khomenko has watched this play out across enough teams, at enough stages, to be specific about where the existing workflow breaks down. The issue isn’t design judgment. It’s where that judgment sits in the process, and what format it’s expected to produce at the end. To understand Khomenko’s proposed fix, it helps to understand exactly where the current workflow stalls. For most product teams, the end of the design process looks like this: a designer produces polished, dev-ready screens inside Figma, and hands that file to engineering. Engineering then interprets those screens, translates the visual decisions into code, and builds from there. That translation step, Figma file to working code, is where the pipeline now breaks down. AI-assisted engineering has made the building part dramatically faster. But the translation step hasn’t changed. An engineer still has to read a Figma file, interpret spacing, extract assets, make judgment calls about component structure, and write the implementation from scratch. That process takes the same amount of time it always did. “The manual workflow inside Figma simply isn’t keeping up with AI-boosted engineering,” Khomenko says. “The design handoff is quietly becoming the thing everyone is waiting on.” The result is a pipeline where everything around the handoff has accelerated, and the handoff itself has become the single point of drag. Figma, in other words, was built for a world where engineering was the slow part. It wasn’t designed to feed a pipeline where engineering moves at AI speed. This is the core of what Khomenko is proposing, and it’s a more significant structural shift than it might first appear. In his workflow, Figma doesn’t disappear. It moves. Rather than serving as both the exploration environment and the final delivery artifact, Figma becomes the visual exploration layer exclusively: the place where brand identity is established, layout logic is worked through, and design direction is validated. It remains the right tool for figuring out what something should look and feel like. But the final delivery, the artifact that actually goes to engineering, is code. Specifically, components built into prepared libraries that engineering can work from and scale directly, without any translation step in between. The difference in implementation speed is the point. When a designer delivers a coded component library rather than a Figma file, engineering doesn’t need to interpret anything. The decisions are already expressed in the language they’re building in. What previously required an engineer to read, interpret, and re-create from a visual reference now arrives ready to use. In a pipeline running on AI-assisted development, that difference compounds fast. “Figma is still the right place to explore what something should look and feel like,” Khomenko says. “But handing over a Figma file and asking engineering to implement it is now the slow path. Delivering in code is the fast path. That’s the shift.” Executing this workflow requires a different kind of designer, and Khomenko is direct about that. The UI/UX designer becoming a Design Engineer is, in his assessment, not a distant possibility. It is a matter of time, and the teams treating it as a future consideration rather than a present reality are the ones most likely to find themselves stuck at the handoff. But Khomenko is equally clear about what this shift doesn’t mean. Delivering in code is one part of the role, not the whole of it. The full design cycle isn’t going anywhere. Applying branding to imagery, producing animations, building marketing materials, these remain essential creative tasks that require a broad and varied toolkit well beyond code. “The designer of the near future isn’t just generating code,” he says. “They’re moving across the full cycle, brand, motion, marketing, product, with design engineering as an important and growing part of that stack, not a replacement for it.” What changes is that code-ready delivery becomes a core expectation within that stack. The designer who can move fluidly from visual exploration in Figma to component delivery in code, while also carrying the brand thinking, motion, and marketing creativity that differentiates products at the surface level users actually experience, is the profile that the new workflow demands. Khomenko is candid that no single version of this workflow has fully standardized yet. Every team is figuring it out differently. The tools are evolving, the handoff expectations are shifting, and the processes teams are building around all of it vary considerably. There is no off-the-shelf playbook. Having worked with many teams, at different stages and through several cycles of tooling change, Khomenko says some patterns are already clear even while the specifics are still in flux. “Each team’s workflow is really custom at this stage,” he says. “But some general patterns of how this is going to look in the near future are already visible.” Those patterns consistently point in the same direction: design judgment moving earlier, final delivery moving into code, and the designer’s role expanding in technical range while staying broad in creative scope. The exact tools and sequences will keep shifting. The underlying logic of the workflow Khomenko is describing is already taking shape in the teams paying closest attention. For founders and product leads, the workflow Khomenko is describing has direct implications for how teams are staffed, budgeted, and structured. Front-load design judgment. The biggest leverage in the new workflow isn’t faster execution, it’s better problem definition before generation starts. Bring senior design thinking in earlier, when it can shape what gets built rather than polish what’s already done. Separate exploration from delivery. Figma and the visual exploration phase serve a distinct purpose from the final delivery layer. Treat them as separate stages with separate expectations, rather than collapsing them into a single step expected to produce everything. Shift the delivery expectation to code. Whether that means hiring for Design Engineer range, developing it internally, or restructuring handoffs, the end of the design pipeline needs to arrive in a form engineering can work from directly. The translation step is where time goes to die, and it’s now the most avoidable part of the process. Protect the full creative stack. Design engineering matters more than it ever has. It is not, however, the whole job. Teams that reduce design to code generation will lose the brand thinking, motion, and marketing creativity that differentiates products at the surface level users actually experience. Stay flexible. The workflow is still forming. Teams that build rigidity into their process now will find themselves redoing that work within a year. The teams getting this right are the ones building enough adaptability into their structures to absorb what’s still coming. “The Figma handoff made sense when engineering was the slow part,” Khomenko says. “Engineering isn’t the slow part anymore. Delivering in code isn’t the future of design workflow. For the teams paying attention, it’s already the present.” Vitalii Khomenko is Principal Designer at Orizon Design and founder of Fluente LLC. Zendesk moves to pay-per-result AI pricing with its new Outcome AI Platform

The Next Web | 2026-11-10

Visit Original Website
Zendesk is changing how businesses pay for its AI, charging them for problems solved rather than for the number of people using the software. The customer service company announced the change on Tuesday at its AI Summit in San Francisco, as part of a new Outcome AI Platform. Under the new plans, customers set an ‘Outcome Budget’ and pay for each eligible issue the AI resolves or task it completes. Zendesk checks each result for quality and completeness, so customers can see what counts towards their budget. As a result, businesses can start small and spend more as the AI takes on more work. Zendesk has backed this model before. Earlier this year, it argued alongside Sierra that AI agents should be paid per result, not per seat, and OpenAI has started trying a similar approach with some customers. Tapan Patel, a research director at IDC, said the change reflects how business buyers now want to pay for AI. Linking cost to verified results, he added, makes it clearer what customers get for their money. The platform is also built for a world where people send their own AI assistants to deal with companies. To help with that, Zendesk is launching the Open Service Directory, a public list where brands can register their official support channels. AI assistants can then use it to find verified help, even for brands that do not use Zendesk. Brands control what they share and when a human must step in. The directory does not access private customer data or take any action itself. Early members include Betterment, ElevenLabs and Telekom Payment. Meanwhile, a tool called Zendesk Bridge lets businesses connect AI agents and apps of their choice, such as ChatGPT, to Zendesk’s systems. Setting up a connection starts with a name, which is kept for audit logs, and a plain description of what the outside agent should and should not do. Administrators then choose exactly what each agent may do, such as reading, creating, answering, updating or merging tickets. An assistant called Admin Copilot suggests which tools to switch on, based on the description. Every action then passes through Bridge, which checks permissions and company rules and asks for human approval where needed. An observability page shows how many requests came from AI agents and how many from people, which ones were blocked and why. It also includes a kill switch. Bridge is in early access, and the directory is launching soon. “People don’t think about which channels, systems, or departments they need to navigate to interact with a business,” said Zendesk chief executive Tom Eggemeier. Other new tools, now generally available, help teams build and improve their AI agents. AI Agent Launchpad studies past customer conversations and suggests where AI could take over. In Zendesk’s demo for a scooter rental firm, it proposes a step-by-step process for rides that will not end, using a connected billing system such as Stripe. Similarly, Agentic Analytics lets managers ask questions about their data in plain language, such as how many tickets are still open. They can then see whether changes are cutting repeat contacts. The launch reflects changing habits, according to a Zendesk survey. It found that 52% of consumers are comfortable relying on personal AI assistants for everyday tasks. Even so, 84% said human help should always be available. The announcement follows Zendesk’s purchase of Forethought, its biggest deal in two decades, as it builds out its AI agents.
