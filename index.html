The new human-crafted Apple Music brand matters in an AI world

Apple just revealed a new brand identity for Apple Music, with the expected turn to Liquid Glass. I’m not much of a fan of their new design language in general; this is where I admit that I’m still running MacOS Sequoia and have grumpy things to say about rounded corner radii and text legibility. It’s just a trend, it will pass, and at some point I’ll probably cave and upgrade when it starts actually affecting my ability to get work done, or more importantly get security updates. Once I adjust, I’ll stop noticing it, and life will go on. As such, I wouldn’t normally find an obvious rebrand like this notable, much less feel like I had much to say about it. But then I happened to catch Apple’s behind-the-scenes video in my YouTube recommendations, and I realized I did have thoughts. It’s a short clip, respecting your time at just a little over a minute long if you want to watch for yourself. Let’s get the obvious part out of the way: It’s pretty peak Apple. More than a little pretentious, with all the resources a $5 trillion market cap can bring to bear on every problem. There’s a design agency (TBWA/Media Arts Lab), a pop hitmaker music producer (Fred again.. and yes, that’s how you spell it), a whole crewed set with cameras and lighting, and a mineral oil tank equipped with a transducer to effectively transmit audio signals into physical ripples. They shot a ton of footage of the oil (chosen for its higher viscosity) interacting with the branding printed on transparent panels. Fast Company has a nice detailed breakdown of the technical aspects if you want to dig in more. It’s a little eye-rolly with the narration, but honestly also lovely in the execution and care. And it made me realize how much I think we’re all going to value this kind of hands-on, human crafting in an era where anything can be generated out of a prompt. Like many Ars readers, I have feelings about generative AI imagery. It’s feeling increasingly impossible to avoid in daily life. My new favorite term is “slop ghouls.” One really unfortunate side effect of that persistence is a new natural tendency to be suspicious of everything you see. Was that picture you just saw hand-drawn, or did someone prompt it into existence with styles stolen from human artists? I see us increasingly place genuine value on not just human-created craft, but seeing the process behind it that shows the hands at work. This Apple video was a neat bit of promotion, but it also provided the receipts. Real people did real things in the physical world to make the final images and videos. The final effect is obviously cleaned up and post-processed. But when I look at it now, I don’t just see Liquid Glass; I see the humans and their time and energy and creativity. To me, at least, that matters. Showing your work, proving the time you put in—I appreciate it. I’m slightly less grumpy for it! Generative AI is likely here to stay. There’s no stuffing the genie back in the bottle. I probably need to find some peace with that, though I reserve the right to still yell at clouds about it. In the meantime, I think we should do more to celebrate human involvement in things and not be shy about showing the process and behind-the-scenes work.

Judge rejects Trump’s wild claim EPA can dismiss data center pollution lawsuits

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-21

Visit Original Website
Elon Musk’s SpaceXAI is stuck facing a lawsuit over alleged data center pollution after Donald Trump failed to convince a judge that his Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has complete control over all Clean Air Act lawsuits and can dismiss any suit at whim. Soon after unpermitted gas turbines were discovered powering Musk’s giant Colossus data center in Memphis, Tennessee, the NAACP sued. Its lawsuit sought an injunction requiring xAI (which, following a merger, is known now as SpaceXAI) to use the best available pollution control technology when operating the turbines and ordering penalties up to $124,426 per day per violation. Without such remedies, the turbines risked drastically spiking pollution in already vulnerable neighborhoods housing predominantly Black communities nearby, the NAACP argued. To block the lawsuit, Trump tried to intervene, arguing that the EPA had the power to unilaterally dismiss lawsuits any time the president decides against enforcing the Clean Air Act. According to Trump, it would supposedly “not serve the public interest” if citizens were allowed to seek massive civil penalties to punish polluting AI firms that were trying to get data centers operational as fast as possible to support Trump’s AI Action Plan. But Trump’s efforts to spare Musk’s firm were halted by a federal judge Tuesday, who ruled that “nothing” in the Clean Air Act (CAA) allows the EPA Administrator to “unilaterally dismiss a citizen suit.” Such a power grab would seemingly give Trump authority to block any lawsuits alleging data center pollution, US District Judge Debra Brown’s order suggested. Even further, she observed that Trump could have used the authority to defend any interests against CAA lawsuits, not just the AI industry’s. The NAACP had argued that the CAA’s text is focused “on ensuring air pollution standards are met and people are protected.” It urged the court to recognize that there is “no way to read” a provision granting citizens a right to sue as Trump has. That would “suggest that Congress, having gone out of its way to preserve people’s ability to sue to stop unlawful pollution when the government has not, also implicitly authorized the government to dismiss those very suits at its whim because it would prefer that the unlawful pollution continue,” the NAACP successfully argued. In a press release, Laura Thoms—an enforcement director for an environmental group called Earthjustice that has partnered with the NAACP to support the lawsuit—accused Trump of seeking “unilateral and unreviewable veto power over citizen suits.” “The Trump Administration has gone out of its way to shield Elon Musk’s company, [SpaceXAI], from liability and to deny local communities their right to protect themselves against illegal pollution,” Thoms said. Thoms’ group is monitoring data center fights nationwide, not just SpaceXAI’s Colossus fight. However, Musk’s company’s battle was the first to expose the AI industry’s increasingly common practice of using unpermitted gas turbines to keep data center goals on target. So, Brown’s ruling is likely viewed as a big win for Americans worried Trump might make it impossible to stop the AI industry from polluting communities at a critical moment when data center demand for gas turbines is exploding. Praising the court for “denying the US’s unprecedented and alarming argument,” Thoms vowed to “continue the fight to hold xAI accountable for the illegal air pollution it has been pumping out around the clock.” Brown slammed the Trump EPA’s reading of the CAA as an “extraordinary position” with no grounding in federal law. Trump tried to warp a single exception that lets the EPA dismiss a CAA lawsuit when it is either suing to require CAA enforcement or otherwise acting to require compliance. Neither situation applies in this case, where the Trump administration has made it clear that it has no intention to sue SpaceXAI over the alleged violations. The federal government’s decision to ignore the problem is partly why the citizen lawsuit is necessary, the NAACP argued. In that scenario where Trump could have dismissed the NAACP’s lawsuit, courts recognize that a citizen lawsuit could clash with government efforts to enforce the CAA or reach agreements with violators. But in the SpaceXAI lawsuit, Trump argued that the government’s right to intervene, which is sometimes used to dismiss lawsuits, was enough to shut down this lawsuit, regardless of the other facts. Brown pointed out that it made no sense to argue that citizens can’t raise CAA lawsuits that the EPA opposes when the CAA also grants a right to sue the EPA administrator for failing to enforce the CAA. “Evidently, Congress contemplated that citizen suits may not always align with the EPA’s judgment,” Brown wrote. Congress even contemplated granting powers close to the authority Trump’s EPA tried to assert in this case, Brown said in a footnote. In 1990, Congress considered amending the CAA so that the EPA could substitute itself and take control of citizen suits over alleged violations. Had that happened, Trump may have been able to step in and drop the lawsuit. However, Congress did not adopt that amendment back in 1990, and Brown suggested that the mere consideration of the amendment favored the NAACP’s reading of the CAA. “At most, the proposed amendment is evidence that at least some members of the 1990 Congress believed that the citizen suit provision as written does not allow the Administrator to intervene and subsequently terminate a citizen plaintiff’s claim,” Brown wrote. Brown also acknowledged that the NAACP’s argument that every case Trump tried to use to seize power to veto the lawsuit “arose in a context where a government acted to require abatement of unlawful pollution, not one where the government acted to shield the polluter from liability.” Ultimately, she agreed that “because the Administrator has not taken any action to enforce the Clean Air Act here, these cases do not support the United States’ position.” Trump seemed particularly upset that SpaceXAI may face large fines. He argued that “punishing unlawful conduct through civil penalties payable to the Treasury on behalf of the United States is ‘a quintessentially executive power.’” As Trump explained, because the CAA is too vague on this point, “the canon of constitutional avoidance” applies, and courts must err on the side of his administration. Simply dismissing the lawsuit would avoid a constitutional clash, Trump argued. The US “fails to identify any ambiguity in the statute’s text to which the Court could and should apply the canon of constitutional avoidance,” Brown wrote. Rejecting Trump’s constitutional “doubts,” she concluded that “constitutional avoidance does not allow the Court to invent a right that does not exist in the statute.” “That the Clean Air Act’s citizen suit provision allows private parties to enforce the Clean Air Act through civil litigation, therefore, does not make citizen suits an impermissible exercise of the President’s executive power,” Brown wrote. And “it does not follow that all suits seeking civil penalties require the exercise of nondelegable executive power.” Had Trump succeeded, Americans who are increasingly opposing data centers nationwide may have been widely blocked from seeking fines when pollution can be proven. Brown said the CAA cannot be interpreted that way, though, since “it can scarcely be doubted that, for a plaintiff who is injured or faces the threat of future injury due to illegal conduct ongoing at the time of suit, a sanction that effectively abates that conduct and prevents its recurrence provides a form of redress. Civil penalties can fit that description.” Critically, “to the extent that they encourage defendants to discontinue current violations and deter them from committing future ones, they afford redress to citizen plaintiffs who are injured or threatened with injury as a consequence of ongoing unlawful conduct,” Brown wrote. When Trump took his first stab at blocking the lawsuit this summer, he didn’t cite EPA authority, just his own. He later amended his motion to specify that he was intervening on behalf of the EPA after the NAACP, and the court immediately pointed out fatal flaws in his arguments. Since then, Brown’s order suggests that Trump’s efforts to kill Musk’s lawsuit stayed messy. As a prime example, Trump goofed up by prematurely filing his appeal after the district court merely acknowledged at a hearing that it intended to dismiss the government’s motion to intervene to drop the suit. Without waiting for the actual opinion to drop, the US quickly went to an appeals court to reverse an order that did not exist yet, Brown said. On the day that Brown at last issued her order, it did not say what Trump expected it to say. Parsing the actual order, the appeals court agreed that Trump had filed his appeal prematurely and denied Trump’s request to stay the district court’s litigation. The appeals court denial came largely because Trump’s appeal was premised on Brown denying the government’s motion to intervene, which she did not. Although Brown denied Trump’s request to dismiss the lawsuit, she is still allowing the government to intervene on other grounds to support SpaceXAI, if that’s its intent. That point remains somewhat unclear, as Brown noted that the government, in its rush to intervene, never said why it’s intervening—apart from bluntly demanding the suit be dropped. She gave the government until October 23 to either explain its position or stay out of the litigation.

Does opting out of smart TV tracking work? Research finds “suspicious” traffic.

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-21

Visit Original Website
Smart TVs running operating systems from Samsung, Vizio, and Roku continue sending data to automatic content recognition (ACR) endpoints after users opt out of tracking in the devices’ settings, according to research published on Monday. The paper from Northeastern University researchers points to potential privacy violations from some of the biggest TV brands. ACR captures video screenshots or audio clips from smart TVs and sends the data to remote servers, where it’s used for things like measuring content viewership and serving targeted ads. The paper, “Watching the Watchers: Privacy Analysis of US Smart TV Automatic Content Recognition,” examined eight smart TV OSes across 12 smart TVs from 10 manufacturers (Amazon, Hisense, LG, Roku, Samsung, Sony, Supersonic, TCL, Vizio, and Westinghouse) to examine when smart TVs use ACR and how effective opting out of user tracking is. Regarding the relatively small number of TVs studied, Ilgar Mammadov, the report’s lead author and a research assistant at Northeastern, told Ars Technica: Together, the smart TV operating systems we tested cover approximately 95 percent of those used in the United States. Because ACR is implemented at the OS level, we expect that adding more TVs running the same operating systems would largely repeat our existing findings rather than reveal new behavior. The researchers made OS-specific control scripts and delivered them over Wi-Fi, while Mon(IoT)r and SPHERE Research Infrastructure software captured network traffic coming to and from each TV. The research included identifying each OS’s ACR endpoint. Some of the OSes, including Samsung’s Tizen, “expose self-disclosing ACR domains containing keywords such as ‘acr’ and ACR provider names,” the paper said. The researchers conducted 5,943 controlled experiments for the report. The report’s findings have their limits, of course. Mammadov noted that each ACR provider and OS studied implemented ACR differently, adding: They differed in the scenarios that trigger ACR, in how effective the opt-out is, and in the ACR modalities used. We would therefore expect TVs using ACR providers outside our study to behave differently as well, and our results should not be generalized to them. That said, our methodology is designed to be reusable. The researchers found that the TVs running Samsung Tizen, Vizio OS (which the report refers to by its former name, SmartCast), and Roku OS continued to send network traffic to the respective ACR endpoints when user tracking was disabled in settings and when tested for specific usage scenarios, including using a first-party streaming app. In Roku OS’ case, the tested TVs exhibited this ACR-like behavior only when using the operating system’s integrated streaming app, The Roku Channel. Further, that behavior is expected and disclosed in Roku’s terms of service and, according to the report, in the TV’s settings on TCL-branded TVs. That’s not the case for Tizen and Vizio OS TVs, which exhibited ACR-like behavior across additional usage scenarios, as shown in the table from the report below. Credit: Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International License Credit: Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International License Ars contacted Samsung and Vizio to ask why TVs running their OSes send network traffic to ACR endpoints when tracking is disabled. We’ll update this story if we get answers. Critically, encryption prevented the report’s authors from determining what the network traffic contained. However, they view the “continued presence of traffic consistent with ACR tracking after opt-out as suspicious.” “These behaviors indicate potential privacy violations; however, we cannot know for certain without access to payload content,” the report reads. Researchers found that “traffic consistent with ACR tracking” remained present in all 30 of Tizen’s first-party streaming app measurements and 41 out of 60 of its “antenna” measurements, specifically when they used a radio frequency (RF) modulator “to deliver custom broadcast TV content with both dynamic video and audio” and to “deliver simulated content with dynamic video but no audio.” Network traffic also continued after opt-out during 18 of 30 HDMI measurements on Tizen. The researchers also recorded traffic to ACR endpoints in more than half the tests in which the researchers displayed “dynamic video with audio via HDMI from an external source” on a Tizen TV. The report’s authors determined that Amazon Kinesis is “the endpoint most consistent with ACR behavior for” Vizio OS and noted that traffic to the domain stops when using third-party streaming apps and screen mirroring, consistent with Vizio’s statement in a 2021 SEC filing that it doesn’t collect ACR data from those sources. With Vizio OS, “a content-correlated endpoint hosted on Amazon Kinesis continues to receive traffic in 100 percent of opted-out scenarios.” Notably, the TVs reportedly sent network traffic to ACR endpoints when the TVs were idle. Traffic to the identified Amazon Kinesis endpoint does not disappear or decrease in any opted-out measurement… indicating that the opt-out setting has no observable effect on the traffic sent to this endpoint. Without knowing the contents of the traffic or what happens to it once it reaches remote servers, we can’t determine whether the OS operators are simply disregarding user preferences. It wouldn’t be the first time TVs have tracked users without permission, though. In 2017, the FTC said Vizio TVs used ACR to collect user data without consent. The company paid $2.2 million to settle the charges, saying its ACR software “never paired viewing data with personally identifiable information.” Vizio said it used the data only “in the ‘aggregate’ to create summary reports measuring viewing audiences or behaviors.” Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton sued Samsung, along with Hisense, LG, Sony, and TCL, last year, alleging that they use ACR to collect personal data “unlawfully.” Samsung made opting out too difficult, the lawsuit alleged. Samsung settled, agreeing to update its terms to more clearly explain its use of ACR. If the TVs are tracking users who declined tracking, it “would raise serious concerns about user consent and transparency,” Mammadov said. He said that is one of the most likely reasons the TVs are sending network traffic to ACR endpoints when tracking is disabled. Mammadov noted that Roku states in its terms of service that Roku OS tracks the use of first-party streaming apps even if users opt out of tracking. “This comparison suggests that the traffic we observed on Tizen OS and [Vizio] OS may similarly reflect ongoing data collection,” he said. Could the activity be caused by a technical bug? Even if that were the case, “the outcome for users is the same: Their opt-out choice is not reflected in the TV’s behavior,” Mammadov said. Mammadov said another possible explanation for the ongoing traffic is that data is transmitted but discarded once it reaches remote servers. The report’s authors consider this “unlikely,” Mammadov said, adding: Transmitting data only to throw it away is an inefficient way to honor an opt-out, since it consumes the user’s bandwidth and adds load on the vendor’s servers for no purpose. Of course, it is also impossible for us to audit independently. The paper highlights a troubling lack of transparency around how smart TV ACR works, what data companies are collecting, and when they collect it. “Despite its importance, little is publicly known about the privacy implications of deployed ACR implementations—in part due to the opaque nature of their deployments and challenges with automated testing,” the report says. The size of the TV market makes dissecting ACR deployments difficult, the paper says, because doing so requires “large numbers of devices and experiments.” Another obstacle is that “smart TVs are locked down,” making “techniques like rooting and TLS interception generally unavailable [and creating] a need instead for techniques that reliably infer ACR activity,” the report says. Smart TV manufacturers’  persistent, opaque tracking practices have prompted more people to explore modifying their device’s software, including using a growing number of software tools designed to turn smart TVs into dumb TVs. Meanwhile, US nonprofit Software Freedom Conservancy is suing Vizio in an effort to make it easier for people to control their TV’s software, including disabling ACR. To help people reduce the risk of their TVs tracking them without consent, the report’s authors created a list of network addresses, domains, and URLs that people can block “to prevent devices or apps from communicating with ACR services” without affecting TV viewing quality. On the paper’s website, the researchers recommended that TV manufacturers “list every input that ACR watches, such as antenna, HDMI, apps, and screen mirroring, in the privacy policy” and “authenticate and encrypt all ACR traffic.” The paper recommends that users remove smart TVs from their network and “use an external streaming device that does not perform ACR, or use the ACR opt-out on OSes where it was effective in our measurements.” For tips on avoiding smart TV snooping, check out our guide on how to break free from smart TV tracking.

For decades now, scholars have argued over what—if anything—we should have gleaned from the Stanford Prison Experiment. That it was unethical to place two dozen male college students in a university basement repurposed as a prison, designate a few of them guards and the rest prisoners, and encourage the former to discipline the latter, was apparent by the time its mastermind, Stanford psychology professor Philip Zimbardo, ended what was supposed to be a two-week exercise after only six days. As the extent of Zimbardo’s manipulation came to light, the 1971 study lost credibility in academic circles, even as its pop-cultural legend kept growing. Fifty-five years after it was conducted, perhaps the only definitive conclusion to draw from the Stanford Prison Experiment is that the Stanford Prison Experiment is not worth repeating. But just try telling that to the reality-TV machine, let alone a platform currently at work on the third, all-celebrity season of a competition series that cheerfully (and, of course, non-lethally) recreates the horrors of its international hit anticapitalist battle royale thriller Squid Game. The New Stanford Prison Experiment, Netflix’s take on a project that was always more convincing as improvisational theater than as scientific research, isn’t even television’s first crack at Zimbardo’s study. The BBC got there first with The Experiment, in 2002, whose earnest attempts to correct for the original’s missteps yielded a more nuanced, if still inherently flawed, look at authority. That the Netflix version of what can now be best understood as a reboot of a famous franchise feels less like social psychology and more like a high-concept season of Big Brother is no surprise. What’s most perplexing is its choice to incorporate female participants in a limited way—and its failure to make any real sense out of what they bring to the experience. Aware that the televised resurrection of a notoriously inhumane experiment was bound to draw scrutiny (the mere announcement of the show’s existence touched off a wave of incredulity), the producers of the six-episode series take pains to make both their safety precautions and their high-mindedness visible. Instead of a retrofitted academic building, the inmates are sequestered in a real housing pod of a Georgia correctional facility. The 30 voluntary participants are said to come from all walks of life, though what we find out about their backgrounds is generally limited to hometown, racial identity, and line of work. According to executive producer Jane Lipsitz, each “underwent psychological screenings prior to entering the experiment.” There’s a safe word any of the participants can, and some do, use to leave the roleplay early. Alison Wood Brooks and Trymaine Lee in The New Stanford Prison Experiment Netflix The appearance of legitimacy is further reinforced by a panel of three experts who provide commentary on what’s happening in the pod. Michael Cantrell, who brings 30 years of experience working in federal corrections, games out the results of the guards’ strategies. Journalist Trymaine Lee, who has covered stories ranging from Hurricane Katrina to the power of the Black electorate, was presumably tapped to bring insight into big-picture issues such as race, violence, and the justice system. But the show keeps the experts’ interjections so brief that he’s rarely afforded more than a quick sound bite. Filling a limited version of the host role is Alison Wood Brooks, a behavioral scientist on the faculty of Harvard Business School. It’s she who introduces the series with a speed-run summary of Zimbardo’s study and its “ethical breaches,” as well as the true but comically vague observation that “in today’s world, the study of power couldn’t be more relevant.” So, Brooks says, it seemed like the right time to retry the experiment in hopes of more accurately answering its original question: “Does power corrupt?” It doesn’t take long to notice that only three of the 30 participants are women—and all of them join the five men selected as guards. It’s not hard to guess why there are no female inmates. Correctional facilities are segregated by sex. Throwing men and women together in close quarters for 14 long, monotonous days is a recipe for uncomfortable, sexually charged interactions, if not full-on harassment or assault. Of course, similar dangers exist in single-sex environments. Abuse of power seems particularly likely when male guards are put in charge of female prisoners, yet precisely this dynamic is the reality in many American prisons. I suppose untrained, inexperienced guards might be expected to pose a greater threat. But you would think that the same safeguards put in place to protect participants’ physical and mental health could be extended to enable a truly coed experiment. (The show doesn’t have much to say about the existence of transgender, nonbinary, or even queer people behind bars.) The architects of New Stanford might’ve avoided all of the above and still meaningfully included women by running parallel exercises in separate pods. Instead, they only examine the behavior of three women—a number so small, it precludes any claim of representativeness—in positions of authority over men. One of them, Laura Berens, is the CEO of her own business and thus presumably comfortable in a leadership role. Shavonnah Jefferson, whose background as a security guard also seems relevant here, has no trouble asserting herself. “You have to speak up,” she says. “Because if you do not, people will run you over.” Yet only Kiana Gaona, a young, devoutly Christian social media manager elected head guard by her shiftmates, gets much screen time in early episodes. Professing to believe in gender equality despite her choice to submit to her husband, she’s a bit erratic. She makes the extreme decision to give an outcast inmate a position of power, then veers dramatically in the opposite direction, punishing him and a fellow dissident with 23 hours of confinement to a cell for talking about a rebellion. From left: Michael Correra, Jacob Jensen, Josh Hublitz, Sonny Robertson, and Brendan Hardey in The New Stanford Prison Experiment Netflix It’s these scheming inmates and the two male guards who initially claim control of their respective shifts who dominate the show for most of the season. There’s an interesting, if under-analyzed, contrast between the latter men’s leadership styles. Chris Lee, a prototypical new-school executive, wants to wield influence by inspiring his charges, while Chet Hudson, an oil rig supervisor, likens his new job to breaking horses. The resulting inconsistency fosters a bit of unrest. But it’s only after an extremely reality TV plot twist in which Lee, Hudson, and their fellow male guards switch roles with the most rebellious prisoners that a truly telling storyline emerges. Hudson, who as a guard had been the strongest proponent of a strict hierarchy, starts saying things like: “When people unite, no matter what, they’re in control of the power.” Then he makes a big deal of leaving the experiment early, claiming that he has nothing to learn from being a prisoner. For a certain kind of person, one that’s really having a moment right now in American politics, inequality only becomes a concern once they lose their place at the top. Although the men’s role reversal remains the dominant drama, the shakeup also puts a new spotlight on the women, who are reassigned to an all-female shift. They quickly settle on Berens as head guard, citing her leadership experience. (It’s worth noting that neither of the two Black guards, Jefferson and Nicholas Washington, are shown to be considered by their peers for head guard.) “The female shift gave me an opportunity,” Berens reflects. “Obviously, the guys on my shift weren’t treating me super equal.” Unfortunately, this isn’t obvious based on the footage we’re shown, and it certainly doesn’t explain why Berens, the sole woman on her original shift, joined the majority that elected Hudson head guard. Regardless, she proves to be the most competent leader in the experiment, striking the right balance between firm rules and humane treatment. Her best moment happens when the shift made up of rebellious ex-prisoners loses control and she steps in to quiet the inmates and advise her counterpart to protect his guards. Aside from the self-evident notion that some women make strong leaders (and some men weak ones), it’s hard to know what the takeaway from the introduction of an all-female shift is supposed to be. Maybe because they really do all respect women in positions of authority, or more likely because they don’t want millions of people watching them act sexist on TV, the inmates barely adjust their behavior when Berens’ team walks in. Another probable reason for their compliance is that they’re so intent on antagonizing the ex-inmate shift, which many feel is being rewarded for bad behavior. The women don’t come into the pod with the same baggage. From left: Shavonnah Jefferson, Hadley McDaniel, Joey Malloque, Laura Berens, and Chris Lee in The New Stanford Prison Experiment Netflix The expert commentators don’t really help make sense of the situation. When it comes to the gender issue, they could’ve supplied context about differences in how men and women typically wield authority or how men tend to perceive women in power. Maybe they did and that footage didn’t make it into the final edit. Either way, we see a lot of the experts noting that the all-female shift is fascinating without digging into specifics. The closest we get to synthesis comes from Berens. “I guess I really did have 22 men listening to me,” she marvels, attributing her success to kindness, humor, and mutual respect. “What if there are more women leaders who can lead in this type of way?” This is a subject far older than this Lean-In-era epiphany, yet New Stanford stops short at gesturing faintly in the direction of conversations that could theoretically be had. The series’ shallow approach to gender doesn’t just weaken its depiction of the three female participants. It also neglects to acknowledge the explicitly gendered aspects of the male hierarchy, with its charismatic, physically strong leaders and smaller, less socially adept outcasts. In that sense, New Stanford replicates an ancient form of patriarchal bias that casts men as the norm and women as an atypical other. Maybe it’s silly to criticize something as inherently unserious as a reality show for failing to internalize the lessons of The Second Sex. But this particular reality show tries to justify its controversial existence by proclaiming its seriousness with regards to psychology, philosophy, and current events. So it seems fair to judge it by those lofty ambitions. To that end, it isn’t only gender that the series glosses over. Racial disparities occasionally surface—one Black inmate justifies his obedience to authority by explaining that for his community, fighting back might result in an “accidental shooting”—yet go largely unexamined. Anyone hoping for timely debate over the purpose or efficacy of the criminal justice system is bound to be disappointed. By the time the guards are dismissed, in the finale, so that the prisoners can take responsibility for their own fates, the whole “experiment” feels like a bait-and-switch. New Stanford wants to reel in viewers with the notoriety of the original study and the promise that hot buttons will be pushed. But then, perhaps because the producers are afraid to alienate any segment of Netflix’s diverse audience, it shrinks from every opportunity to make meaning out of what happens in that housing pod. And so, as different as the two exercises are, this latest Stanford Prison Experiment becomes as pointless as the first one.

On Oct. 19, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved dostarlimab (brand name: Jemperli) to treat a specific type of rectal cancer called microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H)/mismatch repair deficient cancer. This cancer is caused by the accumulation of certain genetic mutations that prevent rectal cells from repairing DNA damage and accounts for 5-10% of rectal cancers. In a study of 154 people with this type of rectal cancer who were treated with dostarlimab, 68% had a complete clinical response at one year, which meant doctors could not see evidence of tumors on imaging or other methods for tracking the tumors. In the study, which the drug's manufacturer, GSK, submitted to the FDA, people received nine infusions of the drug: once every three weeks for six months. “Not only did they reach clinical complete response, but they maintained that for a year,” says Dr. Andrea Cercek, section head of colorectal cancer at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center and principal investigator of the study. (Some of the earliest patients have continued to remain tumor-free for even longer. ) “That really suggests that the response is durable and real.” Dostarlimab is a checkpoint inhibitor, which means it releases the brakes that hold back the immune system from recognizing and attacking tumors. The drug allows immune cells to destroy cancer, making it a powerful weapon for seeking out cancer cells no matter where they spread or hide. “We have completely leapfrogged over standard of care with a simple immunotherapy that melts the tumor away,” says Dr. Luis Diaz, head of solid tumor oncology at Memorial Sloan Kettering and one of the trial investigators. Standard treatment for rectal cancer involves chemotherapy, radiation, and surgery to remove the rectum that requires patients to wear colostomy bags for the rest of their lives. The dostarlimab approval means more patients whose tumors have specific mutations could avoid those aggressive therapies altogether. “We have patients who would have lost the ability to bear children who have had children because this therapy replaced chemotherapy, radiation, and life-altering surgery in one swoop," Diaz says. "That’s a big deal.” Kelly Spill is one of them. Diagnosed in 2020 when she was 29, shortly after having her first baby, Spill spent nearly nine months trying to figure out why she continued to have constipation and see bloody discharge in the toilet. Because she had recently given birth, doctors first attributed the symptoms to her pregnancy, and when they persisted after delivery, to internal hemorrhoids. It wasn’t until a doctor ordered a colonoscopy that they found a tumor. “The doctor told me it was Stage III colorectal cancer, and that the treatment plan would include chemo, radiation, and surgery. And that I would never carry another baby again, and would most likely have a colostomy bag for the rest of my life,” says Spill. “It didn’t seem real until I heard the words that I was not going to carry another baby. I immediately stopped thinking about me, and started thinking about my husband and what it meant for my son. We had just started our lives together; we were supposed to have all these kids.” Sascha Roth also faced devastating news when she was diagnosed with rectal cancer in 2019 after seeing blood in her stool. A resident of Washington, D.C., she had a friend with a similar diagnosis who had received chemotherapy, radiation, and surgery and recommended his surgeon at Memorial Sloan Kettering. Roth assumed she would follow the same strategy, but she learned that she had Stage III cancer and that she wasn’t a good candidate for surgery because the tumor was located so low in the rectum. Her tumor also had mutations that suggested it wouldn’t respond well to chemotherapy. “It was a double whammy that threw me down,” she says. But the surgeon introduced her to Cercek, who was waiting to hear from the FDA about starting an immunotherapy trial for patients like Roth. “They told me I would be the first patient in the study, and there wasn’t scientific evidence that it would work.” Still, Roth wanted to try. She agreed to become Memorial Sloan Kettering’s first patient on the study, and then spent two months waiting for the FDA to greenlight the trial so she could begin receiving the drug. “I was calling Sloan Kettering incessantly to see if the FDA approved [the study],” she says. “The second the FDA gave its stamp of approval, Dr. Cercek’s team called me to New York to start the first round of treatment less than a week later.” Not long after that, at Spill's first appointment at Memorial Sloan Kettering to discuss chemotherapy, she learned that the mutations on her tumor made her a good candidate for a study they had just begun. Only three patients had enrolled ahead of her (including Roth), so there was no data on whether it would actually treat her cancer. Spill, who was at the appointment with her mother, decided she had nothing to lose by joining the trial. “If it doesn’t go well, we could go back to option A,” Spill remembers her mother saying. By her second infusion, Spill felt the tumor was shrinking because she no longer had major constipation. By the fourth infusion, scans showed the tumor had shrunk by 50%. The study originally called for patients to get chemotherapy, radiation, and surgery if needed after six months on dostarlimab. But a nurse pulled Spill’s mother aside after that appointment to tell her that the first three patients in the study did not need any additional treatments because their tumors had disappeared. Following Roth’s results, the study was revised to make chemotherapy, radiation, and surgery an option only if needed: if the tumors hadn’t gone away. It wasn’t a guarantee that Spill would have the same results as those first patients, but “it gave me hope, and all I could think about was maybe I could have another baby,” she says. After all of her dostarlimab infusions, Spill, too, no longer had any tumor left and was declared cancer-free. Her doctors advised her to wait two years before trying to get pregnant again to ensure the cancer wouldn’t return. Once she was cleared, she got pregnant and gave birth to a girl in 2023, and later a boy in 2025. “I am now five years cancer-free, and last September got the boot from Memorial Sloan Kettering,” she says, meaning she no longer needs to follow up with her oncologists there. She now sees her primary care doctor for colonoscopies every two years. “I see beauty in absolutely everything in life now,” Spill says. Not only does the immunotherapy appear to work well in treating these rectal cancers, but it also comes with relatively few side effects. About 20% of those in the study reported fatigue, rash, diarrhea, and uncontrollable itching. Roth, who received her infusions during the COVID lockdown, drove from D.C. for her treatments and was able to make the roundtrip in one day. "It's just proof that you can really do the things you want to do. I didn't feel tired or sick after [the treatment], and there was never a time when I was at home recovering," she says. "I watched my mom and dad go through standard chemo and radiation and anticipated that would be what my treatment was like. It was nothing like that." You’ve been standing in line for 20 minutes, famished and desperate to order one of the viral bagels that keep selling out—or for six hours outside a concert venue, determined to claim a spot at the barricade to see your favorite band. And then someone strolls up and slips in line ahead of you. Apparently, the rest of you have been standing around for the ambiance. How much of a sin are line-cutters committing? “You are ripping the fabric of society apart,” says Nick Leighton, an etiquette expert and co-host of the podcast Were You Raised by Wolves? Queue-jumpers have to live with that, he adds. “But worse, we have to live with you.” Here’s exactly how to handle this maddening situation—including when and how best to speak up. If a stranger jumping ahead of you feels strangely personal, Richard C. Larson, a professor emeritus of data, systems, and society at MIT who has studied queuing, understands why. “We only have a finite number of minutes of life on Earth,” he says. The cutter’s implied message: “A minute of my life is worth more than a minute of yours.” Before you decide whether to confront the offender, think about what speaking up would accomplish. Are you trying to reclaim your turn or get revenge? Sometimes, the impulse is “to sort of let them know you think they’re a jerk,” says Ryan Martin, a professor of psychology and dean of the College of Arts, Humanities and Social Sciences at the University of Wisconsin–Green Bay, known online as the Anger Professor because he studies strong emotions. Martin would speak up to help someone recognize “that what they did wasn’t OK and that I wanted them to go to the back of the line,” he says. But he’d also consider: “What is going to allow me to have the kind of night I want to have?” If you spend the concert replaying a confrontation instead of singing your happy heart out, you’ve lost more than your place in line. Leighton's gut check for situations like these is to ask yourself: Is this the hill you want to die on? “That’s always a good etiquette question,” he says. Consider your safety, too. If the person seems hostile or you feel uneasy approaching them, you can ask staff for help or let the cut go. “Health and safety always trumps etiquette,” Leighton says. Marie Helweg-Larsen, a professor of psychology at Dickinson College who studies social norms, has explored what makes line-cutting so rage-inducing. For a 2008 study, she and a colleague surveyed U2 fans waiting for prime concert-floor spots, asking how they would react to hypothetical line-cutting scenarios. Interestingly, fans reacted just as negatively to someone cutting behind them as ahead of them—even though their own place wasn’t affected. “You have to have people follow the rules,” she says, “or your investment into the line falls apart.” Helweg-Larsen’s co-author also heard fans invoke a higher authority when someone misbehaved: “Bono wouldn’t like that!” You don’t need a rock star’s moral authority—or a grand speech—to point out where the line starts. Helweg-Larsen’s suggested opener: “Excuse me, but the line starts down there.” Aim for an “‘I’m just trying to be helpful’ kind of vibe,” she says. That approach gives the person a chance to correct their mistake without having to defend their character. And you don’t have to believe it was an innocent oversight to deliver the message graciously. “Even if they’re doing it on purpose, you still need to use that tone,” Leighton says. He’s been on the receiving end of a polite correction himself: At a deli, he once joined what he thought was the back of the line, only to discover he’d guessed wrong. He was happy for someone to point him in the right direction. If you decide to speak up, do it promptly. “Your window of opportunity is short,” Leighton says. “As soon as it happens, that’s when you need to say something.” Waiting until you’ve spent five minutes glaring at the back of someone’s head won’t make the conversation any easier. For a line that represents a serious investment of time, Martin suggests spelling that out: “Hey, I’m not sure you noticed, but there’s actually a really long line here, and people have been waiting all day.” You’ve identified the problem and given them an opening to say “Oops.” Of course, some situations are murkier than a stranger breezing past the entire queue. Someone returning from a bathroom break is different from four friends arriving hours later to join the person who saved their places. Leighton is sympathetic to a water or restroom run. Holding spots all day for a last-minute entourage? “I don’t love that,” he says. Sometimes, your kind reminder won’t be met with the same level of helpfulness: They saw the line. They’re staying put. Now what? If you feel comfortable continuing, Martin suggests a firmer follow-up: “I really think you should go back to the back of the line.” You’re making a direct request without adding an insult. Larson advises skipping any confrontational nicknames or demands—like “What do you think you’re doing, bud?” You’re pointing someone toward the back of the line, not challenging them to a duel. If the person still refuses to move, resist the urge to keep arguing. “We can’t control everybody,” Martin says. Continuing to push someone who’s already refused could lead to “a more significant confrontation.” At a concert venue, amusement park, or other staffed location, you can ask an employee or security person to step in. “You can always appeal to a higher authority,” Leighton says. Whether they’ll enforce the rules is another question, but it’s an option worth trying when the cutting is particularly egregious. If you decide to drop it, drop the parting shot, too. A sarcastic “Must be nice!” might feel irresistible, but Leighton advises against adding more rudeness to the encounter. “It will not achieve anything,” he says. As Larson puts it, “A fight can ruin your whole day—or life.”

They are known for a strong bond between carbon and fluorine, which the environment struggles to break down. PFAS are everywhere: They spew from factories and slough off products and into water with normal use. Scientists have found them in everything from human blood to Antarctic snow. One 2022 report found that 89% of the US population had PFAS in excess of two nanograms per milliliter of blood plasma, a common threshold for potential adverse health effects, and 9% of the population had passed the riskier level of 20 nanograms per milliliter.
