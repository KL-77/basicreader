What did the Liberty Bell sound like? Science is on the case.

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-15

Visit Original Website
The Liberty Bell, with its famous crack, hasn’t been rung in 180 years, but that hasn’t stopped scientists from pondering what the bell might have sounded like when tolling. In honor of the nation’s 250th anniversary, researchers at Penn State University have modeled its acoustic properties, drawing on data collected from one of the bell’s replicas. The work was first presented at a meeting of the Acoustical Society of America in Philadelphia earlier this year, followed by an article in Physics Today. Historians aren’t sure exactly when the Liberty Bell acquired its distinctive crack, just that it occurred sometime between 1817 and 1846. An 1846 account in the Public Ledger described the ringing of the bell on February 23 that year and mentioned that the bell had developed a crack, which someone had attempted to fix by filing the sides down. But it didn’t work: The February 23 ringing extended the crack even more, so much so that “the old Independence Bell … now hangs in the great steeple irreparably cracked and forever dumb.” Nobody has heard it ring since. This wasn’t the first time the bell had cracked; the rim cracked shortly after it arrived in Philadelphia in 1752, the first time the clapper struck while testing the sound. It was recast by two local foundry workers, John Pass and John Stow, not once but twice, because the first recast produced a sound that one bystander likened to coal scuttles banging together. The second recast’s sound was deemed acceptable, and the bell was typically used to summon the Assembly, among other purposes, until it fell into disuse after the American Revolutionary War. As for why the bell was so prone to cracking, a 1975 study of the bell’s chemical composition revealed that the metals used formed “an extremely brittle alloy” that also made it easier for souvenir collectors to chip off pieces from the rim. We do know something about the general acoustics of bells, dating back at least to a seminal 1987 paper by Thomas D. Rossing and Robert Perrin exploring the forms and vibrational modes of church bells, carillon bells, and handbells. A given bell’s sound is determined by relatively simple geometry—which determines the vibration patterns radiating sound to listeners’ ears—and the material of which it is made (bronze), which determines the exact pitch and duration of the ring. Per Sean Collier, co-author of this latest article, relying solely on the meager online information about the Liberty Bell to replicate its sound produces “a terrible sounding bell that in presence does not match the silhouette of the iconic bell.” There are several full-size replicas of the Liberty Bell made in the 20th century, including one housed on the Penn State Behrend campus, and one can tap that bell to produce a ring. But scientists desire more quantitative information, such as the specific notes, which notes ring the longest, and how the bell deforms as it vibrates. And that requires modal analysis, a technique used by bell manufacturers to assess tuning and make any necessary corrections. For instance, Western church bells are typically tuned to the first five normal frequencies, according to the authors—the hum note, fundamental (the note that is most dominant to the human ear), tierce, quint, and nominal—following well-defined ideal ratios. “Musically, the frequencies sound as a minor triad, which is why church bells are commonly perceived as having a somber tone,” they wrote. Further, “The human ear perceives the pitch of a bell as a subjective tone called the strike note,” arising from the combination of how the mode shapes interact, and the listener’s own expectations. Collier and his co-authors, Jon Young and Aaron Stearns, used the Penn State Behrend replica to collect data on mode shapes, natural frequencies, and loss factors. They glued accelerometers to different parts of the replica and then tapped various areas on the bell with a hammer equipped with a force gauge. The accelerometers captured the bell’s response to being tapped, and the force gauge measured the force of impact as a function of time. The authors then extracted the bell’s natural vibrational frequencies from that data, along with the corresponding mode shapes and loss factors. The results: The replica’s hum note was 174 Hz; the fundamental was 323 Hz; the tierce was 381 Hz; the quint was 510 Hz; and the nominal was 623 Hz. The ratios of those frequencies fell very close to those of an ideally tuned bell. And the mode shapes of the fundamental frequency closely matched those of typical Western church bells. Measuring loss factors proved trickiest. Collier et al.’s measured values were within the typical range, but loss factors are impacted by the type of bronze used and casting imperfections. The 20th-century bell-making processes used to cast the replica were different from those in the 18th century used to cast the original Liberty Bell. So the loss factors might have been similar; it’s just harder to predict. Physics Today, 2026. DOI: 10.1063/pt.103283cd08  (About DOIs).

Russian drones are suspected of sinking a cargo ship and leaving chemical tankers ablaze near the Black Sea coasts of Bulgaria and Romania, respectively. The latest attacks mean that at least seven ships have been attacked in the exclusive economic zones of NATO member countries—and even within Romania’s territorial waters if Russian involvement is confirmed in the case of the chemical tankers. Such attacks have spurred requests for NATO to redeploy counter-drone systems to provide more protection. In a social media post on October 15, NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte stated that NATO had increased “counter-drone capabilities” along the alliance’s eastern flank after meeting with the leaders of Bulgaria and Romania. The latest attacks this week occurred within 24 hours between October 14 and October 15, when the two chemical tankers were set afire after Russian drones were detected in Romanian airspace, according to Deutsche Welle. Both ships were sitting at anchor near the mouth of the Sulina canal that connects the Black Sea to the Danube River, which has become a crucial shipping export route for Ukraine, Reuters reported. The Comoros-flagged chemical tanker Yara J was struck within Romania’s territorial waters. All crew members were rescued and accounted for. However, Romanian authorities are still searching for seven missing sailors after rescuing five people from the San Marino-flagged tanker Wine Trader, according to Reuters. This second tanker was struck while sitting just one mile outside Romanian territorial waters. Reuters cited an anonymous NATO source as attributing the drone attacks to Russia. A NATO statement also described a Marshall Islands-flagged bulk carrier named Sea Destiny as also having been targeted inside Romanian waters overnight, along with a Tanzanian-flagged tanker named Dania J coming under attack in the Danube port city of Izmail in Ukraine. But there were no immediate details about damage or casualties. This comes just days after suspected Russian drone attacks sank a cargo ship and forced the evacuation of another vessel within two dozen miles of the coast of Bulgaria in the Black Sea on October 13. Those attacks occurred within Bulgaria’s exclusive economic zone. One of the cargo ships that came under attack was the Ukrainian-operated coastal freighter Chelsea 3, registered under the flag of Malta. Bulgarian Prime Minister Rumen Radev told the Russian ambassador in Sofia that “it makes no sense for Ukraine to destroy its own ships in the Black Sea, and the results of these strikes favor Russia’s interests,” according to The Associated Press. All 15 members of the Ukrainian-owned Chelsea 3 were safely evacuated by Bulgarian rescue operations after the ship was reportedly struck by three aerial drones. But authorities were still searching for 11 missing crew members of the Guinea-Bissau-flagged cargo ship Dignity, which sank after coming under attack, The Associated Press reported. Five mariners were initially rescued from that ship. These incidents mark the latest in a growing series of unprecedented attacks on Black Sea shipping in the exclusive economic zones of NATO countries. One week earlier, aerial and sea drones sank the Togo-flagged cargo ship Alfa Watan and left the Palau-flagged cargo ship Able afire about 80 miles (128 kilometers) off the coast of Bulgaria on October 6. The 18 crew members of the Able were rescued—two required hospitalization—but the 10-member crew of the Alfa Watan has been presumed dead after rescuers failed to find any survivors. Such incidents first began when the Turkish-owned cargo ship Royad Mammadov came under drone attack and eventually sank near Romania’s Black Sea coast on October 5. All 11 crew members were rescued. In response to the attacks, Bulgaria has stepped up cooperation with affected NATO member countries and the alliance as a whole. On October 14, Bulgarian Defense Minister Dimitar Stoyanov told the national parliament that the country would join NATO’s Drone Edge initiative that includes member countries committing a total of $40 billion toward developing more counter-drone capabilities over the next five years, according to independent news publication The Sofia Globe. NATO officials began examining the possibility of moving existing anti-drone countermeasures to the region after receiving requests from Bulgaria, Romania, and Turkey, diplomats told Politico. The three countries are also cooperating on an initiative to safeguard freedom of navigation in the Black Sea, which Bulgaria Prime Minister Radev plans to present to the European Union this week. Naval forces from NATO countries have avoided military exercises in the Black Sea since Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, in accordance with the 1936 Montreux Convention that grants Turkey authority to restrict passage of ships through the straits leading into the Black Sea. This Turkish enforcement has also prevented Russia from reinforcing or rotating its Black Sea fleet during the war. Despite lacking much of a conventional navy, Ukraine has managed to largely neutralize Russia’s warships and disrupt Russian-linked shipping and ports by using aerial and sea drones. But Russia has countered with its own drone attacks on Ukrainian ports and vessels, along with sometimes targeting ships from other countries in the region. Since the fall of 2023, Ukrainian ships have typically stuck to sea lanes running through Romanian, Bulgarian, or Turkish territorial waters in the Black Sea, according to the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace. Such Black Sea shipping is especially crucial for Ukraine’s agricultural exports of grain, accounting for well over 90 percent. The recent spate of probable Russian attacks in the Black Sea is now disrupting shipping carrying Ukraine’s latest harvest to the rest of Europe, with some cargo being rerouted overland or through the River Danube, Politico reported. These unprecedented attacks in waters near NATO countries coincide with a growing wave of incidents attributed to Russian hybrid or gray-zone warfare tactics. Such incidents include an attempted explosive drone attack on Ukrainian cargo aircraft at Leipzig Airport in Germany and a Russian drone boat loaded with explosives found floating near a Romanian offshore gas platform in the Black Sea. But since the start of the war, Turkey, Romania, and Bulgaria have continued upgrading their navies while forging closer cooperation on maritime security, according to Carnegie. The growing dangers for shipping in the Black Sea will likely accelerate this cooperative trend—and for the NATO alliance as a whole. Microplastics in water aren’t inert and make damaging chemicals on their own

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-15

Visit Original Website
One reason plastics are used so often is that they’re chemically inert, meaning bags, food containers, and cups don’t react with what we put in them. Yet they can still break down into microplastics, which turn up in drinking water, human blood, arteries, and brains. The consequences of that are uncertain. “Whether microplastics can directly cause human disease is still being researched,” said Wei Min, a chemist at Columbia University. In a new Nature study, Min’s team took a step toward answering this question by demonstrating that microplastics are likely not as chemically inert as we might expect. Min’s lab has spent years working on microdroplet chemistry, studying how water broken into tiny droplets behaves at its boundary with air. These droplets have been reported to generate reactive oxygen species, or ROS, a family of harmful, oxygen-containing molecules. “ROS will essentially bump into proteins, lipids, DNA, and cause damage,” Min said. “You could break a DNA strand into segments. You could mutate DNA; you could misfold proteins.” Many animal studies have tied microplastic toxicity to oxidative stress, which is caused by ROS. So the team wondered if the boundary between water and plastic might do something similar. “We got inspired by that field,” Min said. In the first test, the team stirred polystyrene powder into water containing methyl orange, a yellow dye that ROS break apart. Over 10 days, the solution faded almost to colorless, losing nearly 90 percent of the dye. In ethanol or other solvents, the dye stayed intact. Min and his colleagues concluded that whatever chemistry polystyrene was undergoing needed water to happen. So they ran more tests. First was electron spin resonance, a technique that directly detects radicals—atoms or molecules with an unpaired electron that makes them particularly reactive. The polystyrene powder and water mixture contained hydroxyl radicals, a highly reactive ROS. Next, the team used a fluorescent sensor that lights up only when it reacts with hydroxyl radicals. “You don’t see it, but once it reacts with local ROS, it turns into a bright color,” Min said. Under a microscope, the glow concentrated on the surfaces of the plastic particles and kept building over time. The effect was not unique to polystyrene either. Scientists tested six common plastics—polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene, PVC, PET, and acrylic—and all of them generated radicals. The most water-repellent plastics, polyethylene and polypropylene, were the most active, while PET and acrylic, which contain chemical groups that attract water, were the least. “The simplest answer is the most water-repellent ones change the water structure the most,” Min said. In ordinary, or bulk, water, each molecule is held in a network of hydrogen bonds with its neighbors. But a few layers of water molecules pressed against a water-repellent surface lose some of those partners. “Their structure is no longer the regular water structure. They are perturbed, and their energy is also significantly increased,” Min said. Water always contains a small number of hydroxide ions, and turning one into a hydroxyl radical requires stripping away an electron. In bulk water, that costs a lot of energy. But simulations run by the group showed that, at a polyethylene surface, hydroxide ions short on hydrogen bonds needed far less energy to form radicals. On top of that, the plastic surfaces build up a negative electric charge in water, which further lowered the energy cost. But Min’s team still had doubts to address before making any conclusive claims. Min’s earlier work on microdroplet chemistry has drawn a lot of criticism, with some researchers arguing that the reported reactive chemicals come from contamination or measurement artifacts. To avoid similar objections, Min and his colleagues used several independent detection methods. They also added a detergent-like surfactant that coats the plastic and disrupts the water layer at its surface. They found that the more surfactant they added, the weaker the plastic’s electric charge became and the fewer radicals it produced. “We only change one parameter while keeping all the rest [the same],” Min said. “We’re using exactly the same plastics, the same water, the same everything.” Impurities, Min explained, are typically stable molecules with no reason to spontaneously generate radicals. He is careful not to overstate it, though. “To be absolutely clear, we cannot 100 percent rule out the involvement of impurities,” he said. “But our controls and our theoretical computation studies highly suggest that this is intrinsic to the microplastic-water interface.” Finally, the team tested if this interface chemistry matters for biological molecules. And it most likely does. Proteins and DNA can gather on plastic surfaces, and lysozyme, a common test protein, also began clumping into aggregates there. Adding caffeine, which mops up radicals, largely stopped the clumping, pointing to the radicals as the cause. Mouse red blood cells that touched polystyrene particles broke into fragments, and the damage from each type of plastic tracked how many radicals it produced. The effect also persisted in blood serum. Min stressed, though, that this indicates that microplastics make people sick by generating ROS. While animal studies often point to ROS as possible roots of oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, ROS can be produced in many ways, including through basic metabolic activity. Min admitted comparing the magnitude of this effect to the impact of ROS our own cells make is rather tricky. “If you are really at the interface, you could be significantly affected by this effect, whereas if you are just a few microns away, you don’t feel it anymore,” Min said. Exposure is another unknown here. Min also emphasized that no one can yet say how many microplastics an average person takes in each year from food, water, and air. The plastics in the study were mostly fresh, lab-bought powders, unlike weathered particles we encounter in the environment. In the team’s tests, older particles with more oxidized surfaces produced somewhat weaker signals. “This aging process is very heterogeneous,” Min said. “Some microplastic particles were made 50 years ago, some were made five days ago.” Finally, there is the question of size. Microplastics are usually defined as particles smaller than five millimeters, while the minimum cutoff varies between 100 and 1,000 nanometers. Min’s team did not test how variation in microplastic particle size affects water-interface chemistry, but the researchers suspect it might play a role. The same mass of plastic broken into nanoparticles has far more surface area, which might make the effect larger. “This may be another reason for nanoplastics to be more dangerous,” Min said. The researchers argue that if water-repellent surfaces drive the water interface chemistry, plastics could be designed to be less water-repellent, either by changing their molecular structure or including additives. First, though, the team wants to focus on the question their study left open. “We definitely want to see how much this effect is directly contributing to disease or biological problems in animals and humans,” Min said. On Thursday, Donald Trump called off the Pentagon’s highly controversial plans to livestream a firing squad execution that is still on schedule for December 3. However, Trump emphasized in interviews that it wasn’t up to Hegseth to decide if the livestream would happen the way the secretary of defense prescribed on the podcast. “I’ll make a decision, ultimately. Neutron's first launch slips again amid trouble with carbon composites

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-15

Visit Original Website
Rocket Lab announced Wednesday that its Neutron launch vehicle won’t fly until 2027, citing an issue discovered during manufacturing of the rocket’s carbon composite structures. The announcement was public confirmation of what nearly everyone inside Rocket Lab and the wider space industry already knew. The most recent official update from Rocket Lab in August suggested the company still held hope of delivering the first Neutron rocket to its launch pad at Wallops Island, Virginia, before the end of this year, a schedule that would have almost certainly pushed the first flight into 2027. But Rocket Lab’s statement Wednesday about Neutron’s schedule suggests the company is still struggling with manufacturing the rocket’s large booster stage. Neutron’s reusable first stage is made of carbon composite structures, which offer low weight, high strength, and corrosion resistance. But carbon composites in aerospace are generally harder to manufacture than metallic structures. Peter Beck, Rocket Lab’s founder and CEO, identified a manufacturing defect as the reason for Neutron’s latest schedule slip. The problem lies in the dome sections at the ends of the rocket’s first stage propellant tanks. “Recently, we identified an issue with the manufacturing process for the propellant domes specifically, and this caused us to delay production and investigate further,” Beck said in a video released by Rocket Lab. Engineers discovered voids, or air pockets, between layers of carbon fiber that make up the domes’ composite structure. Carbon composite manufacturing involves laying down carbon fiber layers in a mold, then curing, or heating, the material to bind the fibers together. But this is a tricky process, especially for large structures that won’t fit into an autoclave, a high-pressure thermal curing chamber common in modern aerospace factories. The Neutron rocket’s first stage is the “largest pressurized structure ever made out of carbon composite,” Beck said. “Because of its scale, we’ve had to develop new out-of-autoclave processes to build Neutron, and this includes custom cure cycles for the very large pieces, like the forward and aft domes for the propellant tanks on Stage One.” Inspections revealed voids in a newly cured forward dome, defects that could affect the structure’s strength, durability, and reliability, Beck said. “They don’t necessarily mean that the dome would fail in flight, but our practice is to proceed only when we believe the system has the appropriate margins.” Engineers traced the voids to a recent design change intended to strengthen the rocket’s structure. Rocket Lab introduced the design change after a Neutron propellant tank unexpectedly ruptured during a hydrostatic pressure test in January, a failure blamed on a manufacturing defect at a “critical join” in the tank. Officials said the failed carbon composite tank was manufactured by a “third-party contractor using a manual hand-lay process,” a decision made to allow tank testing to move forward while Rocket Lab brought online an automated fiber placement machine, which is now operational. Rocket Lab also deleted joints in the first stage tank structure and increased the thickness of some sections of the carbon composite. “Those thicker sections are where the voids formed,” Beck said. Rocket Lab said the problem appears to be solved after modifying the manufacturing process again. An aft propellant dome made with the updated procedure passed inspection. “We’ve solved the problem,” Beck said. “That gives us complete confidence to move forward with the new forward dome. That process to make a new forward dome, combining with all the time that it took to investigate, identify, and resolve the issue, has pushed Neutron’s first launch date to next year.” The Neutron rocket is significantly larger than Rocket Lab’s Electron launch vehicle, also made of carbon fiber. The light-class Electron rocket is 3.9 feet (1.2 meters) in diameter, while the medium-lift Neutron measures 23 feet (7 meters) across at its widest. Rocket Lab completed qualification testing on Neutron’s expendable second stage and payload fairing last year. The rocket’s design is unique: The upper stage and payload rest inside the first stage’s nose cone during the initial phases of launch. A few minutes after liftoff, Neutron’s booster stage will open its clamshell-like nose cone to release the upper stage and payload to continue accelerating into orbit. Then the first stage will close its payload fairing and return to landing on an offshore platform for reuse. Neutron’s first stage methane and liquid oxygen tanks are the largest elements of the rocket, and they must withstand internal pressure and the rigors of launch and reentry. Rocket Lab’s checklist for the first Neutron flight is long. Final qualification of the first stage is still pending, as is qualification of the rocket’s methane-fueled Archimedes engine, nine of which will power the Neutron booster. Rocket Lab reported in August that it was heading into the “final stretch” of engine qualification testing. Once that is complete, teams will begin the launch campaign on Virginia’s Eastern Shore. Launch campaigns for new rockets often take months before a first flight, involving multiple integrated fueling tests, countdown rehearsals, and a static fire. Engineers often discover new problems during integrated testing, requiring repeat tests and tweaks to procedures and hardware on the launch pad and the rocket. That is the purpose of such testing. Rocket Lab announced the Neutron rocket in 2021, aiming for a first flight in 2024. The Neutron development timeline has now doubled. Beck said Wednesday that Neutron’s development remains “on an ambitious timeline.” In previous statements, Beck has emphasized Rocket Lab’s focus on quality and its unwillingness to launch an “unproven product.” “Our aim is to make orbit on the first try,” Beck said last year. “You won’t see us using some qualifier about us just clearing the pad, and claiming success and whatnot, and that means that we don’t want to learn something during Neutron’s first flight that could be learned on the ground during the testing phase.” Rocket Lab has achieved great success with its Electron rocket, with a capacity of deploying up to 660 pounds (300 kilograms) of payload into a low-altitude orbit. Electron has made 97 flights to date, with 56 consecutive successes. With Neutron, Rocket Lab seeks to compete for a wider range of launch contracts, eyeing opportunities to sell launches to the US military and operators of satellite megaconstellations. Its lift capability to low-Earth orbit is nearly 29,000 pounds (13,000 kilograms). “We’re building the program’s long-term infrastructure at the same time as we build our first rocket, so that we can scale quickly, unlock global launch crunches, and commercial customers, governments, and our own future satellite services are also counting on it,” Beck said Wednesday. “But I’ve also said that we won’t rush to the pad with unnecessary risk, and that development programs like Neutron have their challenges.” Neutron’s latest challenge popped up just as the rocket’s prospective customers clamor for more launch capacity. SpaceX, the market leader, is pivoting to the giant Starship rocket and will, in the next few years, retire from commercial service its workhorse Falcon 9 rocket, without which many satellite startups would be unable to put their payloads into orbit affordably. That puts Rocket Lab in the mix to scoop up business from satellite operators whose payloads don’t match up with Starship’s capabilities. But there’s stiff competition. Other rocket companies, such as Relativity Space, Stoke Space, and Firefly Aerospace, are working on medium-lift rockets to address the same market.

Tanden can reflexively rattle off the particularities of housing protocols I couldn’t begin to decode; she titters with satisfaction upon recounting the intricacies of a long-discarded Australian cap-and-trade proposal. This is a skillset of someone who is happiest in the darkest corners of government—the swampiest of swamps—a realm of acronyms, white papers, and multihyphenate bureaucracies. And that is exactly what makes Tanden’s transformation into one of the country’s most widely derided Twitter pundits so bizarre. It all began, like so many things did, in 2016. Tanden—who has close ties with the Clintons, and has spent her entire professional life within the ironworks of the Democratic Party—backed Hillary for president. (She served as an unofficial adviser to the former first lady and secretary of state throughout her run.) Meanwhile, an amassing contingency of voters regarded Clinton tepidly at best, and were more inspired by Bernie Sanders. As the primary grew testier, Tanden took to Twitter to defend the sanctity of normie liberal values, and to implore (some might say scold) Sanders supporters into lining up behind the establishment candidate. (“I’m old enough to remember when the left was the strongest fighters against fascist governments,” she posted, just months before the election.) Some portions of Tanden’s audience were amenable to her frustrations, but others were happy to make her a vessel for their exasperations with the overarching Democratic brand. They called her an empty suit, or a soulless enabler of corporate politics, or, at other times, a drunk and an idiot. Tanden only dug in further, her annoyance with the party’s leftward flank recursively expanding, in a stalemate that persists to this day. Tanden has since cemented her status as the unlikely face of a feud that continues to bifurcate the Democratic polity; a wound that dramatically fissured in 2016, and, to this point, has yet to heal. She isn’t exactly a firebrand, but she is very much available; willing to take on all comers, vociferously, at seemingly any hour of the day. If you scroll through Twitter, you might encounter Tanden engaging in a testy back-and-forth with progressive journalist Mehdi Hasan. Other times, you’ll see her riposting a man named @JrGoob. The pattern repeats ad nauseum, in slightly different permutations. I’ve seen Tanden get into a headline-making spat with progressive blogger Matt Bruenig; I’ve even watched her carry on a one-sided vendetta against Susan Sarandon. (Tanden was furious about the actress’s support of perennial Green Party nominee Jill Stein.) Unsurprisingly, her current focus is the aforementioned Hasan Piker, a man who is no stranger to controversy and has courted plenty of internet feuds of his own. Throughout the summer of 2026, when Piker was popping up on various midterm campaign trails, Tanden condemned him as a hatemonger who dabbles in antisemitic tropes—someone who has no place in the big tent—with her usual aplomb. “Proud to be one of the people fighting off the predator,” said Tanden, just days ago, while quote-tweeting a profile of Piker by veteran center-left columnist Jonathan Chait, which does, indeed, characterize the streamer as a marauding force within Democratic politics. It is the prolificness of her social media output that made Tanden one of the very few people in politics to suffer professional ramifications from her conduct online. In 2021, when the Biden administration nominated Tanden to run the characteristically opaque Office of Management and Budget, senators on either side of the aisle roadblocked the confirmation. One of them was Bernie Sanders; he wasn’t happy with Tanden’s tweets. You might be wondering what use any of this is. Especially considering how, online, Tanden appears to be profoundly alone. Sure, she has her allies in and around the Democratic Party, but her fellow policy nerds are not spending their evenings on X. She often seems to be a solitary voice crying out against the onslaught. Every tweet is met with an infinite scroll of rejoinders; no observation she posits goes unpunished. And yet, despite all the tirades thrown Tanden’s way, the isolation doesn’t bother her. Perhaps because she understands that the internet is not real life, and that her message is resonating with a silent majority. More than anything else, that’s what keeps her around. “There was a woman on the Hill who told me she was in a group chat where people share my tweets and they appreciate me standing up, even though people scream at me,” said Tanden. “Even when everyone is yelling at me, people will hear what I’m saying.” Really, If you ask her about any of her online kerfuffles, Tanden sticks to that refrain: “People viciously attack normie Dems, and nobody defends them,” she said. “The problem with the online world is that people can say insane things, and it becomes conventional wisdom unless you have an alternative.” Through that lens, clapping back at Bot420Crazy rings a bit more existential—a battle for the soul of liberalism that Tanden genuinely feels is at risk of being hijacked, and can perhaps be course-corrected by browbeating one shitposter after another. Frankly, that might be what is required of our think tank executives in 2026, in an era of mass disinformation and flatlining public trust. It is not enough to do the work of pragmatic policymaking. You must be prepared to stridently defend it to the trolls. And it’s important to remember that Tanden has been involved in basically every big-ticket Democratic Party project of her lifetime. She had stints on Bill Clinton’s presidential bids, and Hillary Clinton’s senatorial operation. She burrowed deep in the recesses of the early Obama administration, welding together the voluminous Affordable Care Act. She volunteered for the Dukakis campaign, for God’s sake. In fact, she is so enmeshed with the Democratic establishment that Joe Biden did eventually find a job for her. (She chaired the gloriously ambiguous “Domestic Policy Council.”) All of this has made Tanden something of an idealist. She believes in principled liberalism with the exact same vigor with which the revolutionary vanguard in her mentions wants to dismantle our sclerotic institutions. No wonder they’ve never seen eye to eye. “I’m a big government-policy wonk. That’s my whole life. I’m a big government liberal. And most people in government are trying to solve problems,” Tanden told me. “This might sound absurd, given the level of cynicism, but I actually do believe the Democratic Party is a force for good in this world.” The problem, of course, is that a waning number of Americans agree with Tanden. The Democratic Party is unpopular, and has been for years. Liberal consensus is smarting after two humiliating defeats by Donald Trump. Nobody should be surprised that voters are seeking alternatives further out on the left, as a handful of key Senate races this midterms cycle demonstrate. For instance: Tanden is on record endorsing Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez’s Green New Deal resolution. One ambitious report published by the think tank floated the radical idea of curbing the credit-card swipe fees enforced at grocery stores. And how about the fact that Patrick Gaspard, who ran CAP while Tanden was away in the Biden world, has since emerged as one of Zohran Mamdani’s advisers? Sure, she’s done plenty to rankle hardcore leftists beyond her posts. Chief among them is an incident in 2015, when CAP hosted Israel’s Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu for a softball Q&A. (Tanden brought this up unprompted during our interview. She blamed a Pollyanna-ish Obama administration. “Ben Rhodes was like, ‘This is a good thing to do.’ I would have never done it, but they were trying to get him on a path to be less insane,” she said. For what it’s worth, CAP has struck a more adversarial tone toward Israel in the intervening years.) Naturally, the past decade of abuse has taken a toll on Tanden. She speaks about herself like she’s a martyr for the Democratic mainstream—a human shield sponging up all the slings and arrows at the left on behalf of those in her coalition who have much more at stake. Tanden willingly participates in the roasting, and as a woman of color, she’s destined to be on the brunt end of the internet, but as far as she’s concerned, it’s all been worth it. “I’m not a politician, I’m never going to run for office. I’m not going to lose any voters,” she said. “The only consequence is that people might yell at me online.” Here is where I must admit that, like so many other Americans, I too have lost some faith in the liberal orthodoxies she holds dear. I am one of those Obama-voting millennials beaten down by the MAGA imperium, to the point that I’m now no longer convinced that a return to normalcy is even possible. And ultimately, that is what Tanden is up against. She remains confident that with the right people and policies in place, the ossified gridlock gumming up the mechanisms of American democracy can be purged, without drawing too far outside the lines. In the past, she has portrayed those casting doubts that such a resolution could be achieved as irksome malcontents. For a time she used the term “alt-left” to refer to disaffected socialism-curious youths, drawing direct parallels between them, and, you know, the Charlottesville Nazis. One of Tanden’s most consistent mantras is that she, and people like her, are more serious about gaining power and saving democracy than those further on the fringes. (“Please name a competitive election where your preferred politics has actually won,” she memorably barbed leftie commentator Jeet Heer in 2018.) Alas, Tanden is committed to that dream during a moment in our national story when it has become disturbingly common to believe such an idea to be laughably fantastical, or worse, totally contemptible. With that said, the more time I spent around CAP, the more I found myself narcotized by the contact high of establishment liberalism. The hope and the change, the transcendent conviction that the systemic levers of government are not corroded beyond repair, the optimist I used to be—it all came rushing back. I traipsed down corridors full of smart young people tapping away at their computers, seemingly doing things. I passed by auditoriums with heavily fractionalized pie-charts projected onto massive screens. The hallway to the bathrooms was lined with the movie posters that CAP has screened as part of their free film series, Reel Progress. (They included the much-ridiculed 2004 Best Picture winner Crash, and a Pete Buttigieg documentary produced by Amazon.) Eventually, I am deposited into a conference room with two CAP agents who tell me about their plan to defang Citizens United, the 2010 Supreme Court decision that allowed unfettered corporate money into our elections. They intend to accomplish this with a complicated retrofitting of the corporate chartering code that would allow individual states to rescind the ability for companies to make political donations. They’ve been plugging away on this policy for years, and they’re finally seeing results. The measure has already passed in Hawaiʻi, and it will be on the ballot in Montana come November. I found the encounter winsomely demystifying. This is really what these people do all day. If only more Americans understood that behind the checkered partition of Washington, you’ll mostly find a legion of wonks tinkering with their pet projects. Despite so much evidence to the contrary, some folks, Tanden included, are trying to rescue this country through the raw vehicles of competence. I took my leave of CAP and jumped on the Amtrak back home to New York City. Tanden has finished up her marathon day with an appearance on CNN; she sparred with Scott Jennings about Donald Trump’s flagging poll numbers. A few hours later, with the sun hidden underneath the ocean, I whipped out my phone and discovered Tanden, up late and on Twitter, in the midst of yet another public spat—this time with the journalist and onetime CAP contributor Wajahat Ali. Ali asserted that Tanden had tanked the Democratic Party’s public image; that she had sold out to the PACs, the corporations, and the Israel lobby. “Hopefully the party you constantly shit on will win despite you in 41 days,” Tanden retorted. The residual optimism that briefly consumed me slowly deflated. We were right back where we started. It is still the summer of 2016. At this point, it probably will be forever. For those who haven’t owned an e-reader,  E Ink displays are great at what they’re designed for. They can retain what's on the screen without continuously consuming energy by using electrical pulses to move charged pigment particles within individual pixels, enabling them to store the screen state physically. That makes e-paper devices very power efficient, but it comes with a huge trade off: Changing those pixels is a glacially slow process compared with the modern displays we're all accustomed to. The screen that Bo used to make his InkBoy, for example, has a refresh speed of around 300 milliseconds, which is actually on the faster end of e-paper displays - but only really practical for displaying static images. For those times when a full-fat editor is overkill Don't need a full-fat productivity suite and worried about where your documents are being stored? Welcome to TinyLittleOffice, a set of applications where the document is the link itself. Using a URL to stash information isn't new. Look at many URLs, and you'll see a lengthy sequence of seemingly random characters at the end. Using that space to stash data isn't novel, but using it for documents is an interesting approach. TinyLittleOffice comprises a selection of tools, including a basic word processor, presentation builder, spreadsheet, calendar, and a drawing tool. The theory is a simple one: create a document and get a link. The link is the document. The document isn't saved on a server or made into an attachment (although it is possible to export to a variety of formats), so it is, in theory, private. That is, unless you're worried about what the server is doing when it opens the link. Using the URL for documents is far from a completely new idea. Paul Ford's (the author) "co-conspirator," Rich Ziade, came up with the idea of TinySheet six years ago: a simple spreadsheet that ran in the browser and kept its data in the URL. "We moved on," wrote Ford, "and sadly, those sites fell off the web, and the old links all broke." Years later, the concept was resurrected and expanded to include other productivity tools. The server code is on GitHub, which should, in theory, at least ease concerns about the new links rotting away. We tested the project, and it is indeed very impressive from a technical standpoint. It can easily create short documents and spin up spreadsheets. Sure, it won't make LibreOffice lose any sleep, but it's a fun tool, and sending increasingly lengthy URLs (there is a URL shortener on tap, though they do expire) is a lightweight alternative to a document attachment. And there's the rub. Why not simply use a tool that creates a document? As Ford acknowledges, "There are lots of tradeoffs. This is not, in any way, how web apps are expected to work. The links can get unwieldy pasted in chat, and you can't upload video or images, full stop. It's not collaborative like Google Docs. It's collaborative like handing out zines at a street fair." However, to carp about what TinyLittleOffice doesn't do is to miss the point. It is lightweight by design – it has to be in order to cram document data into the link. PARTNER CONTENT: A million reasons why advanced computing matters for genomics When a million veterans shared their genetic data by enrolling in the Department of Veterans Affairs' “Million Veteran Program,” they expected nothing back. This did not surprise Ravi Madduri, a senior scientist with the Data Science and Learning Division at the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE) Argonne National Laboratory. He is abundantly clear about the terms under which these veterans donated their data: “Even after giving everything to the country, they found it in their hearts to give more without expecting anything in return.” That gift, which constituted the nation’s largest biorepository and most ancestrally varied cohorts of veteran data in the world, created a debt that Madduri, who also holds a joint appointment in the Department of Human Genetics at the University of Chicago, is working to repay. “What I feel when I look at that data is that I have an obligation to deliver to the veterans on their intentions in doing this,” he says. Those intentions, as he reads it, are that others, veteran or not, can benefit from whatever the data can reveal. After arriving at Argonne as a graduate student in 2001, Madduri has spent the last two decades at the intersection of supercomputing and biomedicine, much of the time building the computing that turns the veterans’ data into results impacting areas from post-traumatic stress disorder to heart disease to cancer. He explains that machines have largely held up their end, but the obstacles between precious data and delivered results are not always solved with faster machines. Understanding the challenges is the path toward better science. Any two human genomes differ at millions of specific positions. A genome-wide association study asks a seemingly simple question across an enormous volume of data. For example, does a given genetic variant travel together with a particular medical condition? On its own, the question is simple. Multiplied across every variant worth testing and every condition recorded in a patient's chart, it becomes an enormous math problem, which is what supercomputers exist to solve. The harder question is whether the code is written to let them. Madduri's team has run this problem on the Summit system at DOE's Oak Ridge Leadership Computing Facility and on the Aurora system at the Argonne Leadership Computing Facility. On Summit, the team found that the job would not finish on the machine's CPUs. As the cohort grew, the matrix the analysis needed for each group no longer fit in memory, and the job stalled. “We decided to open the box and look at the code,” Madduri said. They found that the issue was not merely a speed problem, but a coding one. The analysis had been written for ordinary processors because that is what the genetics community was running on. Underneath the algorithms, though, the code spent nearly all of its time doing one thing: multiplying very large grids of numbers. This is exactly the operation that graphics processors exist to perform, and what makes them the engine of modern artificial intelligence training. As Madduri notes, the same computational architecture accelerating AI discovery “is also foundational for population genomics.” So, the team, led on this effort by Argonne's Alex Rodriguez, rewrote that part of the code for GPUs, and an analysis that would have taken roughly 481,000 CPU-core hours in the original version became tractable. This shift did more than speed up a slow job. It made a virtually impossible job possible. The workhorse they accelerated is a widely used genetics package called SAIGE, which made the rewritten version SAIGE-GPU, described in a separate paper in Bioinformatics. "We are not good at coming up with names. Sorry," Madduri says sheepishly. Before SAIGE-GPU, nearly every genome-wide association study started by picking a disease then looking for the variants linked to it. Madduri is blunt about the cost of that approach. “One of the challenges I found in biomedical research is that it is very incremental,” he explains. “Every year, they get new datasets, they analyze the data, they find a new set of genetic variants, and this happens across all of the different diseases.” The reason, he adds, is limited access to large-scale computing. His team chose not to be incremental and decided to solve the computational challenge once and for all. “One of the challenges we set for ourselves was to just take all the data that we have available, all the genetic data for all the veterans that have been sequenced as part of the Million Veteran Program,” Madduri says. The team, the Million Veteran Program consortium, published the results in Science, with Madduri's group among the authors, and published the computational work separately in Bioinformatics. The study reported on 2,068 traits across 635,969 veterans, and the results flagged 13,672 regions of the genome linked to one or more conditions. About 1,600 of these regions surfaced only because nearly a third of the participants were not of European ancestry. According to the Science paper, most genetic studies to this point have been drawn overwhelmingly from people of European descent. These 1,600 associations were previously “invisible” in the populations studied. That ancestral range also shaped how the analysis had to be run. The groups could not simply be pooled. That is, if a condition is more common in one ancestral group than another, the analysis has no way to tell a genuine disease marker from a marker of simply belonging to that group. Each group had to be run separately, and the results combined afterward. The 1,600 associations exist in no single group's results. They appear only when the separate analyses are combined. Madduri is careful about what the whole exercise means and what it does not. “This is all preclinical,” he says. “This is the discovery phase.” The analysis produces a statistical map of where to look. Turning any one of those 13,672 regions into a treatment takes years of downstream work by researchers who pick up and follow the data, which is why Madduri says the team never stopped. “Things that took 14 days when we wrote the Science paper now take less than a couple of hours,” he says. While this means getting an answer sooner, the real win is being able to ask the same question of a bigger population “with a larger cohort size and more statistical rigor than the paper that we published a couple of years ago.” The veterans’ genomes have an unusual advantage over other medical data. One agency gathered the data under one agreement, which is what made bringing them to Summit possible. In medicine, Madduri says, data “cannot be shared with one another as freely as you would probably share cosmology data.” The alternative is to move the model, he says: “Federated learning allows you to train models without moving data around.” This means each institution trains on the copy it already holds, behind its own walls, and only sends back the adjustments the model learned. Those adjustments are combined into a single updated model, which goes out to everyone, and the cycle repeats. Asked how several institutions are persuaded to compute together without pooling anything, he corrects the premise. “It is not the institution,” he says. “The first layer of that puzzle is people wanting to work together, to collaborate.” Then, the part that makes it work: “It is a people federation.” Madduri was a co-developer of APPFL, the Argonne Privacy-Preserving Federated Learning framework, which provides a means to help “move” models rather than data. The machinery underneath is closer to access control than to machine learning. "You do not have to write code for each scheduler," he says. Globus Compute presents one interface across the facilities' schedulers, and APPFL submits its training jobs through it. Even with these technical challenges, the harder problem is permission. “All the computing that happens is authorized computing that is tied to a project allocation,” Madduri says. Federated training means that each collaborator’s job has to run under their own institutional credentials on a machine they are personally entitled to use. A site with data nobody else has makes that shared model better for everyone. "If one site has data that are unique to it and other sites do not have that, then the model becomes more robust," Madduri adds. Madduri has been a regular attendee at the SC Conference since SC01 in Denver, which was themed “Beyond Boundaries,” and focused on moving data across them. “I was a grad student, and I had a demo in the DOE booth demonstrating my master’s thesis, which at that time was to use the Internet to move large amounts of data reliably from one point to the other,” he says. That capability was called reliable file transfer, and, in his words, it “has now transformed into what is called ‘Globus’.” The first tool he helped build moved research data around. The latest one lets the data stay in place. Since SC01, Madduri has been to the conference roughly 20 times and is specific about what he takes home. “I learn parallel processing, how people are scaling AI training, how people are dealing with parallelization, how people are dealing with resource utilization monitoring,” he notes. His group attends the Computational Approaches for Cancer Workshop every year. Lately, the interest has moved to AI agents. “The agentic approaches are the next frontier for us,” he declares. Madduri is also aware that he walks in unusual research domains. Before biomedicine, he worked with high-energy physicists, earthquake engineers, and cosmologists. “When I go to SC, I live in many different worlds,” he says. “I am part of a very small group of people that go to a [combined] human genetics conference and a supercomputing conference.” For Madduri, his debt is not settled. More than a decade after the first veterans enrolled, the Million Veteran Program data have produced a map but not yet a cure. What stands between those two things is the work of getting separate institutions to compute as one. To date, that still is being done largely by hand. “For the earlier experiments, it was us talking to NERSC, us talking to Argonne, us talking to Oak Ridge,” Madduri says. “It is a lot of human in the loop.” He is hoping the American Science Cloud being built under the DOE’s Genesis Mission will take that over: “It would be automated, run using APIs.” For now, Madduri remains focused on turning data into triumph, using a very special cohort of data to reveal the next breakthrough against cancer or spare someone a lifetime of anxiety. How much more can nuclear power plants give?

In this age of rising electricity demand, people are trying to squeeze every last bit of power out of every power plant possible. Over the past few weeks, there’s been big news about nuclear power plants trying to do just that. Constellation, the largest nuclear operator in the US, announced plans to add nearly a gigawatt of new capacity across 11 existing nuclear reactors at six plants. And on October 5, the Department of Energy awarded a $4.2 billion loan to Vistra for previously announced upgrades at three plants. Pushing existing reactors to produce more could help provide more clean power for data centers, quickly. But just how much additional power can we count on? Upgrading equipment at existing nuclear power plants can allow them to reach a higher capacity; the process is called a power uprate. Operators have been doing this since the 1970s in the US, but it’s been less common in recent years as demand for electricity plateaued and cheap natural gas put financial pressure on nuclear power plants. Some plants closed, and putting in more money to produce more electricity didn’t make much sense at others. Between 2001 and 2010, the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission approved over 80 uprates. Between 2011 and 2020, the number was 33. But with the rise of AI and the related boom in electricity demand, there’s been a shift. We’re seeing shuttered plants like Palisades and Three Mile Island race to reopen. And existing plants are looking to uprate again. Some uprates are minor, involving changes to instrument setpoints or calculations at the plant. But more major overhauls, which can boost reactor power by as much as 20%, require replacing plant equipment. Generally, uprates involve using fuel that’s more highly enriched, or a higher volume of fresh fuel, in the nuclear reactor. That helps produce more steam, which is used to turn a turbine and generate electricity. To handle that additional energy, the main turbines and equipment such as pumps, motors, generators, and transformers are upgraded. The most recent major uprate approved in the US was for three reactors at the Browns Ferry nuclear power plant in Alabama. A $475 million project saw that plant expand its power capacity by 14% between 2018 and 2019. Things could pick up soon, though. Constellation announced plans this week to spend more than $4.3 billion on uprates to 11 reactors. The additional electricity will be enough to power roughly 700,000 US homes. Behind it all? Big Tech money. Google agreed to buy electricity from these plants, and the 20-year agreement was a major factor in making the uprates happen. As Joe Dominguez, Constellation’s CEO, told the New York Times: “These are capital intensive, labor intensive projects that require a different price point than the market trades at, frankly.” Also in the last few weeks, the US Department of Energy shared that it will commit over $4 billion to help finance upgrades and modernization for Vistra nuclear power plants across Pennsylvania and Ohio. Those projects will add 433 megawatts of power capacity, and Meta has already signed on to buy electricity from the plants in a long-term deal. A note about cost: On a per-kilowatt basis, that Constellation project is less expensive than building a new reactor, but it’s still more expensive than building a new utility-scale solar farm with energy storage or a combined-cycle natural-gas plant. Even at this high cost, uprates are in high demand. Today, the US nuclear fleet has roughly 98 gigawatts of total power capacity. Depending on who you ask, there’s somewhere between three and eight additional gigawatts on the table from uprates. A pretty significant chunk of the potential uprates in the US are already accounted for—the NRC already expects applications to add 2.4 gigawatts of power to reactors through 2032. It’s crucial to put these plans (and the total potential for uprates in the US) in perspective, though. PJM, the grid operator that covers much of the East Coast, expects about 30 gigawatts of additional peak demand because of AI by 2030. All the possible nationwide nuclear uprates would cover just a fraction of that. Making the most of existing nuclear reactors is a relatively easy win at a time when we need all the zero-emissions power we can get. But there’s still a lot of demand, and if we want more nuclear power to meet it, eventually that will require actually building new reactors. Pig kidneys that were cooled to -150 °C and stored in a freezer for months have been successfully rewarmed and shown to work again for the first time, say scientists. They hope to replicate the findings with human organs. The kidneys—which are similar in size to human ones—were left in a glass-like state, in one case for over a year. Once they had been rewarmed, tested in machines, and transplanted into pigs, the kidneys regained their color and made urine—key signs they were working. “Ten years ago … I wouldn’t say I had high hopes it was actually going to work,” says Erik Finger, a transplant surgeon at the University of Minnesota who co-led the work. “After we did the last transplant, I walked out of the room and said, ‘This is a big deal.’” The research was shared at the preprint server bioRxiv and has not yet been peer-reviewed. The kidneys aren’t perfect, and the researchers have yet to demonstrate that they can sustain life over the long term. But the results represent a milestone achievement, say others in the field. “Thank God that someone’s actually managed to do it in one organ and prove that it works,” says Peter Kilbride, a cryobiologist and cofounder of Kyros Kairos, a company focused on improving funding for cryopreservation research, who was not involved in the research. “For years, people have been trying and failing to get to this step. Finally, we’ve got this breakthrough.” “Their work is amazing and will enable us to truly, indefinitely bank organs,” says Korkut Uygun, a biomedical engineer and professor of surgery at Harvard Medical School, who was also not involved in the research. “It is a revolutionary time for transplantation.” Every year, tens of thousands of organ transplants take place in the US alone. These procedures are thought to have saved 2 million years of life in the last 25 years. But there’s plenty of room for improvement. One major challenge is the lack of available organs. Even when an organ becomes available, it can be difficult to get it to the right recipient within 24 to 36 hours, which is the current limit for preserving human organs. People who receive organ transplants must also take drugs that suppress their immune systems for the rest of their lives. These drugs are meant to stop their bodies from rejecting the new organs. But they come with side effects and increase the risk of infections and cancer. The ability to bank organs for long periods of time holds the promise to address all these challenges. Doctors would have more time to match organs to recipients. They’d be able to limit the risk of organ rejection, too—potentially by preparing recipients, and their immune systems, beforehand. “With real organ banking … instead of the patients waiting for the organ, it’s the organs that are waiting for the patients,” says Finger. Scientists have been able to cool cells to ultra-low temperatures through a process called vitrification, which essentially turns them to something like glass. This procedure is already used to store eggs, sperm, and embryos. Vitrifying whole organs is much harder. Some problems can be avoided with cryoprotectants—chemicals that work like antifreeze and prevent damaging ice crystals from forming. But these chemicals tend to be incredibly toxic. It’s also difficult to rapidly and uniformly cool large tissues, and just as hard to evenly rewarm an organ all the way through. Working with John Bischof, a bioengineer at the University of Minnesota, Finger and colleagues have come up with a solution. Once an organ has been loaded with a cryoprotectant, they infuse it with iron oxide nanoparticles. These nanoparticles don’t do anything to the cells. But when it’s time to rewarm the organ, they can be activated with an electromagnetic field. When the radiofrequency of that field switches between two states, the particles also flip, realigning themselves with the field. That causes them to release heat throughout the organ. Adjusting the power of the field allows the team to control the rate of heating so that it gets faster once the organ reaches -120°C. The researchers successfully vitrified rat kidneys using this approach in work published in 2023. Back then, they were able to rewarm kidneys that had been kept in a freezer for up to 100 days before transplanting them into rats. Since then, the team has been trying to achieve the same success with bigger organs. Pig kidneys are thought to be a good model for human ones—they’re about the same size and look similar. Rat kidneys weigh around one gram; pig kidneys are around 100. It hasn’t been easy. The cryoprotectant used for vitrifying rat kidneys didn’t work as well in the pig organs. “We had to rediscover and modify the cocktail,” says Bischof. “That’s why it’s taken us three years.” The first step in the procedure is to remove a kidney from a donor pig. Once it’s out, the kidney is flushed with a commonly used organ preservation solution to remove the blood, bagged, and temporarily laid on a bed of ice in a cooler so it can be taken over to the engineering lab. There, team members unpack the kidney and hook it up to a device they’ve designed to control the organ’s temperature and pressure. Then they start to load it up with the cocktail of cryoprotectants, slowly enough to ensure an even spread through the organ but quickly enough to minimize any toxic effects. It takes around two hours. The next step is to infuse the organ with the iron oxide nanoparticles, which are pumped through the organ for around seven to eight minutes, says Finger. Then it’s time to cool the organ by pumping cold nitrogen vapor into a chamber housing it. It’s a delicate process, and the team must ensure that the organ is cooling all the way through. At the same time, they must lower the kidney’s temperature as quickly as possible to prevent ice from forming. “Oftentimes there are multiple people doing different things to make sure that things don’t go awry,” says Bischof. Once the organ reaches the vitrified state at around -120 °C, it “behaves like a glass,” says Finger. “At that point, you can stay stable for long periods of time—we think indefinitely.” The team then pauses the cooling process to ensure that the organ is glassy throughout. Otherwise, it could crack. After the pause, if everything goes well, the kidney is ultimately cooled to -150 °C. Once it’s in the freezer, it can stay there for days, weeks, or even months. The team has kept a small number of kidneys banked for over a year. The real test of these kidneys comes when they’re taken out of the freezer, rewarmed, and flushed of their chemicals and nanoparticles. Can they support life? So far, the team has tested some of them in a couple of different ways. In one set of experiments, the researchers put rewarmed kidneys on perfusion devices—systems that are already used in transplant surgeries both to provide organs with nutrients and to help doctors assess them while they’re out of the body. In another set of experiments, Finger and his colleagues transplanted rewarmed kidneys into pigs and monitored them for two hours. In February 2007, the hulking dual-­rotor casualty evacuation helicopter had just flown three wounded soldiers to a field hospital in Balad, north of Baghdad, when another call came: an emergency mission to fly through an insurgent-controlled area in broad daylight. Typically, helicopters would be in a spot like this only at night, and with all lights turned off. As it passed near the city of Karmah, in the eastern side of Al-Anbar province, an insurgent fired a portable missile launcher. The tail gunner, who likely saw the shot roaring in, yelled for the pilot to make an evasive move. But the missile had already locked on, and the ensuing blast started a fire on board. After the pilot battled the failing machine for 58 agonizing seconds, Morphine 1-2 plunged to the ground. All seven crew members were killed. Why was the helo there in daylight? “It was getting blood and bringing it back. It was [on] what we call a ‘blood run,’ and [it’s] the highest of priorities besides casualty evacuation,” explains Lieutenant Commander Robert Murray, a Navy officer and program manager at DARPA, the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency, who studied this incident as an intelligence officer. Today, a chronic shortage of blood in both the military and civilian worlds is fueling a race to develop synthetic alternatives. About 90% of preventable battlefield deaths are due to hemorrhage—bleeding that happens when a blood vessel ruptures—and it’s the leading cause of maternal deaths worldwide. A study published in Transfusion in 2025 found that of 1.2 million trauma patients in the United States who potentially needed blood before reaching a hospital, just 1% received it. The shortage results in about 10,000 preventable deaths each year in the US, according to the American College of Surgeons. Globally, that figure is likely in the millions. One reason for the shortfall is that blood has a storage problem. Whole blood lasts only 35 days when refrigerated, so banks separate out components to stretch the timeline. But that’s an imperfect solution. The platelets that help wounds clot are typically stored at room temperature and last for a week or less. Hospitals and the military freeze the red blood cells that carry oxygen, as well as the protein-rich plasma that restores volume and aids coagulation. Once thawed, though, both will spoil within days. “What happens if I don’t know when I’m going to be attacked? Well, thaw it and you let it expire, right?” Murray says. A JAMA study of combat injuries in Afghanistan suggests a magic number for someone who needs a transfusion: You have 36 minutes to get started. That’s why Murray—who, in addition to his military experience, has a PhD in physiology—is leading a DARPA effort to address blood’s battlefield problems. He’s in charge of a new $73 million dollar program called RAPIID (for “resuscitation and prevention of ischemia-induced dysfunction”). Its goal is to create shelf-stable versions of the three major transfusion constituents: plasma, platelets, and red blood cells. As it gets underway late this year, RAPIID picks up on decades of work to synthesize blood or help it last longer. Most recently, that includes a prior $47.5 million DARPA project called FSHARP (for “fieldable solutions for hemorrhage with bio-artificial resuscitation products”), in which researchers have demonstrated promising results in animals. With RAPIID trials for the different components occurring in parallel, the goal is to have bags of dried blood products deployed in the field by 2029. That puts Murray in the position of a conductor coordinating an orchestra of clinical trials with the FDA, while also sourcing the paraphernalia that goes along with transfusions—everything from combat-durable storage bags to tests that could check how much artificial blood a patient already has. Getting it all done will be tough, but the military is feeling the pressure to address its shortage. Blood drives may not deliver in time after a mass-casualty event, and a JAMA study of combat injuries in Afghanistan suggests that if the injured person needs a transfusion, the magic number is 36 minutes. “If something is survivable—and generally we classify most all hemorrhage as survivable—[you’re] almost guaranteed to survive if you get the blood before that 36-minute mark,” Murray says. After that, the chance of survival “starts to drop precipitously.” In the mission to make portable, shelf-stable blood components, plasma is the low-hanging fruit. Researchers have known how to isolate and freeze-dry plasma, the yellowish liquid that makes up 55% of whole human blood, since the late 1930s. On the battlefields of World War II, its mix of water, salts, and thousands of types of proteins helped restore blood volume in wounded soldiers—and to some degree controlled bleeding. In the 1960s, the US abandoned freeze-dried plasma because it carried a risk of transmitting hepatitis to recipients from infected donors. But screening, testing, and drying techniques improved, and starting in 2010, the US military launched a yearslong effort to rebuild its capacity for producing these products. Over the past two years, the FDA has allowed two types of freeze-dried plasma to be used in emergencies by the US military—and one can even be used by civilians. As for platelets, efforts at extending their life date back to the 1950s, but they haven’t seen a ton of success. Bioengineers have, for example, attempted to attach the membranes of platelets to synthetic particles. The most successful project comes from a company called Cellphire, which freeze-dries actual human platelets. The product has shown safety and efficacy in humans in clinical trials, but its dependence on donors means it’s unclear whether this approach can scale. Red blood cells have been similarly tough to preserve or mimic. Their most critical component is hemoglobin, which carries oxygen and comes encased in a cell membrane. For more than a century, researchers have been looking for ways to deliver hemoglobin without the immunological challenges created by this exterior, including the fact that donors’ and recipients’ blood types need to be compatible. Evolutionary pressure developed that wrapper for very good reason, though: “Naked” hemoglobin tends to break down quickly in the bloodstream, and that can cause organ damage. It also scavenges nitric oxide, our body’s natural mechanism for opening up blood vessels, and causes those channels to constrict. This problem became all too clear in the 1980s and 1990s, when fears that transfusions could transmit HIV sparked interest in a class of membrane-free delivery mechanisms called hemoglobin-based oxygen carriers (HBOCs). One, named HemAssist, consisted of hemoglobin subunits linked together to avoid the rapid-breakdown problem. It had the added benefit of lasting roughly a year in cold storage (red blood cells last about 40 days)—but it didn’t solve the nitric oxide problem, so it constricted blood vessels. In a clinical trial, 24 of the 52 people who received HemAssist died, compared with eight out of 46 in the control group. The parent company terminated trials in 1998. A review of clinical studies published in JAMA in 2008 further solidified HBOCs’ fate, finding that they generally triple patients’ chances of heart attack and increase the risk of death by 30%. More recently, researchers have tried to grow red blood cells in a lab using a technology called “blood pharming,” which involves making them from progenitors known as induced pluripotent stem cells. The stumbling blocks? Cost and yield. DARPA launched a pharming initiative in 2008, using stem cells from discarded umbilical-cord blood, but was unable to get the cost below tens of thousands of dollars per unit of red blood cells. They pulled the plug and offloaded the research to the Uniformed Services University. Even if the cost were lower, though, pharmed blood can’t be stored in a backpack. “These are still cells—they still require a refrigerator, they still require special handling,” Murray says. It won’t be easy to solve all these challenges at once. “Each one of those projects individually is a massive undertaking,” says John Holcomb, a surgery professor at the University of Alabama at Birmingham, who was a combat surgeon in Mogadishu, Somalia, during the battle that inspired Black Hawk Down. “I think that they will probably be successful individually. How we use those individual products, or combine those products together, I think remains to be seen.” Several years after DARPA gave up on lab-grown blood, Murray was stationed at a base on Okinawa, an island 400 miles southwest of the Japanese mainland, where he witnessed the vulnerability of the blood supply firsthand. The site houses a blood bank that serves the roughly 27,000 Marines commanded from the island—plus thousands more military personnel on the base and deployed within a 100-million-square-mile stretch of the Pacific called USPACOM, which ranges from western India to Hawaii and from Antarctica to the Arctic. “The blood center is right next to the hospital, which is about 200 meters from all of the satellite communications on that island,” Murray told me. A missile strike could take out all three. Murray wondered: Is there a way to produce synthetic blood that could last longer? As he completed his PhD in physiology in 2018, the military was also awakening to the vulnerability of the Okinawa facility. The 2018 National Defense Strategy, which shifted military focus from terrorism to a potential ground war with China or Russia, showed war planners the risks of relying on a single site for blood runs covering all of USPACOM. DARPA then got serious once again about making fake blood and kicked off the FSHARP program in 2023. Murray came aboard as a deputy program manager in DARPA’s Biological Technologies Office in 2024 and was named a program manager in 2026, just a few months before RAPIID put out its call for proposals this past May. Before he took the helm, related work had been underway for years. Aided by FSHARP funding, researchers at a company called KaloCyte continued testing a technique—originally developed by doctors who are now at the University of Maryland, Penn State, and the University of Pittsburgh—that “upcycles” hemoglobin from expiring donor blood. “We take that hemoglobin and then we package it in a lipid nanoparticle that functions like an artificial cell membrane,” explains CEO Elaine Haynes. The product, called ErythroMer, is essentially a new kind of HBOC. The membrane prevents breakdown, limits dangerous scavenging of nitric oxide, and also helps the hemoglobin take up and release oxygen. Each ErythroMer cell includes a lipid called KC-1003, which acts like a sentry: In response to high pH in the lungs, it signals another small molecule within ErythroMer to move away, allowing oxygen in. Then, in tired tissue, where pH is lower, KC-1003 brings the small molecule back, releasing the oxygen. Using a well-understood process called lyophilization, similar to one that World War II–era scientists used with plasma, the team freeze-dries its product to store at room temperature, where it can remain stable for two years. In a 2016 study of mice and rats with up to 70% of their blood removed, ErythroMer stabilized blood pressure and oxygen delivery—without the devastating constriction of blood vessels that sank HemAssist. FSHARP furthered that work, helping fund studies on how the HBOC interacted with plasma and platelets. Much like the handle of a Swiss Army knife, the synthetic platelet has specialized attachments—peptides that serve as the biological equivalents of a corkscrew or a nail file. KaloCyte, though, still has plenty to figure out. Haynes says the company can source only about 1,000 to 2,000 units of expiring hemoglobin a month. So it has licensed a yeast strain that it aims to use to create its own hemoglobin in the lab. Over a video call from her desk, Haynes showed me a sample of ready-to-use ErythroMer. It was encased in a heavy-duty storage bag with two chambers. One was full of scarlet powder—the hemoglobin product—and the other of sterile water to rapidly rehydrate it in the field. Eventually, she explained, under Murray’s master plan, “you’ll have another container with the synthetic platelet, and then a third container with the plasma.” But achieving that vision depends on finding a synthetic platelet that works. To do that, DARPA has turned to Anirban Sen Gupta, a bioengineering professor at Case Western Reserve University. “Traditionally platelets were thought of [as] blood cement, but it’s not that simple,” he explains. “They are, in my opinion, like drones, surveilling the blood vessel wall continuously, looking for signs of injury.” Sen Gupta’s team, which also includes collaborators at the University of Pittsburgh, used FSHARP funding to continue work on a product called SynthoPlate. Fabricated from a lipid nanoparticle that he likens to the handle of a Swiss Army knife, it has specialized peptide attachments—the biological equivalents of a corkscrew or a nail file. The first version has three: One binds to a chemical called von Willebrand factor, which helps platelets stick to the walls of injured blood vessels; another attaches to collagen, which acts as a scaffold for regrowing cells; and the third mimics a blood protein called fibrinogen, which acts like a Lego connector that allows the synthetic platelets to stick to real ones near a wound. Like ErythroMer, the product is freeze-dried and can be reconstituted with sterile water. It keeps for over a year at room temperature and at least three months at a desertlike 122 °F. In a 2026 paper in Advanced Science, the researchers showed that SynthoPlate cut bleeding time and blood loss by more than half in mice with low platelet counts. In a separate study, they also safely injected the faux platelets into rats’ bone marrow—a more useful delivery site, given that a bleeding patient might not have an available vein. Sen Gupta and a colleague have spun this work out into a company called Haima Therapeutics. The good news, as he sees it, is that the pharmaceutical industry excels at mass-­producing millions of billions of lipid nanoparticles—witness Operation Warp Speed, in which these blobs of fat held delicate snippets of mRNA in covid shots. “The magic of the artificial platelet is in all those peptides that are decorating the surface of the particle, but the scalability of the platform is very well established,” he says. Around the world, prospective blood alternatives abound. Researchers at Cambridge University in the UK are trying to pharm red blood cells for people with rare blood types or blood disorders like sickle cell anemia, and other researchers have also cultured platelets and red blood cells. Japanese scientists are already testing their own version of encapsulated human hemoglobin in human subjects. Over the course of the next three years, RAPIID will move DARPA’s efforts closer to real life. In addition to ErythroMer and SynthoPlate, the agency in early October announced funding for five other companies working on blood components. Among them is a firm called Abioremedi that eschews hemoglobin for synthetic versions of other oxygen-carrying proteins such as cytoglobin, neuroglobin, and myoglobin encased in albumin—the most abundant protein in human plasma. Octapharma USA won the sole contract to supply plasma to RAPIID and will also be competing in platelets with an FDA-approved pair of clotting proteins it affixes to a disc-shaped nanoparticle. With those players in place, the agency will focus for the next year on getting technologies through large FDA-regulated animal studies. Murray has set up a bit of a Hunger Games scenario: Each contractee working on blood components has only one year of guaranteed funding. He says he’s taking advantage of advancements in the field since FSHARP launched but is also hedging against any manufacturing or regulatory setbacks ahead of his 2029 deadline. Getting cut doesn’t mean the product is bad, just that the time frame was too demanding. The second and third years will focus on human trials, overseen by the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus. That will include using the products in situations where the risk of side effects is outweighed by the danger of doing nothing, such as with severely injured people in ambulances. The possibility of fatal outcomes has invited second-guessing about DARPA’s choices. For Shibani Pati, who directs the Center for Research in Transfusion Medicine and Cell Therapies at the University of California, San Francisco, Murray could be making things too “DARPA hard” on himself by backing products like SynthoPlate and ErythroMer, which are previously untested in humans. “They need to make a very concerted effort to take what they’ve already developed and push it through to approval through the FDA,” she says. “Not start with moonshot projects that will take forever to get through.” For platelets, Pati thinks the RAPIID program would have done better to tap Cellphire, since its products have already received millions from the Pentagon and shown safety and efficacy in humans. A different HBOC that sources hemoglobin from cattle, called Hemopure, has been approved for use since 2001 in South Africa, she notes. Holcomb, the surgery professor, worries the effort will run into issues with interactions between the products. He notes that individual blood components have more than one role: Red blood cells also help with clotting, platelets also are anti-inflammatory, and plasma helps tune coagulation. Murray says that FSHARP’s animal studies tested all the products together, using whole blood as a baseline, precisely because of such possible interactions.
