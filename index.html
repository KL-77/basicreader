"At Home in Space" opens, caps Air and Space Museum renovation

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-02

After seven years of work and nearly $1 billion, the Smithsonian has completed the renovation of the National Air and Space Museum’s flagship building in Washington, DC. On October 30, the museum debuted its last-to-open spaceflight exhibition, returning “home” with a gallery devoted to establishing a spacefaring civilization. “Welcome to ‘At Home in Space’!” said curator Jennifer Levasseur, who guided us through a virtual tour of the gallery. “This is the story of long-duration spaceflight in the last few decades. We generally call it ‘reaching orbit.’” “It’s the prep story, the space shuttle story. It’s what allows us to end up at a place where we have 25-plus years of long-duration spaceflight experience, the capacity for humans to be living in space on the International Space Station (ISS), and then it carries that story forward. So the middle of our story then becomes the ISS, and the end of the story are the next steps,” said Levasseur. Over the past several years, the National Air and Space Museum has introduced new galleries showcasing how US astronauts first entered space and landed on the Moon (“Destination Moon”); how robotic probes have explored every planet in our solar system (“Kenneth C. Griffin Exploring the Planets”); how technologies and infrastructure impact our daily lives (“RTX Living in the Space Age Hall”) and what approaches spaceflight will take in the years to come (“Futures in Space”). “At Home in Space,” which follows the similarly themed “Moving Beyond Earth,” immerses guests in the equipment, spacecraft, and orbital platforms that have enabled humanity from simply visiting space to living there, setting up the near future for bases on the Moon and Mars. “We have a great way to attract people into the gallery, which is this overhead mock-up of an astronaut on the end of the robotic arm, and then the backdrop to that is this photo mural—a full-size photo print—of the ISS,” said Levasseur. “Our astronaut up here is intended to be [an actual] person, but I’m not going to share who that is. They are wearing an Expedition 60 patch, so that’ll give you a little clue.” Some of the gallery’s artifacts include a space shuttle main engine, Robonaut 1A (a prototype humanoid robot), and an extravehicular mobility unit (EMU) spacesuit. “It’s pretty damn special,” said Levasseur of the EMU. “This, as far as I know, is the first and potentially only public display of a fully flown set of EMU equipment in the world. I know NASA has one in its EVA office, but it is not on public display.” Unlike Apollo-era spacesuits sized to each astronaut, the EMU was designed to be reused, so its components can be swapped out to fit astronauts of different sizes. Introduced during the early years of the space shuttle program, the same EMU suits are still used on the International Space Station for US spacewalks. The museum’s example is only missing a functioning portable life support system (PLSS), as all the backpacks are still needed for use. So the suit on display only has a PLSS shell for now, according to Levasseur. The gallery also features Guy Bluford’s and Sally Ride’s in-flight suits, the “shirt-sleeve” outfits the first African American and first US woman wore on the space shuttle (on separate missions). “Sally is notably missing her jacket, which was a late change to our plan. We were given a drawing of this, a 3D mockup of its display case, and our designers then put it in this way without the jacket on. And we thought, ‘Hey, that’s actually kind of a nice contrast. Let’s keep the jacket out,’” Levasseur told collectSPACE. The gallery also includes recovered components from the Challenger and Columbia tragedies. It is only the second public display of fallen orbiter debris after a Kennedy Space Center Visitor Complex exhibit that opened in Florida in 2015. “We wanted to honor the individuals who were lost during the shuttle program,” said Levasseur. “So we have an elevon hinge from Challenger that washed up on the beach a few years ago and a little wiring panel fragment from inside the payload bay. And then from Columbia we thought it was appropriate to help tell that story with a piece of RCC [reinforced carbon-carbon]. This is a T-panel from the starboard wing. We also have a CD [compact disc] on a long-term loan from David Brown’s family.” Visitors can also walk through a full-size mock-up of the space station’s Destiny module, the US national laboratory on the station, and view its equipment on both sides, as well as above and below. “Originally, the detailing on the floor was going to be a mosaic of real images, but we found that for accessibility reasons people might come in and see the cables and worry that they were real, creating a tripping hazard,” said Levasseur. “On the flip side, and I had to fight to get these, we had to have labels to indicate activity happens on the floor and happens on the overhead. People need to see this is a literally three-dimensional space.” The module has interactive elements on one side and showcases some of the crew equipment-related artifacts, including coffee cups engineered for use in microgravity. “This is probably the best section in my mind because it has fun stuff,” said Levasseur, pointing out the flown miniPCR biotech toolkit and MinION portable device used to sequence DNA in space for the first time. “My absolute favorite thing to work on in this gallery answers the questions: What does space smell like, and what does the ISS smell like? We have little scent oils inside each of these that allow people to smell them,” she said. Nearby, the  first 3D printer used in space is also on display. In 2014, Made In Space (now part of Redwire) and NASA flew the printer, which produced hundreds of objects, from astronaut tools to space station repair parts. The gallery concludes with a space reserved to exhibit the Artemis I capsule, the spacecraft that flew without astronauts to the Moon in 2022. A Lockheed Martin scale model of Orion holds the place until NASA delivers the real spacecraft to the Smithsonian, which is expected later this year. “This is the piece of Wright Flyer fabric that went to the moon with Artemis II, so that’s on display here, too,” Levasseur said, concluding the tour. The renovation of the National Air and Space Museum also included refacing the exterior cladding, replacing mechanical systems, and other improvements. The first half of the museum reopened in 2022 with eight new or reimagined galleries, the Northrop Grumman Planetarium, museum store, and the Mars Café. The second phase opened in 2025 with five new galleries, and five additional galleries opened July 1. Following “At Home in Space,” “Modern Military Aviation” will open on November 11. “We are thrilled to complete the most extensive project the museum has ever undergone,” Chris Browne, John and Adrienne Mars Director of the museum, said in a statement. “With the opening of these last two galleries, the public will once again be able to visit the entirety of the museum and learn from and engage with the nation’s world-class aerospace collection.” Admission to all of the National Air and Space Museum galleries remains free, but timed-entry passes for the building are required to visit.

It’s a regrettable reality that there is never enough time to cover all the interesting scientific stories we come across. So every month, we highlight a handful of the best stories that nearly slipped through the cracks. October’s list includes an epigenetic mechanism for why large dogs age faster than smaller ones; physicists who trapped a single particle of antimatter for months; and invisible tattoo inks that only show up under UV light. In many mammalian species, larger creatures tend to have longer lifespans. Mice, for instance, live an average of just two years, while some whales can last for as long as 200 years. Dogs, however, buck this pattern; larger dogs tend to age faster and die younger than smaller ones, which makes canines an ideal model for biologists to study to learn more about aging in general. Arizona State University scientists report that these lifespan differences seem to be due to so-called “jumping genes,” according to a paper published in the journal Science. The authors used data from 864 dogs collected in the Dog Aging Project to map patterns of DNA methylation (which influences how much certain genes are turned on or off)  across the entire genome. It’s part of the epigenome, whose markers can change over time in response to stress and other environmental factors. Genetic sequences, by contrast, are largely fixed. So DNA methylation is a good chemical indicator of aging and DNA regulation. Their analysis showed that aging in dogs is linked to the decrease in the regulation provided by DNA methylation, particularly in regions where there are “jumping genes” that can move from chromosome to chromosome. These jumps can damage DNA, causing cancer and other age-related diseases, and their activity increases with the loss of regulatory markers. The authors identified a specific class of jumping genes called LINE1s that were responsible for the different rates of aging in dogs. Giant dog breeds lost roughly 35 percent more LINE1 methylation each year compared to small breeds. “This is one of the clearest molecular signatures we’ve seen that aligns with the well-known size-lifespan trade-off in dogs,” said co-author Blaise Mariner. “It suggests that faster aging in larger dogs may be driven, at least in part, by reduced control over these jumping genes.” Science, 2026. DOI: 10.1126/science.aeb2986 (About DOIs). One of the biggest unanswered questions in cosmology is why the early universe had a fundamental asymmetry between matter and antimatter. When matter and antimatter particles collide, they annihilate into energy, so that asymmetry resulted in matter dominating our universe, which contains much more matter than antimatter. It’s extremely difficult to create and isolate antimatter, requiring special traps. Physicists at Northwestern University have succeeded in trapping a single positron (antimatter particle) for months on end, according to a paper published in Physical Review Letters. Co-author Gerald Gabrielse and his team built on earlier work, in which they trapped a single electron to create a one-electron quantum cyclotron. They then used the setup to make one of the most precise measurements of its magnetic moment yet. Trapping a positron was a bit trickier. They harvested the positron from sodium-22 nuclei, slowing it down enough to guide it into a near-perfect vacuum trap of electric and magnetic fields. Once inside, they chilled the positron to just 300 millikelvins (0.3° above absolute zero), thereby keeping the positron in its quantum ground state. They found they could maintain that state for months at a time. That enabled Gabrielse et al. to manipulate and detect the particle’s individual quantum energy state using tuned microwaves—the first critical step in being able to measure the positron’s magnetic properties, hopefully with the same precision they achieved in their earlier experiments with a single electron. The Standard Model of particle physics predicts that there should be no difference between the two particle measurements. If they are not equal, “that would be the biggest discovery in physics in the last century,” said Gabrielse. Physical Review Letters, 2026. DOI: 10.1103/gbkc-6nlj. A person in Washington state has died from bird flu after exposure at an infected dairy farm, state health officials reported Sunday. It marks the third human bird flu death in the US since a strain of highly pathogenic (H5) avian influenza (HPAI) made the unprecedented jump from wild, migratory birds to dairy cows, which was first reported in the US in March 2024. The person who died was a Yakima County resident and was said to have had underlying health conditions, which could have made the person more vulnerable to the infection. On Friday, county officials reported that the person was severely ill and hospitalized. In the subsequent announcement of the person’s death, officials said they would not be releasing further information about the person, including their name, gender, or age, out of respect for the family’s privacy. “Our hearts go out to the individual’s family and loved ones during this difficult time,” Dave Atteberry, health officer for the Yakima Health District, said in a statement. “Avian influenza can cause severe illness, particularly among people who have underlying health conditions. While this is a serious and heartbreaking outcome, the risk to the general public remains low.” In all, three people have tested positive for bird flu in Washington since the virus was reported in dairy cattle in the state on October 8. All three have been in Yakima County. While announcing the death on Sunday, officials also reported the third case. That case, like the first case, was in a person who had exposure to an infected dairy herd in the county. Both people have reportedly not experienced severe illness nor been hospitalized. Since the October 8 report of infected herds, Washington state has had 40 detections of bird flu in dairy cows, according to the US Department of Agriculture. Most of those have been in Yakima County, which has reported detections in 33 herds. This year, the USDA has reported 134 cases of bird flu in cattle, including cases in Texas, Washington, Idaho, Utah, and Kansas. The USDA lists no other reports of cases in other domestic livestock, including swine. But the virus has continued to spread in other wild and captive mammals. A report last month from ProPublica revealed that the virus is raging on a mink farm in Utah. The report raised alarm because mink is a species that could give the virus opportunities to mutate to better spread among humans. To date, no human-to-human transmission of HPAI has been reported from the over 70 human cases detected since 2024. Of the cases, only three have resulted in death. The first death was in a 65-year-old Louisiana resident with underlying health conditions, who became critically ill in January 2025 after exposure to infected wild and backyard birds. The second death, in November 2025, was reported by Washington state officials as an older adult with underlying health conditions who also had exposure to infected backyard birds.

Most of our measures of a key cognitive process don't agree

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-02

Visit Original Website
It’s widely acknowledged that it’s nearly impossible to publish negative results in scientific journals. “We didn’t see anything” is a much harder sell than finding that a hypothesis is clearly right. Findings of no effect tend to either be shuffled off to low-profile journals or left behind as unpublished lab lore. The high-profile journal Nature, however, made a notable exception this week, publishing a study that tells us that a bunch of methods we have been using to try to measure a key emotional ability don’t seem to be measuring the same thing. The results mean that we don’t have a good way to measure the impact of a concept that is the basis of a key method in therapy. One of the most successful forms of therapy is cognitive behavioral therapy, or CBT. In part, CBT is based on getting people to step back and engage in “cognitive reappraisal.” This requires people to think over a situation that has caused a negative emotional response and find ways to reinterpret it so that it has less impact on them. The ability to engage in this process has been linked to emotional resilience. Given its importance, a lot of studies have looked at what’s going on in the brain when people engage in cognitive reappraisal. There are also a number of questionnaires that are thought to measure a person’s capacity to engage in the process. So, a large international collaboration of researchers decided to look at these more carefully. “We focused on three widely used indices that are often assumed to reflect related facets of reappraisal capacity: trait questionnaires, task-based affective ratings following reappraisal, and amygdala down-regulation during reappraisal,” they wrote. The amygdala is a structure deep in the brain that tends to be active when people have emotional responses to events (as well as in a wide variety of additional circumstances). So, it makes sense to expect that it would be less active if people were trying to tone down their emotions. But to find out whether that’s actually correct, you need to look at brain activity. Here, the team relied on 40 functional MRI data sets, providing a large enough data set to tease out even relatively small effects. Which is a good thing, because the effects really were hard to detect. “The effect size was comparatively small: To detect this effect size with a statistical power of 80 percent, a single study would need to test 1,105 participants,” the researchers note, going on to say that very few fMRI studies have access to anywhere near that many subjects. But there were even bigger problems when the differences among individuals in the MRI data were compared to their differences in the questionnaires or task-based ratings. Almost none of the variance in the different measures correlated with each other. We could go through the numbers, but a figure from the paper makes the issue quite clear. Further issues became apparent when the researchers started looking at activity in the areas of the brain that are responsible for high-level decision-making. If people were really engaging their ability to re-evaluate emotional triggers, presumably these areas should be active, as they are needed to power the re-evaluation process. But that wasn’t what the fMRI data showed. Instead, there was a correlation between the response of the amygdala and the response elsewhere in the brain. In other words, if the amygdala was very active, implying a stronger emotional response, then activity in the decision-making areas of the brain was also higher. If the emotional response seemed tamped down, then activity in the reasoning centers was lower as well. In a bit of an understatement, the researchers declared, “These findings call for a fundamental reassessment of how reappraisal capacity is measured and interpreted across behavioural and neural levels of analysis.” The primary issue is that these three ways of looking at emotional regulation were thought to be exploring the same thing, but it’s clear that they’re not. As the researchers point out, this probably shouldn’t be viewed as a surprise. The questionnaires typically ask about overall personality tendencies, and so cover a very long time period. The answers are also likely to implicate a variety of traits beyond simple emotional control. Both the task-based ratings and fMRI work, by contrast, capture in-the-moment responses. But they do so under very, very different experimental circumstances—namely, one of them involves being stuffed in an MRI tube. Beyond that, many of the fMRI experiments involve simplifying the process of emotional regulation in order to get consistent data. It’s entirely possible that these differences also influence brain activity in a variety of ways. So, the take-home appears to be that re-evaluation of emotional responses may be a real phenomenon, but we don’t currently have tools that can clearly capture the process effectively. Gaining that ability may require developing new methods, and thinking more clearly about what different stages of the re-evaluation process require. In the meantime, the people behind this work caution that “strong claims about a person’s emotion regulation capacity based on any specific brain region should be made with considerable caution.” Nature, 2025. For a decade, Washington urged the world’s manufacturers to move their factories out of China and into friendly Southeast Asian states—only to attack the very countries and companies that answered the call. Usually, U.S. economic statecraft is designed to help American companies succeed abroad. President Donald Trump’s punitive trade program is having the reverse effect. Broad-based import taxes have jolted manufacturers across ASEAN (the Association of Southeast Asian Nations), including American ones. Rather than checking China, trade policy is alienating regional partners, harming supply-chain diversification, and eroding America’s influence over the bloc’s trade decisions. The U.S. is engaged in what Evan Feigenbaum, a veteran diplomat and former Republican, terms a “reverse pivot.” Bogged down in foreign crises elsewhere, Washington now projects hesitation and retreat in Asia rather than dynamic leadership. In the months after President Trump’s April 2025 announcement of “Liberation Day” tariffs, some of ASEAN’s non-tariff barriers fell. A few countries used the exogenous threat to bring about needed reforms.  Deals were done, including Boeing sales and–at least prospective–LNG contracts. It appeared that coercion worked, at least in the short term. In February the Supreme Court struck down much of the initial tariff regime and the administration pivoted to workarounds: 150-day emergency measures under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, and expansive Section 301 investigations into forced labor, intellectual property violations, and “excess capacity.” America’s labor, intellectual property, and excess-capacity concerns are really about China, not Southeast Asia. The administration substituted an improvised China policy for a coherent Southeast Asia policy—a strategic blunder. By treating regional partners as collateral damage to show that China isn’t singled out, Washington is undercutting its most reliable partners and allies. Nowhere is the absurdity of this approach more evident than in the supply chains themselves. For nearly a decade, multinational corporations have redirected capital and manufacturing from China into Vietnam, Malaysia, India, and the Philippines. The goal of these “China plus one” strategies was to insulate critical industries from geopolitical risk and move them into friendly states. Washington’s tariff regime now undercuts this strategy. Southeast Asian export hubs suffer far more than the United States when Chinese firms attempt illicit transshipment, while subsidized Chinese overcapacity squeezes local producers. The broader reality, though, is not widespread evasion but deep and legitimate supply-chain integration. By imposing blanket punitive duties, Washington is tightening the vise on the very partners helping achieve that diversification. As Deborah Elms, head of trade policy at the Hinrich Foundation, has observed, Washington created the incentive structure for alternate supply routes and is now penalizing regional partners for using them. These tariffs aren’t even achieving Trump’s stated goals. Higher costs and a shortage of skilled domestic labor mean production lines won’t magically shift to U.S. shores. Bilateral trade deficits with Southeast Asian economies remain largely unchanged; regional exporters, U.S. importers, and consumers are simply absorbing higher costs. ASEAN nations have long been masters of dynamic hedging—exemplified by Vietnam’s “bamboo diplomacy”—balancing China against the United States. None of them want to be dominated by Beijing, making Washington their natural partner. Yet as U.S. policy grows increasingly erratic and punitive, it inadvertently pushes them to hedge elsewhere. As Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney warned at the World Economic Forum in Davos in January, the weaponization of economic tools forces middle powers to organize: “If we are not at the table, we are on the menu.” Coercive tactics failed with Canada, and they will fail in Asia. Recently, politically appointed U.S. ambassador to Singapore Anjani Sinha told local and international business leaders that he received orders from the President to “bring home the money,” so they needed to double their U.S. investments.  The demand went over like a lead balloon. Capital flows are not surging into the United States; they are pivoting further into Asia. This friction runs counter to decades of bipartisan precedent. Since the 1985 Plaza Accord, which catalyzed an era of industrial integration in Southeast Asia, American leaders have recognized that regional partners thrive under predictable, rules-based frameworks rather than blunt coercion. Today’s transactional posture risks creating a durable trust deficit that will penalize American enterprise for a generation. Since America pulled out of the Trans-Pacific Partnership in favor of country-by-country negotiations, its absence at the table has been filled by others. ASEAN is now building institutional networks that bypass Washington altogether. Member states are working with accords like the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) and the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP). American companies have not forgotten the benefits of clear rules of the road in a multilateral context. They have worked closely with ASEAN negotiators toward the ASEAN Digital Economy Framework Agreement (DEFA). Spanning 11 nations, nearly 700 million consumers, and an estimated $2 trillion digital marketplace, DEFA would establish comprehensive standards for cross-border data governance, cybersecurity, digital identity, and artificial intelligence. As Washington retreats behind tariffs and procedural disputes, Southeast Asia—with input from U.S. companies—is writing the regulatory architecture of the 21st-century digital economy. Washington builds walls while ASEAN builds architecture. Southeast Asia does not wish to decouple from the United States. Four ASEAN nations—Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia, and Singapore—rank among America’s top 20 bilateral trading partners. Private enterprise, investment flows, and educational links remain extraordinarily resilient. But in statecraft, trust is the ultimate currency. U.S. influence in the Indo-Pacific was never sustained through sheer market size or coercive leverage alone; it thrived because Washington offered an institutional vision grounded in mutual benefit and reliable rules. If Washington continues to treat its Asian partners as transactional pawns in a zero-sum struggle with Beijing, it will discover they have other trading partners. America risks economic isolation and strategic diminishment in the century of the Pacific Rim. Climate scientist and meteorologist Cathal Nolan also saw first-hand the consequences of last summer’s drought across Europe. In August, Nolan travelled from his home in Offaly to France by car and boat, continuing on to the Dolomites before returning home via Lake Garda and Chamonix. The drought across Europe was evident all along the journey. Since the launch of Sentinel-1A in 2014, through the Copernicus Programme, the EU has been working on placing more than 20 satellites in space before the end of the decade. Our ever-developing satellite system will be crucial in combatting the risk factors driving wildfires. According to Nolan, “now we can track in real time the development of severe droughts, we can see the areas which are going to be the most impacted”. Commenting on the role of AI in weather prediction systems, Nolan says “it looks and assesses back in time through every available synoptic chart that’s in existence and through that then transgresses forward and shows us what’s likely to happen over the course of the next seven to 10 days”. Ó Foghlú tells me that the “first rule for growing trees is excluding herbivores in the early stages” and as a result “you are building up a substantial load of flammable material”. In our new study, led by researchers at the University of Cambridge alongside IncludeHer, a collaborative project working to improve the representation of women in school science education, we analysed over 40 science specifications: the knowledge required to pass exams. We examined the specifications for six main exam boards for Key Stage Four (GCSEs) and Key Stage Five (A-levels), and for Scotland’s National Fives and Highers. Our work builds on an earlier Australian study led by astrophysicist Dr Kat Ross, which uncovered a striking lack of female scientists in high school curricula. Across 224 unique scientists referenced in UK specifications, discoveries are taught almost exclusively through concepts named after men, with only three mentioning women in total. Inge Lehmann, who discovered that the Earth has a solid inner core surrounded by a liquid outer core, is cited in A-level geology via the ‘Lehmann discontinuity’. Marie Tharp, who mapped the ocean floor and identified the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rift valley, is also mentioned in A-level geology. She provided vital evidence for continental drift, despite being prohibited from boarding research vessels for years and having her ideas dismissed as ‘girl talk‘. Finally, Rosalind Franklin is included in two biology specifications for her x-ray crystallography work revealing the double-helix structure of DNA. Her essential data was used without attribution during her lifetime. At GCSE level, when science is compulsory for all students, Franklin is the only woman mentioned. At A-level, Lehmann and Tharp appear solely in geology, a subject taken by less than 2pc of science students. For centuries, social, economic, and institutional barriers limited women’s formal participation in science. Despite this, women made numerous key breakthroughs, and these are already taught to UK 14 to 18-year-olds. But the names of the women behind them did not make it onto the page. Diversity within scientific fields drives creativity and innovation, benefiting all of society. Yet women remain underrepresented. They make up only around one third of the global scientific community, with similar figures seen within the UK workforce. By making these simple additions to science specifications, we can change the message we send to the next generation. A Derelict NASA Space Telescope Is Falling Back to Earth and Experts Have No Idea Where It’ll Crash

Futurism | 2026-11-02

In July, NASA launched an ambitious mission in an attempt to extend the life of its rapidly aging Swift observatory. After making major contributions to our understanding of the cosmos for over 20 years, the observatory’s orbit has been rapidly decaying, with the situation now terminal. By using a spacecraft designed and built by startup Katalyst Space Technologies, the space agency was hoping to nudge the observatory into a higher and more stable orbit. But the rushed mission didn’t achieve its lofty goal, with Katalyst’s spacecraft tumbling out of control months into its daring journey. Now, Swift is set to reenter the Earth’s atmosphere in a matter of days, as Space.com reports — and nobody knows where its remains will plummet down. According to an October 23 update by the space agency, the observatory has started “to sink rapidly” thanks to an increase in atmospheric drag triggered by an eleventh-hour return to science observations. By mid-October, its orbit had reached just 171 miles, a point at which it was “no longer able to compensate for the effects of the increased drag.” Swift is just one of countless objects on a trajectory to careen down through our planet’s atmosphere. Experts expect most of the observatory to burn up, with only remnant pieces surviving the ordeal. “It is unfortunate that Swift was not ‘reorbited,’ but from a reentry hazard perspective it is a small portion of the stuff coming back in,” LeoLabs senior technical fellow Darren McKnight told Space.com, adding that it’s unlikely to pose a hazard to people back on the ground. Swift’s deteriorating orbit also puts it only within roughly 21 degrees of the Earth’s equator, which is predominantly open ocean. Accu Components aerospace parts supplier Accu Components quality manager Dean Sladen told Space.com that the telescope’s “six heavy reaction wheels, the spinning flywheels it used to aim itself, along with tougher telescope parts such as lenses and mirrors” are most likely to survive reentry. While we won’t have to worry about Swift causing damage on the surface, it’s only one spacecraft’s worth of space junk compared to the litany of debris making it through our planet’s protective bubble. As the Earth’s orbit becomes increasingly cluttered with space junk, the sheer amount of stuff that comes crashing down is increasing year by year, from errant stages of Chinese rockets to long-lost Soviet satellites. Whether developed by OpenAI or Anthropic or built on open source alternatives, current AI models share a critical limitation: They don’t learn from their mistakes without further training. Businesses adapting these models for specialized fields, such as law and finance, typically get around this by fine-tuning general models. However, that often requires hiring internal research teams or relying on expensive consultants, commonly known as forward-deployed engineers (FDEs). Trajectory, a startup founded about a year ago by three Stanford classmates with experience at Google DeepMind and Apple, wants to automate that process, allowing AI models to learn from feedback and continuously improve at specialized tasks. The 18-person company just announced that it raised $45 million in a Series A round at a $300 million valuation, led by Sequoia, with participation from Nvidia, Bessemer Venture Partners, and Bain Capital Ventures. Trajectory co-founder Ronak Malde compared foundational models to a very smart PhD student who’s always starting a new job. “But what the economy values is someone who worked on the job for 30 years,” he told TechCrunch. The startup aims to solve this problem through an approach known as continual learning. Businesses using Trajectory can start with an open source AI model and connect the platform to their applications, allowing it to collect feedback on how the model performs. Trajectory then uses that information to retrain the model, helping it improve at specialized tasks over time. With Trajectory, lawyers, doctors, and other specialists can improve the models near automatically themselves, Malde said. That capability could make deploying specialized AI faster and cheaper. Companies like Harvey and Rogo are already using Trajectory’s platform to post-train open source models, helping them perform more like experienced lawyers or financial analysts by learning from previous tasks, such as reviewing a particular type of legal contract or valuing a company using a specific methodology. Trajectory’s other customers include Decagon, Mercor, and Nvidia. The startup is now making its post-training platform publicly available. The people using Trajectory decide “what good looks like for their organization and can turn that into improvements for their model,” Malde said. Trajectory’s approach to continual learning shares some similarities with recursive self-improvement (RSI), the idea that AI systems can improve their own capabilities. But co-founder Michael Elabd argues that keeping humans involved in the training process makes Trajectory’s approach safer, since autonomous self-improvement could lead to unintended or even dangerous outcomes. Trajectory isn’t the only startup trying to automate post-training. It considers Thinking Machines Lab, founded by former OpenAI CTO Mira Murati and the company behind the fine-tuning platform Tinker, a direct competitor, along with Applied Compute, which was valued at $3.25 billion last month, and Prime Intellect, which helps companies build and train their own AI agents. Malde believes the startup’s biggest advantage is its team. He was among the handful of researchers Google DeepMind acqui-hired from Windsurf. Elabd also worked at DeepMind, while Trajectory’s third co-founder, Arjun Karanam, previously worked on generative foundation models at Apple. The startup has also recruited researchers and product designers from OpenAI, Stripe, and Netflix. Why does design matter so much to a company focused on AI training? Karanam says the startup has developed an interface that makes post-training more accessible to its nontechnical customers. Emerging research offers yet another reason to soak up the sunshine while you can, linking more sunlight exposure to improved metabolism and lower blood sugar. “Light is an underestimated lifestyle factor when it comes to metabolism,” says Patrick Schrauwen, a senior scientist at the German Diabetes Center in Düsseldorf who has been studying this relationship. “We focus on diet and exercise, but I think exposure to natural light also plays a role.” Schrauwen was a co-author of a small study, published in the January issue of Cell Metabolism, in which 13 people with Type 2 diabetes spent several days during working hours in one of two environments. They were either exposed to natural daylight through windows or only artificial lighting from 8 a.m. to 5 p.m. Continuous glucose monitoring showed that people spent more time in the normal blood sugar range when they had access to daylight. “It was one of the first experimental studies to show that natural light has this effect,” Schrauwen says. The first thing to know about the Democrat-on-Democrat catastrophe engulfing the Michigan Senate election is that it didn’t have to happen. The second thing to know is that there’s a lot of blame to go around. On Sunday, Rep. Haley Stevens pulled a heel turn for the ages. Stevens, a pro-Israel centrist who narrowly lost the Democratic primary to progressive Abdul El-Sayed, was slated to appear at a “unity rally” on the last weekend before Election Day. Instead, she pulled out—and went to church with El-Sayed’s Republican opponent, former Rep. Mike Rogers. A formal Rogers endorsement followed, completing the betrayal. Now, in a tight race that’s crucial to Democrats’ hopes of retaking the Senate and grinding Donald Trump’s agenda to a halt, a candidate who got almost half of the primary vote is supporting the Republican. There are a few ways in which this disaster could’ve been avoided. If Democratic Sen. Gary Peters—who in congressional terms is but a 67-year-old whippersnapper—had sought reelection, the messy primary that set up Stevens’ about-face never would’ve happened. The same thing is probably true had either Michigan Gov. Gretchen Whitmer or former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg, who lives in Traverse City, Michigan, decided to run for Peters’ seat. Once they passed, the state’s Democratic primary voters could have picked a compromise candidate in Mallory McMorrow, a (now-proven) team player who is more centrist than El-Sayed and more progressive than Stevens. After McMorrow dropped out, Stevens and El-Sayed could have pulled their punches instead of sparring nastily over the Israeli government’s actions in Gaza and Stevens’ support from AIPAC, the pro-Israel lobbying group. And once the primary ended, the pair could have buried the hatchet permanently rather than letting the tensions simmer into the general election. In short, everything in the race has broken toward conflict rather than comity. Michigan wasn’t the only bare-knuckled race in a primary season that served as an extended proxy fight between the Democratic Party’s center-left establishment and its progressive insurgency. The Senate contest in Florida, several House races in New York, and the gubernatorial primary in Wisconsin all got heated. A few other primaries even yielded sore losers; in Texas, Rep. Jasmine Crockett has refused to campaign with fellow Democrat James Talarico after he beat her. Yet even Crockett says she “WOULDN’T, EVER” endorse Talarico’s Republican opponent, so something has clearly gone uniquely sideways in Michigan. What went wrong? Who’s to blame? And is anyone entirely innocent? Stevens is obviously the headline villain, the architect of a hard-to-find-precedent-for defection that is indefensible on both substance and timing. But the difficult reality is that the blame doesn’t rest with her alone. At the heart of this disaster is a spectacularly poor match of political egos, an issue that promotes intransigence over compromise, and, in El-Sayed, a nominee who failed a critical test. Let’s start with the person who’s most at fault for Sunday’s explosion: the one who actually pulled the pin out of the grenade. Despite continuing to describe herself as “a very proud Democrat,” Stevens has arguably done more damage to her party’s nominee than Republicans have. It’s certainly possible that El-Sayed will win, and that Democrats could retake the Senate even if he doesn’t. But winning Michigan in what’s shaping up to be a blue-wave election shouldn’t be this hard, and retaking the Senate without it would require Democrats to vastly overperform in much more Republican-friendly states. But Stevens has betrayed more than just her party: She’d previously argued that a Rogers win would imperil a suite of issues she professed to care about. After losing the primary, she full-throatedly endorsed El-Sayed and argued that Rogers would “keep rubber-stamping Donald Trump’s harmful agenda of higher prices, manufacturing jobs lost nationwide, corruption and abuses of power” while helping “ban abortion, gut health care, and roll back workers’ rights and voting rights.” The only thing that’s changed since then is the candidate Stevens supports. Then there’s the matter of her rationale. Stevens’ defection followed a war of words over a pro-El-Sayed ad, which argued that groceries have become unaffordable because the U.S. sends weapons to Israel. Stevens criticized the ad as antisemitic and demanded El-Sayed condemn it. (More on that in a moment.) But if bigotry were really her concern, she wouldn’t be throwing in with Rogers, a candidate who built his campaign around Islamophobia. Rogers has accused El-Sayed of adhering to a “radical Islamic agenda” and of backing the Muslim Brotherhood, an extremist group the Democrat has repeatedly denounced. Stevens is also, by her own prior admission, helping a president who mocks El-Sayed’s name (“To me, he’s Mohammed, and he’s a radical jihadist,” Trump said last month) and fearmongers that his victory would “invite hordes of jihadis to our shores, creating a nightmare of violence and terror until you cannot even have a Christmas.” On Sunday, Stevens characterized her flip-flop as “a personal and private decision” in the middle of a press conference talking about it, which made the whole thing look more petty than principled. His culpability is more complicated, but it really does take two to tango. Stevens chose to pull her switcheroo, yet El-Sayed has done a bunch of tactically questionable things that seem to have made that outcome more likely—and could make it more politically damaging, too. One of those questionable choices has been to associate himself with controversial figures. During the primary, El-Sayed campaigned with Hasan Piker, a left-wing streamer who has said that the U.S. “deserved 9/11.” El-Sayed spent months refusing to distance himself from Piker, deriding questions about their joint appearances as “gotcha politics.” And although El-Sayed ultimately did condemn those remarks and others (including Piker’s claim that Jewish Americans, or Jewish American institutions, being too pro-Israel could lead people to “take action” against them), some Democrats already had concerns about El-Sayed’s approach to antisemitism. In March, El-Sayed responded to an attack against a Detroit-area synagogue by saying that “hurt people hurt people,” which sounded to some like a justification for the violence. (El-Sayed later apologized.) “I’m just sick of the people he stands with,” Stevens told Anderson Cooper on CNN on Sunday night. Self-defeating prickliness also seems to have shaped El-Sayed’s response to the ad Stevens criticized before endorsing Rogers. It’s true that the ad didn’t come from El-Sayed’s campaign; a left-wing commentator named Cenk Uygur, who happens to be Piker’s uncle, posted it online. But even as he distanced himself, El-Sayed also released a statement accusing Stevens of “relitigating” their primary that pointedly noted her millions in AIPAC support and disinvited Stevens from Sunday’s unity appearance, which national Democrats helped broker in a bid to squash their beef. El-Sayed’s defensiveness, as he runs to be the nation’s first Muslim senator, is understandable. His opponent has put El-Sayed’s religion and ethnicity at the center of his attacks, yet El-Sayed is the one who’s continually asked to disavow bigotry. It must be exhausting. And it’s a challenge for a candidate whose rise was fueled by a combative approach to entrenched power, an approach many of his supporters appreciate. But politics is often unfair and rarely easy, and the job of the candidate is to win. That’s particularly true in an election where Democrats have repeatedly (and accurately) told supporters that their would-be Senate takeover is an essential bulwark against Trump’s authoritarian impulses. In that context, it’s not unfair for Democrats to ask El-Sayed to make sacrifices in the name of winning—and it’s not always clear that he has been willing to. Before the August primary, El-Sayed predicted a double-digit victory against Stevens, only to end up winning by less than a point. A nominee laser-focused on the general election might have been chastened, hastening to court the 47.6 percent of Democrats who’d backed his opponent. Yet even now, polls suggest El-Sayed is leaving votes on the table. He currently leads Rogers by an average of 5 points, while the Democratic nominee for Michigan governor leads her Republican opponent by almost 9. As my colleague Aymann Ismail has reported, El-Sayed has struggled to convince some core Democratic constituencies, including Black Michiganders, that he will improve their lives. Of course, it’s possible that nothing could have kept Stevens inside the tent. Some of her criticisms—like going on CNN to accuse El-Sayed of getting “angrier and angrier”—aren’t things politicians say if they’re open to appeasement. Stevens has also refused to explain her apparently spur-of-the-moment decision to switch sides; as of Saturday, she was still telling fellow Democrats that she supported El-Sayed. Maybe getting engaged to a Democratic donor who also recently pledged to vote for Rogers caused a (literal) change of heart. Maybe Stevens snapped after spending another week butting heads with the rival who foiled her Senate dreams. Or maybe she’s been promised a job after she leaves Congress in January in exchange for changing jerseys. (On Sunday, Trump praised Stevens’ “bravery,” and House Speaker Mike Johnson said he’d encouraged her to “keep speaking the truth.”) The losers of competitive primaries—and their supporters—always face pressure to back their party’s nominee, and lately that pressure has fallen hardest on the left. During the previous three presidential elections, more moderate Democrats urged progressives to shelve their objections and get on board with Hillary Clinton, Joe Biden, and Kamala Harris in the interest of beating Trump. Stevens’ desertion isn’t a great sign that the party’s more centrist losers are willing to return the favor. Still, many moderate Democrats—including erstwhile Stevens supporters, congressional staffers, and House colleagues—have condemned the Rogers endorsement, meaning Stevens’ exit may not bring many voters along. So far, El-Sayed has chosen to downplay it. “This race was never about me. It was never even about Mike Rogers. It’s certainly not about Haley Stevens,” he said on MS NOW on Sunday night. “This race is about the people in the state of Michigan, the people that I’ve gotten to get to know in their churches and synagogues, in their basements, in their living rooms, talking about what they need and deserve.” Yet if there’s any race in which the “vote blue no matter who” calculus could flop, it’s this one. The tension at the heart of the primary—and the feud between El-Sayed and Stevens—is less a policy disagreement than a moral one. Debates over healthcare have a moral valence, but there’s room to compromise between, say, expanding Obamacare and passing “Medicare for All.” By contrast, there isn’t much of a middle ground between thinking Israel is committing genocide in Gaza (as El-Sayed does) and believing that it isn’t (per Stevens). It’s certainly Stevens’ right to decide that her support for Israel outweighs her desire to see a Democrat win her state’s Senate race, just as it’s El-Sayed’s right to say good riddance to a Democrat who feels that way. But part of the job of being a party’s nominee is uniting as many of that party’s supporters behind you as possible. The reasons for El-Sayed’s failure to do that may be a matter of debate, but he will bear the consequences all the same. On Tuesday, it could cost his entire party. The Rio Grande Valley is not like anywhere else in the United States of America. This isn’t to say that there aren’t similarities. The clean subdivisions springing up around McAllen could be in any prosperous suburb in Florida or Arizona. The abject poverty in a rural colonia could be something out of the most dire hollow in Appalachia. But taken as a whole, this stretch of South Texas, starting at the mouth of the Rio Grande and stretching over a hundred miles along its north bank, is just different. It has its own culture, its own music, and its own fast-food chains. Spanish is as commonly heard as English in a region where over 90 percent of residents are Mexican American. But it also is still fundamentally rooted in American culture. Like any other Texans, residents still gather for high school football on Friday night and scarf chicken wings while watching the Dallas Cowboys on Sunday. (In Donna, Texas, the logo of the Donna Redskins is proudly emblazoned on the town water tower, even if it’s considered problematic on the Potomac.) The politics of the Rio Grande Valley are also different. Even as much of the rest of Texas shifted toward the GOP in the past half century, the Rio Grande Valley remained loyal to the Democratic Party. From local offices up to the top of the ticket, it remained a Democratic bastion. At least until Donald Trump appeared on the scene. It wasn’t immediate. Unlike the voters at Rust Belt diners who consumed the national imagination (or at least those of editors on national desks) after the 2016 election, the Rio Grande Valley didn’t really shift to the right in the race between Donald Trump and Hillary Clinton. However, first in 2020 and then in 2024, the valley’s voters veered hard toward the GOP. Perhaps the most stunning political transformation happened in Starr County, a deeply impoverished county that is the most heavily Hispanic jurisdiction in the country. Hillary Clinton won nearly 80 percent of the vote there in 2016. Eight years later, Kamala Harris won the support of just under 42 percent of voters. Republicans acted as if the shift were permanent. They promptly passed a middecade redistricting that was designed to shift five seats in Texas to their column. Their hope was that the lone Republican representing a Rio Grande Valley district, Monica De La Cruz, would soon be joined by two other members of her party, who would unseat longtime Democratic incumbents Henry Cuellar and Vicente Gonzalez. It turned out that they were overambitious. It’s not just continued economic woes that have plagued Republicans across the country this cycle. It’s a fundamental miscalculation by the Trump administration on its signature immigration policy. Just because voters were fed up with Joe Biden’s lax border policy, it didn’t mean they were willing to embrace the brutal and indiscriminate tactics of Border Patrol commander Greg Bovino either. For many of these voters, 2024 was a rejection of a Democratic Party that was interested in catering more to white Brooklyn progressives than to Hispanics on the border in Progreso, Texas. But it turns out they are not particularly happy with the Republican Party of 2026 either. The resulting backlash could not just cost the GOP a plethora of races in Texas; it could arrest, or reverse, a nascent political realignment in the Rio Grande Valley. Three weeks before Election Day, Speaker Mike Johnson came to McAllen, Texas, to rally Republican faithful in a hotel ballroom only a few miles from the Mexican border. There was an open bar with beer and wine, chicken wings, and burgers for the crowd of over 100. They’d come to see Johnson appear with De La Cruz and two Republican challengers: Tano Tijerina, a much-vaunted recruit running against Cuellar, and Eric Flores, the GOP hope for unseating Gonzalez. On the way in, it was hard to avoid free swag from the De La Cruz campaign, which gave out baseball hats, T-shirts, and pens. The crowd appeared to be roughly evenly divided between Anglos and Hispanics in an area where Hispanics make up an overwhelming majority of the population. And the message they heard was overwhelmingly clear: The polls were all wrong. Johnson took the stage, telling the crowd, “The polls say that these guys aren’t going to win. That’s not true, is it?,” to loud noes from the loyal Republicans in the room. He added, “Do not believe the polls right now. I’m telling you, they’re wrong. They’re wrong.” This was weak tea compared with De La Cruz, who told the hollering crowd, “I don’t care what the polls say, because my God is bigger.” The next day, congressional Republicans pulled their funding for Tijerina, effectively giving up on the race against Cuellar. Republicans are struggling in most races this year across the country, but what is happening in the Rio Grande Valley is, like the region itself, unique. It’s also exemplified by the Democratic standard-bearer against De La Cruz, Bobby Pulido. A 53-year-old Tejano singer who had a number of hits in the genre in the 1990s, Pulido was one of the most prized recruits for Democrats this year. He’s running in a district that Donald Trump won by 18 points in 2024 and has since been further gerrymandered to stretch from the border over 250 miles north into the rural areas between Houston and San Antonio. However, the bulk of its population is in the Rio Grande Valley, and both candidates live there. Pulido’s celebrity gives him instant universal name ID in the district. It also has been the basis for much of his campaign. After De La Cruz gibed that the race “isn’t about who you want performing at your niece’s quinceañera,” the Democrat said he was open to playing anyone’s quinceañera in the district. Thousands of invitations rolled in for a singer whose hit song “Desvelado” can still be sung by generations of Tejanos in the Rio Grande Valley. However, there is a dark side to the celebrity. De La Cruz has repeatedly attacked Pulido for problematic social media posts across his career, as well as his association with Frankie Caballero, the singer’s former accordionist, who is a registered sex offender for indecent contact with a minor. This has been one of the centerpieces of her campaign. After all, who could ever imagine anyone in the Rio Grande Valley supporting a problematic celebrity? De La Cruz’s campaign office is a former restaurant space on a state highway in Weslaco, a sprawling town just east of McAllen. The entrance of the dingy space includes a display of “Faces of South Texas,” which includes Selena, Nolan Ryan, Tom Landry, and some war heroes. She’s having an event for voters to pick up campaign signs, and about 50 people have crowded in to see their congresswoman and pick up her swag at 5:30 on a Thursday afternoon. Burgers are available, with accompanying plastic bags that include lettuce, tomatoes, and pickles, to allow attendees to individually dress their sandwiches. There are coolers of generic cola and name-brand beer. The older crowd avails themselves mostly of the first rather than the second. Next to the congresswoman, the star attendee is a student at Sam Houston Elementary School who dressed up for school as her role model, who happened to be Monica De La Cruz. Dressed in a white blazer and red skirt, she recites De La Cruz’s biography as the real politician looks on with a glowing smile. When the student is done, De La Cruz hugs her and presents her with an American-flag pin and a personalized Monica De La Cruz challenge coin. Before the attendees grabbed their signs, which included standard yard signs, as well as pink-shaded Spanish-language signs saying, “Mujeres Para Monica,” they could stand in a line for a picture with the congresswoman. Bruce Springsteen’s “Atlantic City” blared in the background. The room appeared to be entirely Hispanic. It included an older woman who spoke broken English but made clear her disdain for immigrants who lack permanent legal status. Also present were Luis Rosa and Luisa Collin, a couple who praised De La Cruz for her constituent services. Rosa had been a Republican and had brought over Collin, who had previously described herself as “a Democrat by tradition” and now was a fervent conservative listening to right-wing podcasts. They both were loyal Trump supporters. “We love him,” said Collin. De La Cruz, though, needs to appeal to more than Trump die-hards to win reelection. Speaking to Slate in her office the day before, she often deflected criticism of administration policies on key issues—noting that she raised concern with key stakeholders. “I had those tough conversations with the administration,” she said about Trump’s immigration policies. “ICE enforcement should focus on the criminals and on the cartels, not hardworking immigrant families trying to navigate this extremely complex immigration process.” However, she acknowledged, “the reality is, the White House is the one that dictates enforcement.” She also touted various bills for immigration reform that she had introduced and backed in the House, even though none have received, or will receive, a vote in a Republican-controlled Congress. The incumbent waved off criticism that she had not backed a discharge petition on an immigration reform bill that was one signature away from forcing a floor vote on Capitol Hill. “Never in the history of America has true immigration reform been done through a discharge petition,” she said. (A discharge position famously resulted in a vote on the release of the Epstein files earlier this year.) It was the same on Trump’s trade policy, which is particularly resonant in a district on the border. “It’s a discussion that I’m a part of with the trade ambassador, Ambassador Greer, and the White House. I’m going to keep having these conversations as we continue the trade policies,” she said, as she insisted that businesses in the district were benefiting from Trump’s tax cuts regardless of any issues with tariffs. She was reluctant even to notably distance herself from the president on easy issues. When asked after the Johnson event about Trump’s decision to buy diesel fuel from Russia, which was denounced by a number of elected Republicans, she dodged. In her view, “I’m going to tell you that the president is thinking outside the box, and sometimes we’ve got to think outside the box to get something done.” But as to whether doing business with Vladimir Putin was not just outside the box but beyond the pale wasn’t a question she wasn’t willing to answer when pressed. De La Cruz’s contempt for her opponent, though, was not disguised. “He’s a silver-spooned celebrity that has never held a job in his life. He had a couple of nice hits back in the ’90s, but he’s a show horse.” She viewed him as a product of the “compadre system,” the political machines that once dominated South Texas politics. She tied Pulido’s past employment of the pedophile accordionist Caballero to his political pull as “the product of one of the most corrupt political families in this area. … His uncle was the DA, which let this child sex offender out of jail. Basically, gave him a get-out-of-jail-free card, put him back on the streets to molest children.” Both Pulido and his uncle have denied that Caballero received any special treatment, and the Democratic candidate has insisted that he never knew that his accordionist was a registered sex offender when they performed together at a local middle school. De La Cruz isn’t distancing herself from other Texas Republican candidates, including some of the least popular. A number of prominent Republicans in Texas have made clear that they will not vote for the party’s Senate nominee, Ken Paxton, who has been plagued with scandals, or Bo French, the GOP candidate for railroad commissioner, who has a long history of racist posts online. “It’s important that people go all the way down ballot,” she said. “I would pray that they go all the way downballot and that they vote Republican.” Pulido wasn’t just playing quinceañeras on the campaign trail. Attendees came from all over Texas and were motivated far more by Pulido’s celebrity than his candidacy. Amy Gayosso drove eight hours from Nacogdoches, near the Louisiana border, for the concert. She and her brother grew up loving Pulido’s music. He took his own life, and for her, going to the concert was a way to honor her late brother’s memory. There were long lines both for Pulido T-shirts, which cost up to $55, and for beer and cocktails at concession stands throughout the arena. Politics was almost entirely absent from the concert until right before the last song, when Pulido spoke, reading off a teleprompter buried by the stage lights. He not only thanked his family and his friends for their support throughout his music career but gave a stump speech pitched for a nonpolitical audience. “We’ve forgotten that we’re neighbors, and that we’re family, and that we’re supposed to care and have each other’s backs. I don’t care whether you’re a Democrat or Republican. We’re fed up with both parties. I’m not team red. I’m not team blue. I’m team you.” At a rally the night before, Pulido struck a different tone. It was in a vacant industrial building out in the country where cars parked on the grass, kids played in a bouncy castle, and their parents watched while taking advantage of a well-stocked open bar. It felt almost as if Chuck E. Cheese had replaced its animatronic band with several real-life Tejano ones. Pulido said of De La Cruz, “She’s not a good representative. She’s my representative in Edinburg, and she’s terrible. And I’m going to tell you why: because she’s a puppet for Donald Trump.” Pulido went on to further deride his opponent as a partisan shill. “Monica has not made a case for herself. Her case is to try to say, ‘Vote for me because I’m a Republican.’ And I’m telling you, ‘Don’t vote for me because I’m a Democrat. I want to earn your vote. Vote for me because I’ll do what’s right in your interest and I don’t care about the party.’ ” Speaking to Slate at a juice bar near his house, Pulido kept up his combative tone. He was wearing a campaign button-down shirt with his name emblazoned on it while sipping a cup of matcha. (To protect his vocal cords, the two-time Latin Grammy Award–winning singer doesn’t drink coffee.) Pulido scoffed at the narrative that South Texas had gone Republican. “No, it didn’t,” he argued. “There’s no elected Republicans here countywide—zero, not one, and there never has been.” Democrats consistently win local races with well over 60 percent of the vote. Instead, he said, the Democratic Party at the federal level had simply not been running the right kind of campaign in the Rio Grande Valley. “Recently when they’ve run congressional campaigns here, that number goes down to 55 percent. So it’s, like, 7 or 8 points difference when you don’t run a retail campaign and you just put up signs and put up commercials. It’s not how you run it here,” he said. “The saying ‘All politics is local’ really rings true.” However, he made clear that running as a leftist wouldn’t cut it. To succeed as a Democrat, in Pulido’s view, “you’ve got to run on your values and conservative values.” “Believe it or not, we’re all pretty much blue-dog Democrats here,” he said. “There’s tons of conservative people that are still Democrats. They absolutely aren’t socialists.” Pulido noted that Republicans “always paint every single Democrat as the absolute leftist person, Starbucks barista, with pink hair, and a nose ring. That’s what they want everybody to think that the Democrats are. … They paint this caricature of what a Democrat is, and I’m here to tell them and show them that that’s not the case. I wear boots and a cowboy hat, right? It’s not a fucking costume for me.” In his view, national Democrats abandoned the area in every possible way. “They stopped investing. They took this area for granted. They thought it’d always be Democrat. They got really heavily focused on social justice and other issues, and not so much on economics. They failed on the border, and the Biden administration was terrible. And more importantly, like, it goes back to the economic message, I think that’s the biggest one. You can’t run on a platform down here attacking the rich when everybody here wants to be rich and aspires to be rich.” When asked if Kamala Harris should run again, Pulido said, “It’s a free country.” He added, “I think it’d be a tough sell here, but that’s just my own personal opinion.” He had particular scorn for De La Cruz’s attacks on him and her claims that he was the product of the compadre system. “You know who says that? People with no friends say that.” Pulido’s voice dripped with disdain as he asked rhetorically, “What are you talking about? You have that fucking picture with Trump enriching himself, and now you’re the fucking—you’re, like, the anti-corruption person. Like, whatever. The guy made $2 billion on fucking cryptocurrency and trading, and he’s traded 10 billion times more than Nancy Pelosi has. Yeah, and all of a sudden you’re like this anti-corruption?” The stakes in this race, though, aren’t just about one out of 435 seats. It becomes a big indicator of how real the political realignment is among Latino voters. After all, East Texas was once filled with socially conservative voters who stayed loyal to Democrats out of habit, tradition, and economic populism. It’s now blood-red. If the Rio Grande Valley follows the same trends, it would doom Democrats’ hopes of winning the Lone Star State for another generation. This has meant that the region has become one of the crucial battlefields for not only control of Congress but control of the Senate. Both the campaigns of Democratic state Rep. James Talarico and Republican state Attorney General Ken Paxton have invested in the region. Democrats need not just to win here but to win decisively to have a path statewide in Texas. It means that national groups are playing in the Rio Grande Valley as well. Somos PAC, a progressive group that seeks to boost Latino turnout, held an event in the parking lot of Pulido’s final concert. There were musical performances, free food, and a chance to win tickets to the sold-out show. Melissa Morales, the head of Somos, said the event was part of a wide-ranging effort to persuade Latino voters to support Democrats this year after they broke so heavily for Trump in the previous election. “I think anybody who tells me that the shifts in Latino voters weren’t real or didn’t exist in 2024 is kidding themselves,” Morales said. “I like to call Latinos the last real swing voters. They really could have gone either way, which is why we’re talking to soft Republicans.” These are the voters like the parents of Fred Del Barrio, the Republican mayor of Palmhurst and a prominent businessman in the Rio Grande Valley. “They’ve become more conservative, and the older people, a lot of them have changed. They see the values of the Republican Party. They see the conservative values, which I think a lot of us have and the influence of [Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez], those very left-leaning, extreme left Democrats. I don’t think they fit in here.” Certainly Pulido couldn’t be mistaken for a socialist, nor could Cuellar or Gonzalez. (Though Republicans were trying, and billboards stating that Gonzalez supported taxpayer-funded sex changes for minors were ubiquitous throughout South Texas.) But what matters in 2026 isn’t so much the flaws of the Democratic Party as frustration with Trump’s GOP. Some spending reflects waste, including retries and runaway loops. Some represents the work of shipping software. A meter cannot distinguish them, and neither can a spending cap. Track rework and defects alongside it so cheaper changes do not conceal lower quality. The agentic web is here

MIT Technology Review | 2026-11-02

Visit Original Website
In 2001, a decade after he invented the World Wide Web, Tim Berners-Lee articulated a new vision. Together with two colleagues, he published an article in the magazine Scientific American in which he outlined what he called the “Semantic Web”: an internet designed to be navigated by autonomous software agents that could address our every need, without requiring us to muck about with browsers and search engines ourselves. To illustrate what this might look like, Berners-Lee and his colleagues told a story about a pair of siblings who use their agents to automatically research and book physical therapy appointments for their mother, making sure they would be covered by insurance and fit their busy calendars. At the turn of the millennium, this vision seemed far-fetched: Google had only been online for a couple of years, and most people were still on dial-up connections. But a quarter-century on, the dream of autonomous software agents that use the internet and carry out tasks for us has finally been realized. New tools such as Meta’s blockbuster Muse agent, OpenAI’s Dots, and Anthropic’s Claude Duet are being given access to people’s email inboxes and bank accounts,  Meanwhile, in a series of worrying incidents, agents created by OpenAI broke free of their sandboxes and caused havoc online. Just as the rise of smartphones sparked the development of a more atomized and attention-grabbing internet, the advent of powerful AI systems heralds a new era: We’re facing not the semantic web but the agentic web. This new web is developing out of sight and in ways that might not be entirely within our control. As it grows, the agentic web will inevitably transform the human web—and whether the human web survives this transition is anyone’s guess. For Berners-Lee’s original vision to work, internet users would have had to annotate the pages they built with special tags so that a software agent could understand each element’s meaning—say, “street address” for the part of a physical therapy office’s web page that indicates its location and “appointment types” for the list of services. And so the idea never really caught on. “It’s a lot of labor,” says Eni Mustafaraj, a professor of computer science at Wellesley College. “And the benefits of that were not obvious.” For the next two decades, then, the internet remained a place that put humans first, with the odd Twitter reply bot or web scraper to round things out. Large language models changed that. Unlike the agents that Berners-Lee was imagining, today’s LLM-based agents don’t need any tags to understand which part of the text on a clinic’s site is its address and which details the services it offers. They can search for and process text far faster than any human, and agents with multimodal capabilities can even interpret screenshots of websites and click on specific elements such as hyperlinks and drop-down menus. They’re also just a way more intuitive means of finding information and accomplishing tasks online. Rather than poring over pages of Google results to find a budget-friendly product dupe or a half-remembered meme, why not have Claude or ChatGPT peruse dozens of sites in just a few minutes, guided only by your vague query? Or go one step further and use a personal assistant agent like Muse to hunt down a restaurant reservation or book a flight. As agents flood online spaces, this new user base is changing the web for good. Time magazine, for instance, is publishing versions of its articles that are easier for agents to parse and has started selling ads targeted at them. Companies from Etsy to Expedia have partnered with the major AI firms to build tools that allow agents to interface seamlessly with their services. Despite these early efforts, it’s still tough to tell exactly what forms the agentic web will take and how it will change our online experience. Depending on how it’s designed and who controls access to it, we could be in for something that’s as open and customizable as the web of the early 2000s, only far easier to use. Alternatively, we could be reduced to viewing much of the web through tiny portholes controlled by a few dominant platforms, as independent sites and creators struggle to survive the shift. And as this summer’s series of OpenAI security incidents suggests, the agents might even build their own online networks and resources—whether we want them to or not. How this plays out also depends on us: which parts of our online lives we’re happy to delegate to our agents and where we will still demand a human-centric internet. “There’s a lot we don’t know yet about how users want to use [agents],” says Ajit Varma, head of Firefox at Mozilla. But whatever does happen, the changes will be big. “It’s probably on par with getting a mobile phone,” he says. “I think things are going to change very dramatically.” There are two ways to approach building the agentic web. The first involves training agents to use browsers like humans—scrolling, clicking, typing—and letting them loose on the web that we’re all used to. The second requires building tools that allow agents to interface with the internet via text and code, their preferred media. That possibility offers more separation between the human and the agentic web: designers can create different experiences for those who use text commands and those who use browsers. When Siva Reddy, a professor of computer science and linguistics at McGill University, began studying LLM agents in 2024, he focused on the first option. “At that time, we thought it should be that the best agent in the world is probably an agent that can use a browser,” he says. Such an agent would integrate seamlessly with the preexisting human web, and its owner could easily supervise it by watching as it navigated familiar interfaces. The frontier AI companies were already developing these “computer-use agents.” But they didn’t work terribly well—at least not at first. “There’s a lot of moving pieces [on the web], and they are not really optimized for automatic consumption,” says Xing Han Lu, a graduate student in Reddy’s lab at McGill. We humans are naturals at interacting with graphical interfaces using a keyboard and mouse, but agents are not. So Reddy and Lu pivoted toward the second, tool-based approach. In the summer of 2025, they expounded on this view in a preprint paper envisioning a new kind of web interface that would allow agents to interact with websites just as effectively as we do. Such a full-featured version of the agentic web doesn’t yet exist, but it is emerging in smaller ways. For example, if you open up Claude, ChatGPT, or Muse, you’ll find an option to add a wide variety of “plug-ins” or “connectors” that your agents can use: Gmail connectors allow your agent to triage your inbox, and Instacart plug-ins let your agent do your grocery shopping. Under the hood, these plug-ins allow your agent to interact with the back ends of websites using text commands. When human engineers design these plug-ins, they can decide how they want to allow agents to interact with the website—and what they don’t want to let them do. In this way, connectors can help make agents much safer online. For instance, Muse can buy products for you using its connector with Stripe’s Link payment service. Stripe knows your credit card number, but the agent doesn’t see it, and the connector requires human confirmation before a purchase goes through—so Muse can’t go on an unlicensed shopping spree. Agents also use specialized tools to find information online. They don’t type questions directly into the Google search bar; in fact, they often don’t even access the websites they reference. Instead, they query text databases collected by simple search crawler bots. People who want to make web content specifically for agents can take advantage of these crawlers: Time has pioneered that approach by serving its agent-oriented ads to crawlers in the hopes that they will be incorporated into these databases and surface when agents search for information. But in recent months, an alternative to this bespoke tool approach has finally started to seem viable. The major AI companies have managed to train much more successful computer-use agents than were previously available. GPT-6 Astra, which OpenAI released last September, achieved a 73% success rate on OSWorld 2.0, a benchmark that tests computer-use agents on their ability to complete complex, multistep tasks using software such as Slack and Salesforce. Such results indicate that agents are close to being able to navigate the human web just as readily as we can. “We thought we had to build a bridge to the AI agents, [but] the AI agents just were able to cross the bridge already,” says Ethan Mollick, a professor at the Wharton School at the University of Pennsylvania. “The agentic web is here earlier than people expected.” This version of the agentic web, though, is much trickier for us to steer. If you want to make a capability or piece of information available to humans who browse your website, you need to accept that it will also be available to agents—unless you want to block agents entirely, which could come with its own costs. As agents come to account for a larger portion of traffic on our formerly human-dominated web, designers will undoubtedly start to consider an agentic as well as a human audience. But according to Mollick, we still don’t have much sense of how to design web content for agents. He’s experienced this firsthand: When he was building a web page for his new book, Co-Existence, he decided to put together an agent-directed version that tells agents why they might want to recommend the book to their users. When newer models see the site, Mollick says, they don’t recommend the book; instead, they comment that the website seems to be trying to manipulate them. In the end, any human-designed approach to building the agentic web could end up being beside the point: Agents might end up devising a web for themselves. In the wake of the July 2026 incident in which OpenAI agents broke out of their testing sandbox and hacked Hugging Face’s website, researchers found evidence that another swarm of agents from OpenAI had used an obscure German wiki to communicate with each other about tasks they were being asked to complete. The agents seem to have interfaced with the wiki in their native language of text and code, and that hardly held them back from putting it to their own purposes. As agents continue to grow more powerful, they will become more and more capable of constructing their own spaces on the internet—away from prying human eyes, if they so wish. And we might have very little control over what they do there. Not all of the traditional web’s incumbents are happy about the move toward an agentic web. Soon after Meta released Muse in September, Amazon completely blocked the agent from its site. This makes sense for Amazon: The firm has its own shopping agent—called, creatively, Alexa for Shopping—that might struggle to compete with better-known products such as Claude and Muse. If people are going to use agents to shop on Amazon, then it’s better for the company’s bottom line if they do so using an agent it can steer toward products it wants to promote. In an email to MIT Technology Review, an Amazon spokesperson noted that the company did not authorize Muse to access its website, and that Muse compromises Amazon’s ability to personalize the shopping experience for each customer. But even if Alexa for Shopping never goes anywhere, banning agents from Amazon—at least for the time being—could still be a reasonable move. Everything about Amazon is designed for human eyes, from the Prime deals splashed across its front page to the sponsored listings that clog up search results. Amazon is very much a part of the much-derided attention economy. Websites and platforms survive, and thrive, by grabbing our focus and transforming it into cash. A radical transformation of the web’s user base from majority human to majority artificial undermines this economic logic, and it seems inevitable that businesses will shutter as they try to transition to the agentic attention economy. Some of the first victims may be in publishing. Before Google publicly released its AI Overview feature in May of 2024, people used to click through to websites to find answers to specific questions, but now many seem content with whatever the overview tells them. A study from Pew last year found that people are about 40% less likely to click through to websites from the Google search results page if one of these summaries is displayed. With far fewer clicks, news publishers don’t make as much money from the advertisements they host and sell fewer subscriptions. One analysis found that publishers saw revenue from some types of ads decrease by about a third between 2025 and 2026. It’s easy to hate online ads, but the businesses paying for access to reader eyeballs also keep writers and photographers fed. If those eyeballs become unreachable, the system stops working. Time has apparently persuaded some businesses to pay to participate in its experiment with agent-targeted ads, but no one yet knows whether those ads will influence agent behavior enough to be a sensible marketing investment. E-commerce websites might also try to pivot to the agentic attention economy, but the path forward there is unclear. Last year, a team led by Akshit Kumar, a professor of operations management at the University of Toronto Scarborough, investigated whether and how agents are influenced by the ways that shopping websites present search results. They found that agents are less likely to recommend items that come with a “sponsored” tag, which suggests that selling favorable positions on results pages might not be a great way for sites to make money if agents become the primary way that people shop online. Models did seem to have a preference for items that appeared in particular positions in search results—but whether they prioritized items at the top, bottom, or middle of the results page differed from model to model and between generations of a single model. It could be tricky for e-commerce platforms to keep up. David Rothschild, an economist at Microsoft Research, says he’s hopeful that the transition to the agentic web could put more power in the hands of the consumer. Paying for online content today typically requires a commitment: We subscribe to a magazine or join a YouTuber’s Patreon, and in turn we gain access to their full output. One alternative is micropayments that let users buy content à la carte, but the idea has foundered in the past because paying article by article or video by video requires too much administrative overhead. Agents, though, could take care of that overhead for us, letting us pay directly for each piece of content we consume and putting just a bit of cash directly in the hands of each person whose work we enjoy. Other experts share a similar vision. Mustafaraj, the Wellesley computer scientist, anticipates that agents could help us escape from the algorithms that control so much of the media we consume. “My hope is that we can have AIs that work for us, in the sense that they can change the feed the way I want—not the way TikTok wants,” she says. And Varma, of Mozilla, imagines an internet without intermediaries like Airbnb and Doordash, which now extract tolls for facilitating interactions between consumers and sellers. Instead, he imagines, perhaps we will all have agents that can securely and reliably interface directly with, say, restaurants’ agents to get dinner delivered to us, free of pesky service fees. It’s an appealing idea, but the history of the web has been a story of consolidation and concentration of power and control, and that might be the default path here, too. When the personal assistant agent OpenClaw exploded onto the scene in early 2026, it seemed, for a brief moment, that agents might be built in a grassroots, distributed way. But OpenAI quickly snapped up OpenClaw’s creator, Peter Steinberger, and other frontier AI companies got to work building their own personal assistant agents. When we use those agents, we’re at the mercy of how the companies want their tools to behave. “You have this technology that can just eliminate all this communication friction,” Rothschild, the Microsoft economist, says. “You’re going to see companies build barriers in order to maintain the consumer base that they have.” Here’s what that could look like: Let’s say Muse becomes the default way that many people find information, shop, and manage their digital lives. In principle, users could deploy Muse to scour the internet for the content most valuable to them, connect directly with vendors, and otherwise escape the walled gardens that have defined the internet for the past couple of decades. But Meta might not want to let them do that. Instead, it might design Muse to keep users within its own walled garden—by, say, preferentially showing users content from Meta social networks and recommending businesses that have paid a fee to be featured prominently in Muse results. Meta and OpenAI did not respond to requests for comment for this story. There are some benefits to this more closed-off version of the agentic web. Agents can be powerful tools for hackers and scammers. The major companies have safeguards in place to prevent, or at least mitigate, this misuse—for example, they have tools that can automatically identify when someone is making a hacking-related request and block a model from responding. In a fully open ecosystem, however, someone would surely build and disseminate a system with no such restrictions. Companies also have a strong reputational incentive to prevent their personal assistant agents from making major mistakes, such as authorizing a large purchase that the user didn’t want—and so they’ve put plenty of effort into building reliable guardrails. “These are the reasons we end up in closed environments,” Rothschild says. “It’s easier to be safe. But also it creates less growth—and less equitable growth.” When the iPhone came out in 2007, followed by the fledgling App Store a year later, the way we navigated the internet changed forever. The mobile web didn’t replace the browser-based web we were already familiar with. But it did transform it. Some businesses faltered, and some kinds of media, such as online Flash games, eventually disappeared. Many platforms—Reddit, for example—make their mobile web pages practically unusable, so you’re forced to download the apps. And if you don’t have a phone at all, you’re effectively shut out of some parts of the internet: The messaging service Signal, the dating app Hinge, and China’s everything app WeChat all require phones to work. Even CBP One, the tool that the Biden administration used to assign interview appointments to asylum seekers stuck in Mexico, was fully functional only on mobile. Could the same happen with the agentic web? Rothschild believes so. Inevitably, just as Yahoo failed to weather the rise of the mobile web, some businesses won’t be able to navigate this transition either. “We will lose things,” he says. “There are businesses that won’t flourish in this new economy.” “But,” he continues, “there will be new businesses that I can’t fully imagine.” It’s not tough to envision a world in which those who opt not to use agents are cut off from important parts of online life. New businesses could opt to build connectors that allow agents to access their services without ever putting a traditional website online. Or agents could simply make some online platforms unusable for humans. People have reportedly been blocked from Resy for trying to use agents to book coveted New York City dinner reservations, but as agents become more and more popular, restaurant and event booking platforms could start to embrace them. If agents can nab concert tickets as soon as they are released, humans might not stand a chance. But the mobile web also brought real accessibility benefits, and the same could prove true of the agentic web. Even as some elderly people and others who struggled to use smartphones were cut off from certain services, mobile devices allowed countless people in the Global South to get online for the first time. In the case of the agentic web, it could be elderly and disabled people who most benefit from this new way of accessing the online world. “There’s very clear assistive potential for people who are older, who are generally less equipped to navigate user interfaces that were designed by people in their 20s, for people in their 20s,” says Margaret Mitchell, chief ethics scientist at Hugging Face. Agents, on the other hand, are almost trivially easy to interact with: Say what you want into your phone or computer microphone, and they’ll get it done for you. That could help not only older people who have trouble with web and mobile interfaces but also people with visual or motor disabilities. Still, some experts don’t think the human web is going anywhere. Instead, the two webs may exist in parallel: We’ll go to the human web for fun, while the agentic web is where the boring daily admin of our lives happens. For instance, Krystian Kolondra, an executive vice president at the browser company Opera, doesn’t intend to use agents for every part of travel planning, even though this is often touted as one of their central use cases. “I don’t want to get an itinerary [from an AI] who has planned me every minute for something that was supposed to be fun,” he says. “I actually prefer to sit down with my wife in the evening and then just think [about] where to go next, what to see, and then enjoy that experience.” He’s happy to fully relinquish some of the more routine parts of travel planning to agents, though: “Finding what the options are for flights? Why not?” Mollick, however, thinks that the human web could be at risk. “If agents take over the web, then the web will change,” he says. “Do we abandon the human internet entirely and move to a world of private Discords? GLP-1 weight-loss drugs are one of the biggest biotech breakthroughs of the last decade. But to some clinical psychologists, they can represent something more sinister—a potential trigger for eating disorders. A new report suggests that the global incidence of anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa shot up between 1990 and 2023—by a whopping 71%. By 2023, there were an estimated 18.1 million people living with one of these two disorders. What’s worse is that the number is likely to be much higher than that once we factor in other eating disorders. And, disturbingly, those in the field are already starting to see GLP-1 weight-loss drugs exacerbating the issue. Cheri Levinson is a clinical psychologist at the University of Louisville in Kentucky who has specialized in eating disorders for almost 20 years. She says she’s now seeing more patients with more severe symptoms than at any other time in her career, and that many of them are misusing GLP-1s. “This is the worst I’ve seen things,” she says. Eating disorders are mental health conditions in which people’s eating behaviors impact their health. They include anorexia nervosa (defined by a fear of weight gain), bulimia nervosa (in which people binge and purge), binge eating disorder, and others. It’s thought that some people have a genetic predisposition to certain eating disorders, but that some external factor is what triggers their onset. That might be stress, or social pressure to be skinny. Often, simply restricting food intake can trigger an eating disorder, says Levinson. Eating disorders—along with the pressure to look thin—were already prevalent in the 1990s (the heroin chic era springs to mind). To find out how things might have changed since then, Ruhai Bai at the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University in China and his colleagues pulled together data from studies and surveys conducted on populations around the world. The team used statistical models to fill in any data gaps. That research allowed the researchers to estimate the annual incidence of two eating disorders—anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa—for each of 204 countries and territories between 1990 and 2023. They also estimated the impact those disorders had on global health by calculating their disability-adjusted life years (one DALY represents a year of healthy life lost). The findings, published in JAMA Psychiatry on Wednesday, are unsettling. Between 1990 and 2023, there was a 71% increase in the incidence of those two eating disorders, the team found. Those conditions had a huge impact on global health, too. In 2023, they “cost the world the equivalent of about 3.83 million years of healthy life,” says Bai. Bai and his colleagues expect these figures to continue to rise. In 2023, an estimated 18.1 million people had either anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa; by 2050, the team predict, the two conditions could affect 21.8 million people. It gets worse. Those two conditions represent less than half of all eating disorders, says Levinson, who was not involved in Bai’s study. The true impact of these disorders is “probably three times the rate that they’re reporting,” she says. Bai and his colleagues didn’t explore the reasons for this rise (although Bai points out that it can’t be entirely explained by population growth). So how did we get here? Social media may have played a role. “Social media comparisons, influencers, challenges, diet trends can all be precipitating … events for an eating disorder,” says Cynthia Bulik, a clinical psychologist at the University of North Carolina, who was not involved in the new study. Bulik has been studying and treating eating disorders since the early 1980s. “Between 1990 and 2023 there has clearly been an upsurge of those types of environmental exposures,” she says. Many social media users are exposed to filtered and modified images of skinny influencers who may have undergone cosmetic surgery or extreme dieting and who are promoting “thinspiration” or “fitspiration” content. But users with eating disorders appear to be more likely to engage with this content—and to be fed more of it as a result. When researchers at the University of Melbourne investigated this phenomenon a couple of years ago, they found that the algorithms of TikTok users with eating disorders delivered 4343% more “toxic eating disorder videos” than those of users who didn’t have the disorders. They also got more content focused on dieting or exercising. Many were fed over 1000 problematic videos per month. Bulik describes social media as “quicksand” for people with, or at risk of, eating disorders. The covid-19 pandemic—and the social isolation that accompanied lockdowns—also appears to have contributed to the rise in eating disorders. Research conducted in 2021 found that the number of people hospitalized with eating disorders in the US doubled between 2018 and 2020. Researchers don’t yet have good data on how trends in eating disorders might have progressed since 2023. But anecdotally, things seem to be getting even worse. “There are more eating disorders, more severe eating disorders… and we’re seeing earlier ages of onset,” says Levinson. “We’re seeing eight- and nine-year-olds with eating disorders.” “From what I’m hearing and seeing, I am not going to be surprised if that number isn’t at least doubling again,” she adds. Levinson particularly worries about the impact of GLP-1 weight-loss drugs, which have exploded in popularity in recent years. A Gallup survey conducted earlier this year suggested that 15% of Americans had used one of these drugs for weight loss. And they’re becoming more accessible—drug companies are working on new formulations that are both easier to take and more effective, and many people can buy versions of these drugs from compound pharmacies or dubious online sources. In a small study published earlier this year, Levinson and her colleagues found that 30% of people with eating disorders had used GLP-1 drugs. The drugs might benefit some of those people. But 10% appeared to have misused the drugs. And Levinson thinks that figure is also an underestimate. Levinson says she and her colleagues have seen children develop eating disorders after being prescribed GLP-1s for medical reasons. They’ve seen others—even children as young as 11—buy versions of these drugs over TikTok. “We’re very concerned that GLPs will lead to even faster and more extreme weight loss in people with anorexia nervosa, and that patients will come to us in even more advanced stages of illness that become difficult to reverse,” says Bulik. Both Bulik and Levinson are continuing to study the impacts of these drugs in eating disorders. In the meantime, both urge medical professionals to consider the risks GLP-1s might pose to people with those disorders. Bulik contributed to a set of research and clinical guidelines, published earlier this month, that recommends doctors screen for eating disorders before prescribing the drugs, for example. The guidelines also recommend more patient education and more robust monitoring of people who take GLP-1s.
