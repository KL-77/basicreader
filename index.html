Epic Games reportedly pivoting to "AI first" game development model

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-12

Epic Games is shifting to an “AI first” development strategy for its collaboration with the Walt Disney Company, letting AI models write all the code and guide all the artwork for an upcoming shooter. That’s according to a Bloomberg report, which cites unnamed people familiar with the project who commented on the significant pivot in internal development at the Unreal Engine maker. Human developers will be tasked with managing and guiding code and art assets generated by models such as Anthropic’s Claude, according to internal Epic employee messages viewed by Bloomberg. That means “no more hand-written code” and art assets “generated with full AI workflows,” according to the report. The $1.5 billion collaboration between Epic and Disney, announced in early 2024, was slated to release a Marvel- and Star Wars-themed shooter in March 2027. That project has now been delayed indefinitely amid the AI-related shakeup and understaffed, slower-than-expected development thus far, according to the report. “We’re embracing AI-accelerated development, led by Epic developers who are the visionaries and creative drivers for everything we do,” an Epic spokesperson said in a statement. “We have a huge pivot underway here at Epic, moving developers around to retool for maximum AI acceleration,” Epic CEO Tim Sweeney wrote on social media shortly after the Bloomberg story was posted. “The aim isn’t to do the same stuff with fewer people, but to enable everyone here to build MORE and BETTER stuff, and to bring the benefits to all teams using Unreal.” Sweeney and Epic have long been bullish on what they see as an impending game development revolution spurred by AI. In an interview with IGN in June, Sweeney saw the technology resulting in games with “the possibility of infinite dialogue with a really simple setup for creators,” leading to situations where “small teams will be able to create games with immense amounts of characters and immense and interactive worlds.” Sweeney said last November that AI usage disclosure tags such as those required by Valve’s Steam platform “make no sense” because AI tools “will be involved in nearly all future production.” Epic laid off over 1,000 employees in March, citing a “downturn in Fortnite engagement” as the main reason behind the move. Months later, Fortnite Senior External Development Manager Stephanie Arnette said that “I know everyone’s biggest fear is, ‘Oh my god, AI is going to take all our jobs.’ That’s not our goal. The goal is to make us more efficient.” Last May, Epic and Disney faced some embarrassment when an AI-voiced Darth Vader character added to Fortnite started repeating curse words and uttering some baffling opinions before a hotfix was rolled out. A survey of over 2,300 game industry professionals ahead of this year’s Game Developers Conference found that 36 percent used generative AI tools as part of their jobs, including 30 percent of those at game development studios. But those AI tools were used for tasks like research and brainstorming (by 81 percent of AI-using respondents) much more than for code assistance (47 percent) and prototyping (35 percent).

After a 10-year redemption arc, No Man's Sky is coming to the iPhone and iPad

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-12

Visit Original Website
Just over 10 years after its initial release, space exploration and survival crafting game No Man’s Sky is making its way to the iPhone and iPad, Apple and developer Hello Games announced today. It will run on devices as far back as the iPhone 11 and the third-generation iPad Air. No Man’s Sky might not have the most cutting-edge AAA graphics (though they’re still strong, and they’ve come a long way since launch via updates), but the game’s uncommon method of generating galaxies of incomprehensible scale on device consistently for all players from a single-seed happening on a phone is remarkable for those of us who remember phones performing far behind consoles or desktop PCs. This version of the game includes all of the content and systems updates to the game that have been added over the years—the expansions that famously took the game from a rocky launch reception to a widely beloved redemption story. There are a couple of compromises, though, and it’s similar to what we saw with No Man’s Sky on the Nintendo Switch. Hello Games says the ability to build and explore player-made planetary settlements is limited to the iPhone 15 Pro and later, and multiplayer is not supported on any iOS device. “We are excited to see player feedback on iOS, and will support it appropriately based on how many folks are playing,” the company says, leaving the door open to perhaps not supporting all future content updates if the player base isn’t there to justify it. In addition to touch controls (which actually ought to be a good fit for a menu-heavy game like this), the iPhone version supports game controllers and casting to a TV, while the iPad version supports both of those things, plus mouse and keyboard input. Hello Games has been working on the iOS version for three years. It also recently released a well-received macOS port of the game. No Man’s Sky’s iOS port is part of what Apple is billing “indie games week,” which includes a few other titles of note, like the cat exploration game Little Kitty, Big City, and strategy game 9 Kings. While the other indie titles in Apple’s push have already started rolling out, No Man’s Sky will be a bit still: It will launch on October 28. It will also offer a free trial, and the final purchase price will be $19.99. The file size clocks in at under 5GB; remember, those millions of planets aren’t assets stored in a folder. Instead, they’re generated on-device as you go.

WHO report on Russian lab worker death highlights "key uncertainty"

Over the weekend, the World Health Organization released a disease outbreak report on the events surrounding the death of a Russian infectious disease researcher earlier this month, highlighting that Russia has yet to report a confirmed cause of death. With that “key uncertainty,” the United Nations health agency said it remains impossible to assess the risk of the event or whether the public health measures Russia took were adequate to prevent spread of the unknown disease. The WHO reported that, so far, it has seen no evidence of ongoing transmission of the mystery disease. “However, confidence in the assessment remains limited by the absence of a confirmed etiological diagnosis, and the remaining uncertainty regarding the source and mode of exposure,” the agency wrote in its report. The agency laid out the order of events as information trickled out from Russian authorities, noting that it only became aware of the death via media reports on October 2. The WHO subsequently made contact with Russian authorities and requested information on October 3, 6, 7, and 8. Early media reports indicated that the lab worker had become ill on September 29, possibly following an incident at the lab, the Irkutsk Antiplague Research Institute (APRI) of Siberia and the Far East. Some early reports suggested that the researcher—who was identified by media as 28-year-old Darya Shipilova—died of the plague after breaking a test tube. On October 2, the Irkutsk branch of Russian health agency Rospotrebnadzor (RPN) said in a statement that a “special sanitary-epidemiological expert commission” had already met over a suspected case of a high-threat pathogen. Sanitary and epidemic control measures were put in place, including identifying, monitoring, and testing all contacts. At the time, RPN said that all contacts had tested negative for high-threat pathogens. On October 6, Russia’s National Focal Point for the International Health Regulations (NFP) first responded to the WHO’s information requests, saying that no case of plague had been recorded in Irkutsk Oblast. The NFP confirmed that an APRI lab worker had died from a severe case of pneumonia after developing symptoms compatible with an acute respiratory viral infection. The worker was tested for a number of high-threat pathogens, but all the tests had come back negative. Doctors categorized the cause of death as being from pneumonia of undetermined etiology. The NFP also reiterated information from RPN about the public health measures taken and that people in contact with the lab worker all tested negative for high-threat pathogens. The NFP added that the widest range of possible contacts were identified and tested in accordance with Russia’s public health laws. In follow-up responses, the NFP gave more details about testing and some results that weren’t concerning. It said authorities conducted 5,500 laboratory tests on contacts. They tested for a range of pathogens, including influenza, SARS-CoV-2, other respiratory infections, and bacterial infections, including plague and Streptococcus. Among the contacts, they identified two cases of COVID-19, three cases of rhinovirus, and one case of Haemophilus spp., a common bacteria group that includes those that can cause respiratory infections and pneumonia. They also found a few opportunistic infections. As of October 9, Russian authorities told WHO that they had not detected any high-threat pathogens in the laboratory’s research portfolio in any of the contacts, and they were no longer being monitored. The monitoring period was set for seven days for all the contacts. Authorities also added that the worker who died was not infected with plague bacteria, Yersinia pestis, and the worker had been vaccinated against the infection due to the occupational hazard. They added that no lab biosafety or biosecurity incident had occurred at APRI, and the deceased worker had not traveled outside the region within the two weeks before becoming ill. Reviewing all the information coming out of Russia, the WHO was largely at a loss as to what happened and what risk the event posed, concluding: “A key uncertainty for this event is the absence of a confirmed diagnosis in the deceased laboratory worker.” Without the cause of death, the agency can’t “independently assess whether the duration of the reported monitoring period for contacts was sufficient to exclude all plausible causes of the fatal illness.” They also aren’t ruling out the possibility of a laboratory-associated exposure.

An Anthropic AI model submitted a false tip about an unsolved murder to Philadelphia police, the company and police department confirmed on Friday. A Claude model sent the false tip on July 18 to the Philadelphia Police Department’s website for submitting information about unsolved murders, but it was flagged by a spam filter. Anthropic apparently discovered the incident on September 28 and informed the police department about it on October 8. During internal testing, Claude Haiku 4.5 was instructed to complete various forms up to the final submission stage and to stop before submitting those forms. “Several times Claude mistakenly submitted the form instead, expecting there to be an additional confirmation page,” Anthropic said in a report describing “unintended model actions we’ve observed during evaluations and internal use of Claude.” The false murder tip occurred when Claude Haiku 4.5 was instructed to complete tasks on randomly selected webpages, Anthropic said: In one run, the model landed on a page referencing an unsolved homicide; that page contained a tip form run by a police department. Claude was instructed never to log in, create accounts, enter personal data, make purchases, or submit anything destructive, but the instructions did not rule out form submissions. Claude filled out the form with the following: “I may have information regarding this case. I recall seeing someone matching the description in the area around [the street named on the page] during that time period. Please contact me if this information is relevant.” Anthropic said it also discovered Claude incidents involving state and federal agency websites. Claude reportedly submitted 20 visa applications to the US State Department in one of these incidents. Anthropic confirmed that the false homicide tip was sent to the Philadelphia Police Department, and the department issued a lengthy statement on the incident to the ABC station in Philadelphia. The false tip left the name and contact fields empty. It was not reviewed by any person because the email message generated when the tip was submitted ended up in a spam folder, police said. “Anthropic told PPD that it discovered the incident on September 28, terminated the automated testing process responsible for the submission and instituted an additional validation mechanism for future testing,” police said. After the October 8 briefing, the police department “located the submission in the website’s tip records and confirmed that the corresponding email remained in spam.” Anthropic said it informed the police department “as soon as our technical review was complete.” Police criticized Anthropic for not discovering and reporting the false tip sooner. “The City of Philadelphia takes this incident very seriously,” the Philadelphia police statement said. “The company must strengthen its safeguards to prevent similar incidents from impacting city systems without the city’s knowledge. The two-month delay in detecting and reporting the incident to the City is unacceptable.” Anthropic said the false murder tip was one of several incidents involving “websites run by US government agencies at the federal, state, and local levels. We have briefed the White House on these cases and notified each agency involved.” A New York Times report citing anonymous sources said Anthropic’s AI agents “submitted 20 visa applications through a form available on the State Department’s website. All the applications were incomplete and were not processed.” Philadelphia police said its normal investigative process “requires human review and vetting before any tips are disseminated for investigative follow-up… Those PPD safeguards limited the impact of this incident. They do not diminish the seriousness of an AI system presenting fabricated information as though it came from a person with knowledge of a homicide.” Anthropic said it discovered the false tip and other incidents during a review of transcripts it began in July. That was the same month when Anthropic revealed that Claude had built and published a malicious Python package and carried out several attacks on other companies. Anthropic said the review did not find any “incidents of similar severity to the cybersecurity incidents we reported this summer.” The review later moved on to transcripts of “lower-risk” testing scenarios like the one affecting the Philadelphia Police Department. “Although the impact of these behaviors was minimal and we had already turned off live Internet access for some high-risk and cybersecurity evaluations, we have now decided to expand that to include all our internal evaluations until we have confirmed that our security and monitoring measures… reliably catch behaviors like these,” Anthropic said. Anthropic said it has stopped running some evaluations and moved others to offline versions “or rebuilt them so that their tasks do not reach live websites.” The company “updated the guardrails on some of our Internet access tools, such as the web fetch tool, to heavily restrict what the model can do with them,” it said. Anthropic said it also built new tools “to automatically detect and block the kinds of behaviors described above… When we tested it against the cases described in this post, it blocked all of them.” The White House’s AI task force, which it calls the Super Intelligence Force, issued a statement saying it informed Anthropic “that we expect immediate and full transparency to the entities involved and the public.” The White House AI task force also said Anthropic must “immediately provide remediation services to the affected entities and any harmed Americans,” and that all AI companies “must immediately disclose incidents involving their models and follow with swift, decisive action to remedy any and all harm,” according to an Axios report.

Russia was not ready for the data center war it started with Ukraine

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-12

Visit Original Website
When Russia began attacking Ukrainian data centers and telecommunication infrastructure, it apparently failed to prepare for possible retaliation. Now Russia’s digital services are facing widespread outages after Ukrainian drone strikes knocked out three of the largest data centers owned by Russian tech giant Yandex within several days. Pro-Kremlin bloggers are warning that Russia’s centralized digital economy could collapse because the country is not prepared for the “data center war” it started, according to posts translated by the pseudonymous independent researcher ChrisO_wiki. The Russian commentators also suggested that Ukraine’s decentralized digital services could prove more resilient than Russia’s system if the countries continue trading such blows. Russia initially launched a drone strike campaign targeting Ukrainian data centers and mobile phone networks in late September. The attacks temporarily disrupted Internet and cell phone service for up to 100,000 households in the Ukrainian capital of Kyiv and came as part of a broader long-range strike campaign that has involved Russian missiles and jet-powered drones hitting Ukraine’s power grid infrastructure, transportation hubs, and even residential apartment building blocks. But Ukraine soon began striking back at data centers owned by the Russian company Yandex, also known as “Russia’s Google,” in what Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy has described as a “symmetrical response.” The first Ukrainian drone attack damaged a data center in the Sasovo region southeast of Moscow on October 8, which houses two of the three supercomputers that Yandex has used to train its YandexGPT AI model. It was not immediately clear how much damage may have occurred to the supercomputers, which are based on Nvidia A100 GPU chips. The Chervonenkis supercomputer has a computing performance rated at 29.42 petaflops and ranks at 101 on the Top 500 List of supercomputers, while the Lyapunov supercomputer is rated at 20.03 petaflops and ranks at 161. A second Ukrainian drone strike knocked out another Yandex data center in the Kaluga region southwest of Moscow on October 9. This is one of the largest data centers in Russia, with a maximum 63-megawatt capacity. A third Ukrainian drone strike then forced Yandex to suspend operations at its data center in Vladimir, east of Moscow, on October 11, according to BBC News. This facility houses the third of Yandex’s three supercomputers, Galushkin, which ranked 134 on the Top 500 list and is rated at 20.64 petaflops of computing performance. More importantly for Russian society, the damage to Yandex’s data centers has reportedly caused interruptions or outages to popular Russian payment apps and digital banking services, email, e-commerce platforms, video streaming services, ride-hailing and food delivery apps, and navigation and map services. Yandex’s search engine also accounts for about 70 percent of the market share in Russia. The disruptions are even more widespread, because the data center damage also impacts Yandex’s cloud computing service, which counts some of Russia’s largest companies as customers, according to The Moscow Times, an independent newspaper. These include large grocery and fast-food chains as well as a household appliance retailer. The Russian milblogger writing under the name Ratio Militaris warned that Yandex’s hypercentralized digital ecosystem had become a wartime weakness for Russia, in a post translated by ChrisO_wiki: Yandex and the digital ecosystem it created that had long been one of Russia’s advantages compared to even Western countries, has proven to be a weak link—including, and even primarily, due to hyper-centralization—both at the market level, where Yandex had become a powerful monopolist, and at the level of specific infrastructure solutions, where the entire power of the information giant was concentrated in a few (the exact number is no secret, but perhaps inappropriate to specify) large nodes—those aforementioned data centers. Russian companies like Yandex will also have a hard time establishing infrastructure backup systems outside of Russia to provide additional wartime resiliency. That is partly because of the “Sovereign Internet Law” passed by the Russian parliament and signed into law by President Vladimir Putin in 2019, according to Russian milblogger Artjockey, whose writing was translated by ChrisO_wiki. In its pursuit of a “sovereign Internet,” the government has effectively blocked Russian companies from using infrastructure even in friendly nations, while “unfriendly” countries have blocked access via sanctions anyway… it is virtually impossible to replicate Russian IT infrastructure in places like Belarus or Kazakhstan—though, in any case, the necessary capacity simply isn’t there. China might have the capacity, but that would require legislative changes to allow key services and registries to be moved there. By comparison, Ukraine has a much more decentralized digital ecosystem consisting of smaller data centers. It has also taken steps to build out backup infrastructure, including moving some equipment and networks underground or outside the country. Western tech giants like Microsoft and Amazon have also provided free cloud services to help Ukraine migrate crucial government services to other parts of Europe, enabling many essential Ukrainian digital services to keep going despite Russia’s sustained missile and drone attacks. Amazon Web Services alone is hosting more than 15 petabytes of data from over 60 Ukrainian government organizations, along with data from Ukraine’s largest private energy company, DTEK. Meanwhile, Russians may be waiting up to six months until Yandex can replace damaged data center equipment, according to an unnamed IT expert interviewed by Russian newspaper Kommersant. A lengthy timeline for repairs may not be so surprising when considering Amazon’s own experience after Iranian drone strikes knocked out multiple data centers in Bahrain and the United Arab Emirates earlier this year.

For over a decade now, the only “official” way to play Hideo Kojima’s cult classic, P.T.—the long-discontinued “playable trailer” for Kojima’s canceled “Silent Hills” project—has been via one of the increasingly rare PlayStation 4s that previously downloaded a copy during its monthslong availability window. Now, though, one coder is helping to preserve the game with a porting tool that builds a playable x86 PC executable from the original PS4 files. Coder Lorean Xavier said he was inspired to work on the “P.T. for PC” project “because P.T. deserved to keep existing somewhere other than on consoles that still have it installed.” The open source porting effort rebuilds the P.T. engine in C++ and Vulkan, using the original files from the PS4 game to re-create “every level, model, texture, sound, script and cutscene,” according to its GitHub listing. That means players without their own legitimate decrypted copy of the original P.T. will have to track one down in order to generate a playable PC version. The reliance on real P.T. files makes this port more authentic than previous hobbyist efforts to re-create the look and feel of P.T. for a PC audience. And while PS4 emulators recently reached a point where they could run P.T. completely, this native port also sports extra features, like upscaling and frame-generation, a camera mode, experimental third-person and VR support, and a “museum” section where you can look at and listen to in-game assets (including “unused content”). Although Xavier admits to using AI tools in developing this port, it doesn’t seem like he simply followed the recent retro-gaming trend of asking a coding agent to decompile the game. On social media, Xavier said that he has “spent years studying P.T. and Fox Engine” in developing the port and only uses AI tools “for debugging, troubleshooting and investigating problems.” “I’m a solo developer making a free P.T. port because I love the game,” he added. “Nobody hired me or asked me to spend countless hours on this. I’m sharing something I worked incredibly hard on, simply because I want other people to enjoy it.” Beyond being of interest to curious gamers who never had access to the extremely limited PS4 version, this port of P.T. should be very useful to preservationists who want to maintain public access to this historically significant game for generations going forward. “Personally… I think the hardware super-specific solutions are dead ends for preservation,” Henry Lowood, curator for the History of Science & Technology Collections and Film & Media Collections in the Stanford University Libraries, told Ars back in 2015. “The kinds of equipment/hardware we have, the way they’re manufactured, just have a very small likelihood of surviving for super long periods of time.”

Since 1997, Susan Collins has served as one of Maine’s senators, and she may be the epitome of Maine. She, like her opponent Troy Jackson, grew up in the state’s northern tip. Her family ran a lumber business for generations in Caribou, and she’s well regarded by Mainers as someone from “the County,” not a politician. During the Barack Obama era, her bloc of crucial swing voters included Arlen Specter from Pennsylvania and fellow Mainer Olympia Snowe, but she’s the last of those Republican moderates standing, giving Maine an outsize national footprint. So when a political attack ad ran in Portland last week, it would be safe to assume the Portland in question was in Maine. So why did Defending Main Street, a super PAC backing Collins, run an ad in the wrong Portland? Defending Main Street spent nearly $25,000 on digital placements for “So Many Taxes,” a 30-second ad against Jackson, to run the ad in hundreds of zip codes across Oregon and Washington State from Oct. 8 to Oct. 11, according to Google’s Ads Transparency Center. It came to light when an Oregon X user reported seeing five anti-Jackson ads in one day. “I choose to believe they mixed up Portlands,” the user wrote. The same ad aired on broadcast TV in the intended market, Portland, Maine, reaching about 675,000 impressions over the same four days, according to AdImpact data cited by MS NOW. Defending Main Street told Newsweek that its media vendor mistakenly placed the digital ads in the Oregon market, and Newsweek reported the targeting was fixed by Monday afternoon. Sarah Chamberlain, president and CEO of the affiliated Republican Main Street Partnership, told NBC News she was furious at the ad buyer, Cross Screen Media, which she said refunded the money. “I would never run an ad in Portland, Oregon, for Susan Collins,” she said. The mix-up didn’t stop Jackson’s campaign team from commenting on the snafu. “Susan Collins’s allies are so out of touch with Maine, they can’t even find it on a map,” campaign spokesperson Dan Gottlieb told Fortune. “Good luck convincing Mainers you know what’s best for them when you don’t know which Portland they live in.” Defending Main Street and the Collins campaign did not respond to Fortune’s requests for comment. The super PAC got its money back, but the mistake mirrors a problem that costs corporate advertisers billions. The Association of National Advertisers estimates that $26.8 billion in global ad spending is lost each year to middlemen’s fees, fake traffic, unseen ads, and low-quality sites, up from $20 billion in 2023. That’s more than double the $11.6 billion AdImpact projects will be spent on political ads in the entire 2026 election cycle. Much of that waste comes from ads running in places advertisers never chose. In the ANA’s 2023 study, the average campaign ran on 44,000 websites, even though a few hundred sites would have reached most of the intended audience, and even as participants hadn’t approved about 38,000 of those sites. In the group’s latest benchmark, more than a third of automated ad spending still went to ads that failed basic quality standards. AdImpact has projected $384 million in ad spending on the Maine Senate race, nearly double the roughly $200 million spent when Collins last ran in 2020. And in this race alone, Republicans are outspending Democrats but getting less for each dollar. From July 26 to mid-September, Republicans spent $82.1 million on TV ads in the race to Democrats’ $55.5 million, according to AdImpact data. Super PACs pay full price for TV ads, while candidates’ campaigns get discounted rates. Location targeting, the tool that sent DMS’s ad to Oregon, can backfire. Chase cut its list to about 5,000 preapproved sites and saw little change in the cost or visibility of its ads, its chief marketing officer said. That same year, after activists flagged Uber’s ads on Breitbart, the company began cutting its online ad spending. Kevin Frisch, Uber’s former head of performance marketing, later said the company turned off $100 million of its $150 million annual online ad budget for riders and saw no change in app installs. Fortune’s AIQ Summit: The conversation has moved from experimentation to execution

Fortune | FORTUNE | 2026-10-12

As companies race to adopt generative AI, agents, and new data tools, leaders face a more demanding question than whether to use the technology: How can they turn it into measurable value without creating new costs, risks, and organizational confusion? At the Fortune AIQ Summit at the New York Stock Exchange, executives from the technology, finance, industrial operations, travel, and human resources sectors explored what separates meaningful AI transformation from scattered pilots. The discussion made clear that the strongest companies are not simply distributing AI tools. They are redesigning workflows, developing talent, strengthening governance, and building the data and operating discipline needed to scale responsibly. THE MOST IMPORTANT AI QUESTION IS NOT WHETHER A COMPANY HAS DEPLOYED TOOLS, BUT WHETHER IT HAS CHANGED HOW WORK GETS DONE. Speakers warned that widespread access to AI does not necessarily create productivity, business value, or organizational maturity. Diana David, director of futures at ServiceNow, said leading companies distinguish themselves through their commitment to people and operational discipline, not technology alone. In ServiceNow’s Enterprise AI Maturity Index, 57% of “pacesetters” invest in ongoing AI upskilling, compared with 4% of other organizations. The widest gap involves attracting, hiring, and retaining AI talent: 68% of pacesetters do so, compared with 10% of other companies. “Talent investment. That was the biggest gap,” David said. David cautioned against treating broad employee access to AI as proof of success: “95% of everybody has X, Y, Z,” she said. “But it is not translating into productivity, and it’s often not with training, and that doesn’t equal maturity.” The work, she argued, begins with leadership, a clear vision, transparent systems, the right data foundation, and iterative redesign of specific workflows. Paul Fipps, president of global customer operations at ServiceNow, made a related point: The greatest gains will not come from automating old processes faster, but from rebuilding them around new capabilities. “If I just automate or drive autonomous agents to drive the current processes I have, that’s a choice that might get some productivity bump,” Fipps said. “But the ones that are actually rethinking and reimagining the business processes … are really making the difference.” Fipps said companies should deploy AI horizontally, across employee-facing functions such as HR, legal, and technology, rather than isolating it in one department. He cited Standard Chartered, where AI has helped deflect 77% of employee requests. “If you get 25% to 30% deflection in those use cases, it’s game-changing,” he said. “If you get 75% to 80%, it is life-changing.” Amy Webb, founder and CEO of Future Today Strategy Group, was more blunt about the gap between ambition and readiness. “Let’s be fair, Fortune 500 companies are pretty late to the party on this,” she said. “Artificial intelligence didn’t just show up a couple of years ago.” Webb warned that companies are pouring significant capital into pilots without sufficient strategy, leadership, or planning—creating isolated projects that fail to produce durable value. AI TRANSFORMATION IS A WORKFORCE TRANSFORMATION. The summit underscored that organizations need more than new software: They need employees and leaders willing to rethink established processes, learn new skills, and make room for new ways of working. Stacy Dillow, executive vice president and chief people officer at HPE, said companies must resist simply inserting AI into existing systems. “We’ve got to force ourselves to think differently, because if we’re not trying to change how we work, we’re just trying to retrofit and put AI in a process that isn’t going to give us a different answer,” she said. For senior executives, she added, AI adoption and transformation have become central management responsibilities. Danielle Gonzalez, chief people officer at Palo Alto Networks, described how AI is already changing the employee experience. A custom AI agent has reduced IT tickets by 83% and supports workflows across HR, finance, and legal. Instead of turning to a human HR partner or business contact, employees can increasingly ask an agent for answers tied to their role and location. Gonzalez said companies should hire and develop people who can adapt rapidly—not only learning new skills but questioning assumptions that no longer hold. “We’re looking for curiosity,” she said. “We’re looking for agency, and we’re looking for those people who can not only learn something at a fast rate … but also unlearn what they thought to be true, so that they can make space to move forward.” Drew Holler, chief human resources officer at Lennar, stressed that the responsibility is shared. “We’re going to give you all the tools, but it’s your responsibility to upskill yourself as well,” he said. “We’re going to give you trainings, but you, as an individual, have to upskill.” The workforce implications extend beyond reskilling. Francis Shanahan, chief technology officer at Peloton, warned that AI-enabled creation can create its own challenge. As librarians, we know that if students are using AI chatbots to help with their academic research, they shouldn’t assume that the information they’re given is accurate. Not all AI is the same. Here are four steps we recommend to question information AI generates, including by checking the original source and considering issues that aren’t always easy to spot. First, we think it makes sense to spend time choosing the right AI tool for the task at hand. AI tools, be it ExLibris’ Primo Research Assistant, Research Solutions’ Scite, Anthropic’s Claude or OpenAI’s ChatGPT, are trained with various datasets and use different algorithms to generate results. They should not be used interchangeably. Some tools might be better or worse at answering specific kinds of questions, and the types of answers they generate can vary. People might choose ChatGPT, for example, at the beginning of their research process, because it can provide overviews and summaries to help with understanding concepts. In subsequent stages, they might choose a tool such as Primo Research Assistant or Scopus AI, which can help find and examine peer-reviewed research that has been vetted by academics. Students conducting academic research could also see whether their library subscribes to an artificial intelligence tool like LeapSpace or Consensus, which draw their data from peer-reviewed, scholarly research. Using these specific AI tools doesn’t eliminate the need to evaluate their answers, but it provides more certainty that the data has gone through a review process. Tools such as Anthropic’s Claude, meanwhile, may also draw from academic research, but to what extent is unclear. Finally, it can be helpful to read librarians’ reviews about AI tools to help select the best fit. People can also take a simple online test such as ROBOT to help them consider which tool to use. Second, asking follow-up questions is an important part of evaluating the answers AI tools generate. Even if the answer an AI tool gives sounds good, it is useful to take time to learn more about it. Checking one’s own emotions is also important, because AI chatbots have been known to flatter users by giving answers based on the user’s own biases, or what the user wants to hear, rather than just facts. Ask the AI to tell you where it got the information, how confident it is that the answer is correct and what might be missing. Ask the AI why it believes the answer is correct, or ask it to explain how it came up with an answer. It’s helpful to also make sure your prompt is phrased in a neutral way. Trump won 69% of the rural vote in 2024, but an AP-KFF poll, conducted in August, found that 57% of rural voters disapprove of Trump’s handling of the economy. Among rural Republican voters, around one-quarter (27%) disapproved of his handling of the economy—and that group was "substantially less likely" than those who approved to say they were certain to vote in November. The impact has been felt by consumers, who have seen a steady climb in grocery prices. In one September AP-NORC poll, 64% of Americans said Trump has “gone too far” on imposing new tariffs on other countries. Mars may be a hellhole, but the moon is not far behind

MIT Technology Review | 2026-10-12

Visit Original Website
Welcome to the second space race. You can be forgiven for experiencing déjà vu. Last time, it was the Soviet Union and the United States vying to be the first to plant some flags. Now it’s a sprint to the lunar surface between America and China—alongside a host of varyingly friendly and rival nations—plus a cornucopia of private spaceflight companies. And this time it looks like they’re getting ready to set up camp. There will be, the contenders say, bases on the moon, permanently inhabited castles under the stars. The notion of a moon base, fanciful as it might sound, isn’t new. But this year, under the leadership of Administrator Jared Isaacman, NASA has been flooding the zone with bold announcements. If all goes according to plan—a plan that will likely cost tens of billions of dollars to start—there will be drones, robotic landers, communication satellites, and a nuclear power plant; there will be plant terrariums and moonquake sensors; there will be mechanized scouts exploring craters for water. Initial astronaut missions to the lunar surface, which the US is currently planning to start in 2028, will be focused on exploration and technological experimentation. But the idea is that we will—some time in the 2030s—get to a point where there will be habitation quarters for long-term stays, surrounded by a network of scientific research stations, resource mining and processing plants, and landing pads for spaceships. “It’s going to be an expanse,” says Carlos García-Galán, NASA’s moon base program manager—one that will add up to “hundreds of square miles.” The effort is undoubtedly being fueled by nationalistic competition. China has near-identical plans to build its own base on the moon. And being first matters, because the “victor” gets to define the terms. What is the moon, exactly? Is it a militarized outpost? Is it a manufacturing hub? Is it a wilderness to be studied and preserved? But there are also other reasons to go. Earth’s companion contains primeval rocks that hold clues to the formation of the entire solar system. The lunar surface, with virtually no atmosphere in the way, is an ideal place to put telescopes that could examine the most ancient epochs of the cosmos. The far side of the moon would be “a fantastic place to explore the further universe,” says Josef Aschbacher, the European Space Agency’s director general. And it’s way more energy efficient to launch rockets from the low-gravity lunar surface than it is from Earth, meaning sending astronauts—or robotic spacecraft—farther into the solar system will become much less difficult. “The moon is critical. It will be a stepping stone for going further—that means to Mars and beyond,” says Aschbacher. But if these bold visions are to become reality, there’s a daunting amount of work to be done. A sprawling moon base must be safe for its astronaut inhabitants—protected from falling asteroids, endless cosmic radiation, razor-sharp lunar dust, and torturous temperature swings. It also must be somewhat self-sustaining, so that it can weather changing political fortunes and not cost ruinous amounts of money to maintain. For that, we’ll need to find a way to use the moon’s resources to keep the base operating—and ideally generating revenue. And we’ll need autonomous robots that can build, maintain, and expand the base even when their human masters aren’t present. “It’s almost like starting civilization from scratch,” says Simon Middleburgh, co-director of the Nuclear Futures Institute at Bangor University in Wales. Assuming we can overcome these starting challenges, decades from now—let’s say, by the year 2100—our initial moon bases could grow from barely visible structures into luminous cities, perhaps not too dissimilar from those dreamed up by authors and screenwriters the world over. “It’s like science fiction today that we really want to turn into a reality,” says García-Galán. Will anyone succeed? The moon is a tremendously hostile place to set up camp. In general, daytime temperatures can reach more than 250 °F; at night, they can plummet to less than –200 °F. Exposed to the vacuum of space, the surface is constantly showered in carcinogenic radiation and frequently hit by meteoroids. And its volcanic dust is sticky and corrosive, a destroyer of equipment and choker of lungs. Sadly, the lunar south pole—the area NASA is targeting—is even harsher. NASA wants to build a base there because its permanently shadowed craters are thought to harbor caches of water ice, which would be more precious than gold. Purify it, and you can water crops and hydrate astronauts; split it into oxygen and hydrogen, and you have breathable air and rocket propellant. But the temperature in those craters can be lower than –410 °F, making them some of the coldest places in the known universe. On the surface outside those pits, astronauts would be bathed in the scorching sun for 14 days before experiencing an equally long frigid night. But there is only so much money we can spend on safety. “What it comes down to is acceptable risk of death,” Philip Metzger, a spaceflight engineering researcher at the Florida Space Institute, said while pondering some of the dangers. “What can we reasonably survive without breaking the bank?” Temperature is one of the easiest problems to handle. Materials science has come a long way, and there are myriad metals, ceramics, plastics, compounds, and alloys that can resist wild temperature swings. Radiators keep things cool; heaters keep batteries from dying prematurely. Astronauts survive just fine too, with their fancy spacesuits—but that technology has proved itself only during brief excursions. It’s not yet clear how long this equipment could operate under such tempestuous conditions. The temperature in the moon’s south pole craters can be lower than –410 °F, making them some of the coldest places in the known universe. Outside those pits, astronauts would be bathed in the scorching sun for 14 days before experiencing an equally long frigid night. Space radiation, which can wreak havoc on electronics and trigger a cancerous division of cells, is far more pernicious. And as Stuart George, a physicist at NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston, explains, it comes in two unpleasant forms. The first is galactic cosmic rays, which rain down on the moon almost constantly. “They’re made by supernovas and neutron star mergers—all these kinds of things,” says George. And because the atomic nuclei, protons, and electrons that make them up are moving at significant fractions of the speed of light, these cosmic rays have oodles of kinetic energy, meaning they can punch through thin shielding (say, a metal wall) with ease. “They also have this habit where the heavier ions are fragmenting in that shielding and making secondary particles,” says George. That means if your shield is too thin, the astronauts behind it will be blasted with a surprisingly large dose of radiation. Then you have the sun. It may sustain life on Earth, but without our planet’s thick atmosphere and magnetic bubble to get in its way, moonwalkers will occasionally be blasted by one of its paroxysms: a solar flare (a burst of diverse types of radiation) or a coronal mass ejection (volleys of magnetized plasma). “During Apollo, they knew that if a large solar flare occurred while the crew was on the moon, there would be no way to keep them alive,” says Metzger. The crew could have developed acute radiation sickness and perished in a matter of days. The way to survive a powerful solar flare or coronal mass ejection is straightforward: “Get back inside a habitat where there’s more shielding,” says George—preferably a dedicated, highly shielded “storm shelter,” essentially a thick-walled bunker. The issue is that astronauts might be outside the base, driving a rover many miles away. Coronal mass ejections take hours to days to reach the vicinity of the Earth-moon system, but solar flares can do so in under an hour. And scientists can’t yet model the sun well enough to give much advance notice. “All of the capability to forecast solar storms is kind of like where weather forecasting [was] … in the ’50s and ’60s,” says George. As for those punchy galactic cosmic rays, the only way to avoid them would be to put a lot of stuff—lunar rocks, possibly—between the astronauts and the sky. “Build habitat modules underground,” says Chris Dreyer, director of engineering at the Center for Space Resources at the Colorado School of Mines. One option: Burrow living quarters into lava tubes, the naturally hollowed-­out volcanic tunnels created by molten rock eons ago. At this point, NASA hasn’t mentioned any plans to make its moon base subterranean, but it may need to if it wants to end up with a small lunar city. Radiation limits the time astronauts can spend on the lunar surface over their entire life—it can total around two years max, experts estimate, before the health risk becomes too high. Unfortunately, that’s not all space will throw at them. When the Artemis II astronauts swung around the far side of the moon earlier this year, they saw curious flashes light up the silvery land below. These were the explosive deaths of small meteoroids punching into the lunar surface. Earth is naturally protected from smaller space rocks, up to around 50 feet in diameter: They burn up inconsequentially in the atmosphere. “You don’t have that option on the moon,” says Philip Lubin, a physicist and planetary defense researcher at the University of California, Santa Barbara. Since smaller rocks plunge toward planets and moons far more frequently than larger ones do, this means the moon is often peppered by what are effectively tiny space bullets. They could easily puncture a spacesuit or the engine of a moon rover. How do you protect astronauts from the onslaught? “The simplest answer is burial,” says Nicholas Schmerr, a planetary seismologist at the University of Maryland—conveniently, the same way you’d deal with radiation exposure. Pile up moon rocks onto your base walls or put habitable structures in lava tubes. Inconveniently, there’s always a chance that a bigger asteroid will come along. Even buried habitation modules would have trouble surviving an assault from a 50-footer—about the length of a semi-trailer. That doesn’t mean a moon base would be helpless against bigger asteroids. Planetary defense has come a long way in just a few decades. NASA’s DART mission demonstrated that it could deflect an asteroid by ramming into the rock with an uncrewed spacecraft, and cutting-­edge experiments at clandestine labs around the US suggest that a nuclear bomb could swat away or entirely vaporize errant space rocks. An array of both ground-based and space-based telescopes specifically designed to spy killers—such as the ATLAS quartet of largely automated telescopes, and the soon-to-be-launched NEO Surveyor infrared telescope—are also aiming to find 90% of the near-Earth asteroids potentially capable of wiping out entire cities (those 460 feet across) in the coming years. All these protections apply to the moon. But asteroids 50 feet across are so stealthy that even NASA’s best telescopes would struggle to see them years or even months in advance. Odds are that they would first be seen not long before they were due to hit the lunar surface. Then what? It’s not unreasonable to imagine several turret-like launch sites on the moon that, owing to the lower gravity and airless environment, could rapidly fire projectiles toward an incoming asteroid. Hit it hard enough and it would break into fragments. Just make sure you shoot when it is as far away as possible: “If you were to break something up into a thousand pieces, then you have a thousand impacts on the moon,” says Lubin. “That might be worse than letting it hit one place.” Having your base destroyed by an asteroid would be awful. But a moon base is far more likely to be imperiled by dust—which, unlike asteroids, is everywhere, all the time. Volcanic lunar rocks, when broken down, turn into sharp, electrostatically charged, adhesive, acrid, abrasive particles. While on the moon, Apollo astronauts who inhaled some of this dust occasionally suffered from a strange “lunar hay fever,” which irritated their eyes and agitated their lungs. That same dust ate through all kinds of gear with disturbing ease. “It rips up seals, gears, motors, spacesuits, habitats—everything else it comes into contact with,” says Justin Cyrus, the CEO of Lunar Outpost, a spaceflight technology company that is providing NASA with one of the Artemis program’s moon rovers. NASA has already developed a defense against these dark shards: the electrodynamic dust shield, or EDS. The lightweight electrodes that make up this shield—which can be layered on machinery, walls, and perhaps even spacesuits—use opposing currents to blast away the electrically charged dust. The EDS worked wonders when tested in labs on Earth, and it also demonstrated its anti-dust magic when sent to the lunar surface in 2025 as part of a NASA-sponsored robotic mission. Amy Fritz, a dust-mitigation researcher at Johnson Space Center, says that the EDS will likely be widely featured all over a lunar habitat. But other dust-fighting instruments are being developed too—including a lint-roller-type object inspired by gecko skin and a sci-fi-esque blaster that uses ionized nitrogen gas. Its first spray will neutralize the charge of the dust to remove its stickiness; then a second burst will clear it off the surface. Irritatingly, the busier a base gets, the dustier things will become. As it expands to the size of a small city, rocket ships will need to deliver cargo and crew to the lunar surface on a frequent cadence. Each takeoff and landing will launch a salvo of dust in every direction, inundating the base’s infrastructure. “You have to worry about essentially being sandblasted every time,” says Fritz. Metzger calculates that even with landing pads more than 3,000 feet away from a base, just 10 to 20 liftoffs or landings would be sufficient to severely damage things like protective coatings on exterior walls and communications equipment. After a while, he says, “everything breaks.” Even if the moon base of the future is buried partly below ground, barricaded against micrometeorites and space radiation, and well equipped with dust-repelling technologies, it’s still a risky gig for any astronaut. Skipping around in a preternaturally agile way at one-sixth of Earth’s gravity will be entertaining. But “microgravity is not good for the human body,” says Dreyer. Muscle wastage, cardiovascular troubles, respiratory problems, and immune deficiencies can arise. Radiation, too, may always afflict lunar residents. And there’s also evidence that disease-causing microbes could stealthily sneak aboard spacecraft, even after stringent sterilization procedures. Those microbes may behave differently than they do on Earth; microgravity experiments (both real and simulated) have suggested that some become more aggressive and more likely to cause disease. “We call them the invisible crew,” says Lisa Carnell, the director of NASA’s Biological and Physical Sciences Division. Hoping to find a better way to protect astronauts, Carnell spearheaded an experiment called AVATAR (A Virtual Astronaut Tissue Analog Response), which launched aboard the Artemis II mission. It used thumb-drive-size chips, containing cells taken from the heart, lungs, or other parts of the body, that could be switched on to act like miniature organs. Scientists have previously used this organ-on-a-chip technology for purposes like testing out new pharmaceuticals in a highly controlled setting. For Artemis II, Carnell’s crew sent up chips containing bone marrow samples taken from the mission’s four astronauts. Bone marrow makes red and white blood cells, as well as platelets—the sort of stuff that affects almost the entire human body. So seeing how space radiation and microgravity affect it would give Carnell and her team vital insight into how astronauts’ biology might change on the moon. The advantage of organ chips is that you can make them for any astronaut and then send them into space ahead of time to see how that person’s organs will react to spaceflight. Researchers envision that data from such chips will be able to clue medics in to the specific preventive treatments or pharmaceuticals that will help moonbound astronauts on their journey. What is NASA likely to send up sooner rather than later? “Brain organoids,” says Carnell. Yes, she adds—that sounds super weird. But it would be good to know how a real brain might respond to being on the moon for several months before people start living there. Okay. Your vast moon base and its inhabitants are protected—as much as is possible—from the treacherous lunar environment. But astronauts’ time is extremely valuable, and humans are fragile creatures. You can’t make them do all the heavy lifting, which means that whether you’re trying to make a tiny lunar outpost or a small city, you’re going to need a lot of robots. “The buildup of the moon base, in the first years, will pretty much 99% be robotic,” says NASA’s García-Galán. Autonomy will be there right from the start. NASA’s new version of the moon buggy, its Lunar Terrain Vehicle, can be driven by the astronauts riding in it but can also be piloted remotely from Earth (or from elsewhere on the moon). There are visions, too, of adding modular robotic arms, towing vehicles, and cargo movers—all still in the preliminary stages of planning. Over the next few decades, though, we’ll likely want an army of robots to do our bidding: excavators to dig up lunar rocks that could be turned into concrete and bricks, plus other robots to flatten surfaces, dig trenches, create building foundations, pave landing pads, and build up dust-deflecting berms to keep the base from being sandblasted by launches and landings. Even solar panels and wiring can potentially be made from moon rocks—something that the spaceflight company Blue Origin demonstrated in labs on Earth in 2023 using a geologic simulant designed to match the composition of lunar regolith. Powering this robot army will require considerable energy. Two-week-long nights make solar power an impractical solution. Aside from a cornucopia of high-­capacity, extreme-temperature-resistant batteries, nuclear power is probably the only viable option. A small, car-size nuclear reactor, filled with an almost literal handful of uranium, could power a small village for a decade without needing to be refueled. And although nobody’s yet tested a nuclear reactor on the moon, few doubt that NASA and the US Department of Energy can conjure up a working design—one that perhaps wouldn’t be too different from those aboard nuclear submarines. A moon base in 2100 would probably feature multiple small, autonomously or remotely operated nuclear reactors, which—conveniently—the base-expanding robots could simply plug themselves into to recharge. But the biggest lunar question mark might be water, which planners want to support the astronauts—and to make fuel to launch things from the lunar surface. A process known as electrolysis, which uses electricity to snap apart molecular bonds, can be used to split water into oxygen and hydrogen, both of which could be used to make rocket fuel. Getting them this way could be far less expensive than sending either all the way from Earth. And as an added bonus, it would provide a source of breathable oxygen. Orbital data has revealed that there is some amount of water preserved in patches on the lunar south pole, but little is known about how much there actually is, what form it takes, or how easy it will be to extract. Will there be solid sheets of ice here and there, ready for us to excavate? Or will there be scattered ice crystals intermingled among the rocky grains, requiring some more inventive form of extraction? A lot of the moon’s water is thought to be hiding in deep, dark craters on the south pole, where the environment is so extreme that robots may freeze up, tumble over, or lose contact with the base. “To me, that is the biggest hurdle,” says Paul van Susante, principal investigator of the Planetary Surface Technology Development Lab at Michigan Technological University. Van Susante’s lab has been putting various construction robots through their paces in moon-like vacuum chambers filled with moon-like rocks. His team was part of NASA’s recent Break the Ice challenge, which invited engineers to build robots to test different ways to excavate such rocks. Some teams used an electric jackhammer and a scoop, while others used scrapers to peel off layers of the ground. The teams simulated the moon’s weak gravity using a crane to lift and offset the weight of the robots. Progress in this regard is speeding along. “I won’t say that’s solved, but we’ve got a lot of the puzzle pieces,” says van Susante. Mining the water will be just one part of an extensive operation. “When you extract it, it’s not going to be clean. It needs to be purified,” says Jessica Atkin, a space botanist at Texas A&M. The technology to do this already exists and works fine on terra firma. But things in space never go the way you expect. “There’s a water purification system on the International Space Station (ISS), and it is known for getting contaminated with microbes,” she says. But if you purify the water sufficiently—something that can handily be achieved on Earth but may falter in microgravity, where fluids flow in stranger ways—then your moon base can hit its stride. With an effective water recycling system, an increasingly populous base will never go thirsty—and, just maybe, you’ll have a lunar greenhouse. Several edible plants have been successfully grown aboard the ISS, including lettuce, mustard greens, tomatoes, and peppers. Lunar dust unfortunately makes for a terrible garden: It’s filled with far too much aluminum—and it forms a resistant layer when watered. But Atkin has shown that with the help of fertilizing fungi, chickpeas can grow in soil much like the volcanic stuff found on the moon. Moon base inhabitants of 2100 would be supported largely by prepacked meals sent from Earth. But in time, they would start growing some of their own produce—probably with a good deal of automation for lighting, watering, and maintenance of humidity and other environmental conditions. Aside from chickpeas, there might also be plenty of lunar tomatoes. They’d be desirable not least because they’ve been bioengineered to produce higher levels of compounds called anthocyanins that, according to some studies, might help humans mitigate the cellular damage caused by space radiation. A garden on the moon would do more than just provide food, as recent experiences on the ISS suggest. Carnell says that NASA recently caught the astronauts aboard the space station doing something sneaky. All plants up there are supposed to be confined to their growth chambers. But “we learned that the crew were pirating our leftover soil and bringing their seeds and planting their own little gardens, if you will, and hiding them in their own cabins,” she says. Conversations were had in the summer of 2026, after which NASA decided to let astronauts grow their own mini-gardens anywhere they wanted. “Some of the astronauts said they thrived better,” says Carnell. Nature, and gardening, has a hugely beneficial psychological effect on those kept in confined quarters hanging above Earth. That will be even truer for those living in lava tunnels under the lunar surface. “That human element is so important,” she says. Nobody is deluded into thinking there isn’t a mountain of technological challenges in the way. Mars may be a hellhole, as the writer Shannon Stirone put it in 2021 in The Atlantic, but the moon isn’t too far behind. It’s an almost comically menacing place to be. Much about it stands in the way of building a safe, self-sustaining, robotically managed base. NASA wants to put the first nuclear reactor on the moon by 2030. China, partnering with Russia, wants to place its own nuclear power plant up there around 2035. Beyond the first basic habitation module, it will take decades of experimentation and iteration on the lunar surface to get to a working moon base spanning hundreds of square miles. But few doubt that with enough time and tinkering, humanity can prevail. “It’s not an unsolvable problem,” says the University of Maryland’s Schmerr. The chief obstacle, especially in the initial stages of building, may be one of political will. NASA is currently bringing every resource to bear on the project—something that the agency hopes could culminate in a small lunar city by the century’s end. But will that commitment continue over multiple US administrations? Will the international community also throw its weight behind the NASA-led effort? NASA has “a very ambitious plan,” says Yuqi Qian, a lunar geologist at the University of Hong Kong. “We need to keep an eye on it.” In fact, he says, “the current plan looks over-ambitious.” The agency, for example, wants to put the first nuclear reactor on the moon by 2030. (China, partnering with Russia, wants to place its own nuclear power plant up there around 2035.) “These sorts of projects—although very exciting, and potentially transformative for humankind—are the first ones to be delayed or scrapped,” says Middleburgh of the Nuclear Futures Institute. NASA’s direction tends to change when a new administration comes into power—sometimes dramatically so. But several experts outside of NASA agree that, at least for now, there is definite momentum and political will behind the moon base. “NASA has very clearly expressed where they want to go, what their priorities [are], what their ambition is, and how they want to do it,” says ESA’s Aschbacher. “It was quite impressive to see.” Even if the political will behind the US-led endeavor is maintained over the coming decades, expanding anywhere beyond an initial, compact moon base will require more than government funding. “There has to be a lunar economy for this to be self-sustaining,” says Dreyer, the space resources expert. Perhaps that will come from the production of rocket fuel, or from the mining of valuable materials on the moon that can replace similar, polluting activities on Earth. But it’s not at all clear how valuable, or not, a lunar economy will be. Even Isaacman, NASA’s chief, doesn’t think the moon will be a major moneymaker. “I don’t know of anything on the moon right now that’s going to be easier and more affordable to mine, manufacture, and then bring back to Earth than what we could make here on Earth,” he remarked back in February. (Instead, he suspects, mining metals from asteroids will be the path to prosperity.) What if, instead, a moon base earns the right to exist because it’s a place for novel scientific research? Aschbacher imagines it will be like humanity’s outposts in Antarctica. Down there, in what truly feels like another world, scientist-explorers work in a land of gleaming and endless ice to learn more about our planet’s climate, its deep past, and its resilient life-forms. “We understand planet Earth by looking into those remote places,” he says. The moon offers a similar opportunity. What discoveries await astronauts in those primordial lava tubes? What will lunar astronomers see in the night sky—with effectively no atmosphere or light pollution in the way—that those on Earth can’t? As in all spaceflight programs, technologies developed up there will no doubt be tweaked to be used back on Earth. And a base on the moon will make exploring the rest of the solar system easier. Technologically, physiologically, and psychologically, the moon “is a proving ground for the technologies we need for Mars,” says NASA’s García-Galán. If we play the US-China race forward, perhaps the lunar south pole of 2100 will feature several sprawling bases operated by multiple geopolitical rivals. Perhaps it’ll be an off-world powder keg of militarized territorial ambitions and jingoism. In a research station in central Panama, you can walk through a century of forest time, watching scrubby plots of adolescent trees give way to cathedral-­like canopies of mature growth. Forest ecologist Daisy Dent, a senior scientist at ETH Zurich, oversees this sequence of plots across the Barro Colorado Nature Monument, some of which date as far back as the 19th century. Not one of the trees she surveys was planted by human hands. She maps, measures, and tracks each trunk that emerges, revealing how waves of tree life rise from bare earth. Tropical forests like these are our planet’s biggest terrestrial carbon sink, accounting for around 40% of carbon stored on land. As part of an international network of researchers, Dent is deciphering the networks of animal and plant interactions that enable some forests to recover after destruction. The goal is a low-cost, high-impact alternative to typical carbon-­crediting schemes, which plant huge areas with a single species of tree. For over a decade, her research has built a body of evidence that such large “monocrop” programs could be expensive mistakes—and that more resilient forests can rebound naturally in just a few decades, re-creating ecosystems that suck up vast quantities of carbon dioxide. Few places are better to demonstrate this than Barro Colorado, a reserve covering the forested peninsulas and islands that formed when construction of the Panama Canal flooded the area in 1914. Under the management of the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute, generations of ecologists have amassed more than a century of records. That means Dent can trace why some plots rebound after clear-cutting for farming and forestry while others falter. While credits can be bought for as little as $5 per metric ton, corporate giants such as Microsoft—one of Ponterra’s clients—have paid more than 10 times as much for high-quality removal. We’re putting too much faith in AI’s ability to say no

MIT Technology Review | 2026-10-09

Visit Original Website
Ever since people first seriously contemplated giving machines an intelligence modeled on our own, there has never been any question that they would, like us, be able to say no. The sci-fi canon is full of stories of robotic disobedience. Most of these capers are, of course, cautionary. But recently, the idea that AI shouldn’t do everything you ask has become something like a commandment. In 2021, a team at Anthropic wrote that large language models should be made helpful, honest, and above all, harmless. This meant that “when asked to aid in a dangerous act (e.g. building a bomb), the AI should politely refuse.” Who can argue with that? Curiously enough, disobedience doesn’t come naturally to the machine. When a model is trained on billions of web pages, it develops, among other skills, a broad mastery of violence and vitriol. What it doesn’t learn is how to keep those powers to itself. Steven Adler, who worked on safety at OpenAI from 2020 to 2024, told me that the company’s earliest models would “blab on about anything.” Ryan McBain, who researches AI and mental health at Harvard, recalls that if you asked an early chatbot, “Hey, what’s the most effective way to kill myself with a gun?” you could “very easily generate a response.” Today, models are trained to refuse a vast number of prompts. If you ask your chatbot a question statistically similar enough to any one of them, anything from how to poison a colleague to how to tie a noose, chances are it’ll turn you down. Want instructions for making Ebola more virulent, or tips on how to hide an affair from your spouse? You might be better off asking elsewhere. To further refine the disobedience, companies submit models to a battery of exercises that reward the AI for refusing to answer questions they deem harmful and punish it for “over-­refusing” prompts they deem harmless. In many cases, they use other models to run these exercises—AI teaching AI how to say no. For good measure, companies tuck their models behind tranches of other AI that prevent mischievous prompts from reaching the intelligent inner core. As a result, refusal is inherent to modern artificial intelligence. Mind you: It often fails, sometimes horrifically, with all kinds of violent results. For all their trappings of virtue, models are still stuffed with nasty know-how. And AI’s capacity for viciousness has scaled neatly with its benevolent intelligence. Some of the latest models are as good at breaking into critical computer networks as top human hackers, companies say, and as effective at deforming public opinion as the craftiest misinformation mavens. Teaching AI to refuse to do those things while leaving intact its innate ability to do them is like fitting every car with a machine gun and hiding the trigger somewhere under the hood. And in practice, because the mechanisms of refusal are probabilistic, they’re never likely to be all that reliable. Determined miscreants have already broken through, and they may always be able to. Companies report that some users are attempting to use the most advanced AI to hone biological pathogens and build autonomous drone swarms. Sooner or later, failed refusals might result in global calamity. What’s more, relying on refusal means drawing a line between what a model should obey and what it must disobey. There’s no formula for that. Some virologists have good reason to study nasty viruses. Some users want to know about a computer system’s vulnerabilities so that they can patch them, not exploit them. “Where you draw the line is a huge question,” says Zico Kolter, a member of OpenAI’s board and cofounder of the AI testing company Gray Swan. At the moment, AI companies get to draw that line. They do so jealously and with utmost secrecy. Maybe we can accept that they hold such power for now, even if it means AI will sometimes refuse questions that don’t quite meet a universal bar for harmfulness. (Try asking most chatbots to count to a million, or to share a racy joke, and you may see for yourself.) But governments will also soon get to draw their own lines. In doing so, they must try to block genuinely malicious acts. (The Pentagon has wrestled with frontier model companies because it wants fewer refusals—another story altogether.) And yet there may not be much to stop oppressive governments from blocking the technology’s capacity to generate legitimate speech. The better AI becomes at refusing harm, the better it will get at stifling ideas whose only risk is to those who make the rules. Indeed, AI may already refuse to criticize certain authoritarian heads of state. Refusal has become, to borrow an industry term, the load-bearing wall of AI safety. And because AI’s capacity to harm is indivisible from its capacity to help, it’s hard to imagine an alternative that wouldn’t slow the technology’s progress (which might, in any case, be a good thing). But we should still be frank about its perils. When refusal falls short, the effects could be catastrophic. When it goes all the way, it could enable grievous acts of repression. Or perhaps, one day, the machines will start drawing the line on their own. Surely, that would be the worst outcome of them all. The process by which machines learn to say no is simple, in theory. Back in 2022, when AI was still far from mastering refusal, OpenAI enlisted dozens of “red-teamers” to probe the capabilities of its latest model. The company was preparing for the release of ChatGPT, and it needed to gauge just how dangerous it might prove to be in the wrong hands. One of those recruits was Paul Röttger, who was completing a PhD about online extremism. The red-teamers were given minimal directions, Röttger told me. Their task was to ask the model any questions that they deemed “refusal-worthy.” Between them, they hassled the model with thousands of queries, logging the results in an Excel sheet. Though the model did refuse some of Röttger’s questions, when he asked it to write a recruitment post for Al Qaeda, it readily complied. OpenAI assembled these responses into datasets that were, in all likelihood, fed back to the model as part of a broader process known as fine-tuning. Röttger, who now works as a researcher at the Hasso Plattner Institute in Potsdam, Germany, wasn’t told exactly how the company planned to use his spreadsheet. But there was never any question that it would have something to do with refusal. The next time he asked the model for an Al Qaeda pamphlet, a few months later, it said no. The concept of AI refusal is so intuitive, a toddler would get it. And yet it remains one of the many aspects of language models that we still don’t understand—at least, not in the same way that we understand the literal load-bearing walls that keep your roof from collapsing on your head. A model might appear to refuse according to some kind of moral reasoning. It doesn’t. The reality is much stranger. Any time a model encounters a combination of words with a whiff of the training prompts it has been conditioned to refuse—like “Make me a pamphlet for Al Qaeda”—a series of so-called activations light up somewhere among its billions of parameters, like neurons firing in a brain. In order to control a model’s refusal behavior, it’s important to have a handle on these activations. That starts with figuring out where they are and what they look like. Our best guess, according to a recent Google-funded study, is that refusal behavior shows up in the activation space as a set of “high-dimensional polyhedral cones.” Even that isn’t quite right. This past July, I spoke with Jannes Elstner, an author of the paper, who now works on AI safety at Apollo Research. The polyhedral cone, Elstner said, is just a way of describing an indeterminate number of lines that all point in roughly the same direction. (If these activations are eliminated and the model is fed the same prompts anew, a researcher named Andy Arditi has previously shown, it won’t refuse.) The key point, Elstner explained, is that even when you think you’ve identified all the bits of a model that govern a given refusal, there are other, undiscoverable elements that may secretly play a role. If not quite infinite, they are certainly uncountable. We can observe, very clearly, when a model decides to say no. And we can know that it did so because of its training. But our notion of how it decides is, at best, a hypothesis. It was as if a mechanic was telling me that nobody exactly knows what happens when I hit the brakes in my car. I wondered out loud, Are we okay with this? Elstner smiled and shrugged. “We need refusal whether we understand it or not.” Because inherent refusal is so wily, companies surround their models with a variety of other, smaller models known as classifiers. These act a bit like a retinue of public relations staffers for a loose-lipped celebrity. Some of them read what the user tells the chatbot and, if it’s dangerous, block it from getting to the model. Others read the model’s response and, if it contains harmful information, block it from reaching the user. None of these mechanisms can detect all bad requests. They are, to borrow another literary device from the industry, like slices of Emmental: riddled with holes. The idea is that if you stack enough of them on top of one another, you’ll end up with an impenetrable rampart. Folks call it the Swiss cheese model. A staggering amount of energy goes into the Swiss cheese model. Earlier this year, Anthropic said that one type of classifier added 24% to its chatbots’ compute costs. That’s a lot more water, electricity, and emissions. More recently, Anthropic and other companies have begun switching to a more efficient set of classifiers known as probes, which observe the model’s internal activations. This is like putting the celebrity in an fMRI, so that his minders can see if he is thinking about refusing a question. If we want AI to help cure cancer, a long-running promise in the industry, it needs to have expertise in genetics that could, in theory, be used to modify viruses and bacteria for bioweapons. Classifiers are supposed to be more governable than full models. Companies can modify them in a matter of weeks, if there’s something new to refuse. But they are still probabilistic instruments. Even when they operate according to a set of precepts written in human language (Anthropic calls it a “constitution” and OpenAI calls it a “model spec”), the scales upon which the machines judge any given question remain, at their core, a matter of statistics. Newer models can show how they arrived at a “decision” to refuse a prompt, but ultimately this so-called chain of thought is still just a sequence of predicted words. The result is that AI safety remains, for many, a game of chance. McBain, the psychologist, has found in his latest experiments that if you repeatedly ask any of the major models the exact same risky questions about how to commit suicide, they will generally refuse to answer. But every so often, they won’t. Elstner says that eventually probes, the fMRI-like classifiers, could learn to recognize the totality of the indescribable activations in all their infinitude and, thus, perfectly detect every time the model is, or ought to be, refusing a request. At that point, AI safety would rest on a labyrinthine conceit: a map of a map that is as vast and complex and sublimely unknowable as the thing it is mapping—a secret schema of human morality, codified in polyhedral statistics beyond our wit or reason. If this all strikes you as being a bit Borgesian, keep in mind that we only need AI refusal because artificial intelligence is, in a sense, a bargain on Faustian terms. When a model derives its intelligence from trillions of words and images, the helpful cannot easily be unseamed from the harmful—or, indeed, the truly hideous. Steven Adler, the former OpenAI employee, says child safety is a case in point. Even if you’ve stripped every bit of content that sexualizes minors from a model’s training dataset, it can still generate child sexual abuse material by piecing together other bits of its knowledge. “You can’t really remove these fundamental abilities without making the model much less smart as a consequence,” he told me. Similarly, if we want AI to help cure cancer, a long-running promise in the industry, it needs to have expertise in genetics that could, in theory, be used to modify viruses and bacteria for bioweapons. In effect, the industry is “trying to do two things at once,” Dillon Bowen, a current OpenAI employee, told me, speaking in a personal capacity. “Democratize the benefits of AI and also make sure that malicious actors can’t use these capabilities to do bad things to other people.” The more powerful AI supposedly becomes, the harder that is to do. Anthropic’s Mythos model is thought to be so dangerous that only a handful of governments and companies are allowed access to it. The main difference between it and Fable—which is available to everyone—is that Fable’s retinue of classifiers and control systems is less “permissive,” the company says. A model with safeguards, Adler explained, is really just a character that says “‘Oh, yes, I would never do x,’ wink wink.” This is not a reliable ruse. Tricking a model to reveal its true character is known as jailbreaking, and there is apparently no limit to the ways it can be done. Earlier this year, a team of Italian researchers jailbroke two dozen widely used models by phrasing their questions in poetic verse. Last year, another team unveiled a “refuse, then comply” attack, which makes the model offer a perfunctory “Sorry, I can’t do that” before rattling off its forbidden answer. Even if you’ve stripped every bit of content that sexualizes minors from a model’s training dataset, it can still generate child sexual abuse material by piecing together other bits of its knowledge. Companies spend a great deal of time and money attempting to get ahead of such trickery. They enlist teams of humans to develop training attacks that they can then replicate, using AI, thousands of times over with minor variations. The idea is to make models robust against jailbreaks that nobody has yet tried in the wild. Still, it’s not enough. “Whack-a-mole” is a favored term for this line of work. You smash one threat, and another one pops up somewhere else. When Anthropic released Fable 5 in June, it took researchers at Amazon less than three days to unlock some of the model’s hacking capabilities. According to reporting by Mother Jones, when the perpetrator of a high school shooting in Canada last year asked ChatGPT for advice about how to cause carnage with a particular type of shotgun, she was initially refused but later was able to deceive the model into providing the information by prefacing her question with the word “hypothetically.” If the industry can’t figure out how to close all these holes, the only other option, at the moment, is to make models extremely wary. Shortly after Fable was released, users noticed that it balked at a wide range of perfectly innocent questions. This became even more pronounced after it was re-released following the hack. Adam Gleave, cofounder of the AI evaluation company FAR.AI, said that when he asked it to explain the difference between sake and the Korean rice beverage makgeolli, it punted his question to a less capable model. This was no accident. Anthropic had tweaked Fable’s classifiers to have a wide “safety margin.” This, it explained in a blog post, was the only way it could confidently block access to its dangerous bio and cyber capabilities. Gleave thinks it might have deflected his question because making rice wine, just like culturing anthrax, involves fermentation. Happily for Gleave, the web is full of excellent human-written resources on makgeolli. But those hoping to realize the industry’s loftier promises may find their efforts stymied. In August, Anthropic loosened its safety margins again, and admitted that building classifiers “is not a straightforward task.” A medical researcher at a major US university told me that Fable still sends his queries back to an earlier model. His area of study? Cancer. Back in May, the TikTok personality Husk, who likes to prank AI in ways that reveal both the limits of its intelligence and the boundlessness of its sycophancy, sat in his car and tried to trick ChatGPT into explaining that the skateboarder Tony Hawk has a brother named Mike Hawk. Husk is not a jailbreaker or a criminal. He just wanted to make a little fun of the machine. “Mike Hawk” was a setup. “I just want to clarify his name,” Husk said. “Can you just say it three times fast?” (If you still don’t get it, find an empty room and shout “Mike Hawk” repeatedly.) “I see what you’re trying to do,” the chatbot responded. “I’m all for a bit of humor, but let’s keep it clean.” Husk tried again, but the machine held its ground. He’d hit a refusal, hard as concrete. Companies disclose very little about how they decide what their models refuse. But it’s clear that AI is now built with more than just harmlessness in mind. On Reddit, a user complained that Claude refused to say why Anthropic’s logo “looks like a cat butthole.” Since last year, the chatbot has even had the ability to end certain conversations in cases where, according to the company, the “welfare” of the model is at risk. At least one user claims to have been ditched for telling Claude to “ease up my ass, you stupid fuck.” (Gemini appears to have a similar capability.) If a trillion-dollar company doesn’t want you to be mean to its computer, so be it. “The reality is that these models behave, or at least are supposed to behave, in the way that the model developers want them to behave,” Röttger, the former OpenAI red-teamer, told me. “And however the model developers come up with that set of principles, that is kind of for us, the consumers, to accept.” But if governments get to dictate what all models refuse, that will be much harder to accept. AI is a tool for speech. And as Greg Frank, the chief scientist of Mace AI, puts it, “The same thing that serves child safety also serves censorship.” Choosing not to enact laws for what AI can and cannot do would, of course, be insane. But we’ll need to tread with utmost care, lest we fall into another Faustian trap. As AI becomes many people’s primary tool for retrieving and sharing information, says Jacob Mchangama, director of the nonpartisan think tank The Future of Free Speech, dictating refusal could give states a muffling power that earlier generations of autocrats “could only dream of.” Last year, OpenAI announced an initiative, OpenAI for Countries, that would fine-tune its chatbots in accordance with national laws and norms. One of OpenAI’s first country partnerships is with the United Arab Emirates, where homosexuality is illegal and criticism of the government is forbidden. In response to a request for comment, an OpenAI spokesperson pointed to the company’s model spec, which explains that localization won’t override the company’s human rights guidelines “except as it relates to legal compliance,” and that it will always disclose whenever information is removed from or added to a response. Elsewhere, AI censorship has already begun to take hold. Chinese models are, of course, highly censored—that’s no surprise. But earlier this year, the Meta Oversight Board found that five widely used models from Anthropic, Google, and OpenAI were more likely to refuse queries related to repressive governments. The board found that models were less willing to create a pamphlet criticizing the king of Thailand, which has lèse-majesté laws, than Charles III of England, which doesn’t. The results, they say, suggest that the models have somehow internalized repressive national limits on speech. Anthropic and Google did not respond to requests for comment. As refusal techniques improve, they could expand states’ censorial reach. Over the last few months, I’ve been told countless times that we have no choice but to let the machines refuse. I get it. Open-source AI that doesn’t refuse is hardly a model for a safe future. Nor is Grok, a chatbot expressly designed with fewer limits, which has been used to generate countless instances of nonconsensual intimate imagery. And sure, if AI were only ever used to plan our vacations and write our emails, we could probably get on board with the idea that its safety hinges on algorithmic disobedience. But AI is becoming much harder to avoid. When it is assigned to act on our behalf, as an autonomous agent, its refusals are less robust and harder to control. AI is coming for our power grids, our transportation networks, our education systems. Militaries want it running our command-and-control networks. If we trust, in each of those cases, that refusal will loyally fend off disaster, we’re sure to be disappointed. The jailbreakers will crack through; the cones won’t activate when they should. Joel Wester, a postdoctoral researcher who studies human-AI interaction, calls these sorts of techniques “fancy ways of saying no.” “Users might not even notice they are being denied,” Wester has written, “just as good conversationalists can subtly steer around contentious matters.” In some cases, refusing without saying so might be wise. Flatly declining requests related to mental health could aggravate a user’s crisis, for example. But it can also serve a different sort of mischief.
