Google has announced a “super network” upgrade for its Fi cell service. For the past few years, Google Fi has operated exclusively on the T-Mobile network, but now it’s adding support for a second 5G network. Oddly, Google refuses to say which one. Google’s MVNO (mobile virtual network operator) launched in 2015 as Project Fi with support for both T-Mobile and Sprint service. The service initially only worked on Google’s Nexus phones, which would switch between networks based on signal strength. After T-Mobile absorbed Sprint, Fi switched to being a single-network service in the US while continuing to hop between networks for international travel. With the super network, Google Fi is again a dual-carrier service stateside. Phones connected to Fi will be able to hop between T-Mobile 5G and a different 5G network to provide the best possible connectivity. Google’s blog post announcing the change conspicuously doesn’t mention either network, saying only that it supports “two of America’s best 5G cellular networks.” We know one of them is T-Mobile, and the other must be either AT&T or Verizon. When reached for comment, a Google spokesperson declined to name the second network, which is odd. Looking at the Fi coverage map, my best guess for the super network partner is Verizon. The shape of unserved regions is a closer match than AT&T, but carriers do tend to fudge the fine details of these maps. MVNOs don’t always advertise which network they use, and that information is getting harder to find in FAQs and support pages. Google’s Fi FAQ no longer even confirms T-Mobile as a network partner. Google used to be very upfront about its partnership with T-Mobile, pointing out that, unlike most MVNOs, it gets top prioritization on T-Mobile’s towers. Network information like this is really something consumers should have when shopping for plans—you probably know which carriers work best where you spend your time. There are a few caveats in the super network rollout beyond the mysterious new partner. Currently, network switching is only supported on the Pixel 9, Pixel 10, and Pixel 11 series. Other devices will get access in 2027. You also won’t be able to enjoy traditional dual-SIM functionality if you want Fi to switch networks. This will only work if you have Fi configured as an eSIM with all non-Fi SIMs disabled. If you meet those requirements, super network support is rolling out now at no additional cost on Unlimited Premium and Flexible plans.

Viewers can build one entirely on their own, or describe what they’re looking for and let Tubi offer a starting place that they can edit, rearrange, or rebuild entirely. And while some streaming service companies are turning to AI to help with content recommendations, a human connection may appeal more to certain users, while keeping costs lower for Tubi.

Since Valve announced its Steam Deck Verified program in 2021, players have found that a game’s “Verified” status doesn’t always match how well it actually runs on the handheld. Now, new Steam Deck frame rate data being shared by Valve itself can help players dig deeper into the performance they can expect from many Steam Deck games. Valve has been collecting anonymized frame rate data from Steam players who opt in since February. Yesterday evening, the company announced it has started sharing that data on many Steam Store pages as averages and histograms for both the Steam Deck and Steam Deck OLED. Valve acknowledges that players enabling VSync or frame rate limits can bias the collected data somewhat and that the measurements don’t account for custom in-game settings that Steam Deck users may tinker with to get a game running. Regardless, Valve says it has nonetheless “found that the framerate data consolidates around relatively narrow ranges.” This kind of data can be extremely useful for distinguishing between the wide range of performance levels that can exist among “Verified” Steam Deck games. Both Cyberpunk 2077 and Skyrim: Special Edition earn the same Steam Deck Verified badge, for instance, but the former sees average Deck frame rates in the 30–40 FPS range, while the latter usually achieves 50–60 FPS on the hardware. Valve’s frame rate data is even more useful for games that the company insists are “Unsupported” on Steam Deck because their “graphics setting cannot be configured to run well” on the hardware or because Valve is “still working on adding support for this game.” Despite warnings like these, games like Starfield, Silent Hill 2, and Assassin’s Creed Syndicate, to name just a few, all show extremely playable frame rates reported by actual Steam Deck users. Assassin’s Creed Syndicate in particular approaches a 60 FPS average on the Steam Deck OLED hardware despite its “Unsupported” status. Frame rate data can also be useful for judging the single-player modes of games whose multiplayer doesn’t work under SteamOS. Watch Dogs 2, for example, is officially Unsupported on Steam Deck because of anti-cheat compatibility issues, but Steam Deck players report averages of 40+ FPS on the game’s single-player modes. Of course, collecting and averaging frame rate data for the Steam Deck(s) is made simpler by their set hardware configuration. When it comes to communicating expected frame rates for the wide range of hardware in more generalized gaming PCs, Valve says it is “always looking for new ways to help developers understand the experiences customers are having with their games.” Back in April, JSON files buried in the Steam client pointed to a potential Steam “Framerate Estimator” tool that could let users “Select an App and a PC config to get a chart of estimated framerates, based on the framerates of other Steam users,” according to strings contained within. That could be incredibly useful for Steam users who want to go beyond the listed Minimum and Recommended hardware requirements without relying on shady-looking third-party FPS-estimator websites. In the meantime, is sharing average frame rate data for the fixed-configuration Steam Machine too much to ask?

High oil prices have led to a dip in global carbon emissions

Evaluating the annual global temperatures each year requires context. There is a fair amount of natural variability from one year to the next, so it’s important to focus on the trend over time. Famously, global warming did not end in 1998, as some claimed a couple of decades ago—we just had a run of La-Niña-dominated years after a very strong El Niño. Annual greenhouse gas emissions are much less variable, having increased fairly steadily, but many complicating factors can still muddy the picture. Sometimes those factors conspire to throw a year off the trend. It looks like 2026 will be one of those years. A new analysis led by Corinne Le Quéré at the University of East Anglia estimates that fossil fuel emissions have fallen about 0.4 percent from last year. This probably isn’t a peak that symbolizes heartening progress on climate change, though; the researchers think it’s a short-term dip driven by the war in Iran and its effect on oil prices. This team works on the Global Carbon Project, which compiles stats on fossil fuel use and things like deforestation to track carbon emissions globally and by country. In this case, they looked at the stats and tried to pull out key underlying trends that show us what to expect over the next couple of years. Last year, fossil fuel emissions increased 1.2 percent, a bit above the 2019–2025 average of 0.8 percent per year. This year’s decline featured a 1.9 percent drop in oil emissions, a smaller 0.3 percent drop in emissions from natural gas use, and a 0.6 percent increase in coal emissions. Unlike the pandemic-driven drop in 2020, this wasn’t driven by a widespread decrease in energy demand. Demand did increase, but additions of electric vehicles and solar and wind generation covered almost 70 percent of the growth. Electric vehicles can account for only about 20 percent of that consequential decline in oil emissions, meaning the bulk is due to high prices. One simple way to break down emissions is to connect it to economic output. Given a country’s gross domestic product (GDP), energy use per unit GDP, and carbon emissions per unit energy, you can calculate total carbon emissions. GDP might go up, but if that’s due to less energy-intensive industries or a cleaner energy system, carbon emissions might not rise with it. Between 2019 and 2025, 59 countries cut their emissions despite economic growth, even as global emissions increased by 0.8 percent per year. The researchers estimated that, had the economic growth not been accompanied by the growth of electric vehicles and solar and wind generation, the increase would have been much higher, at 1.9 percent per year. Given the International Monetary Fund’s projection for GDP through 2028, the researchers extrapolated these trends forward as well. From that, we would expect global fossil fuel emissions to continue increasing. That includes the present year—without the shock to oil prices, we would be seeing emissions increase in 2026 as well. So while we could be close to peak emissions, we’re probably not there yet. The growth of electric vehicles and renewable energy is offsetting most of the growth of emissions, but not all of it. That would take just a bit more juice. The researchers write, “If further acceleration of clean technology deployment can be supported through time, a sustained peak and decline in global CO2 emissions could be a few years away.” Nature Climate Change, 2026. NASA’s competition for the development of private space stations that will take flight in the 2030s is already nearing crunch time. But a recently unsealed lawsuit may spice things up even further. The lawsuit involves two of the most prominent companies in this competition, Houston-based Axiom Space and Long Beach, California-based Vast Space. And it is surfacing as final proposals for private space stations are due from companies to the US space agency a little more than one month from today, on December 14. According to the court filings unsealed by a November 4 order from US District Judge Charles Eskridge, Axiom is pursuing a False Claims Act case against Vast in which Axiom accuses Vast of presenting NASA with a fraudulent mission-planning document that incorporates Axiom’s proprietary information. “Axiom Space filed this false-claims action on behalf of the government because public-private partnerships in human spaceflight depend on candor, capability, original work, and integrity,” an Axiom Space spokesperson told Ars. “Presenting another company’s hard-won experience as its own does not meet that standard.” In response, Vast said the lawsuit was about competition, not fraud. “NASA opened private astronaut missions to more than one provider because competition drives innovation, delivers better value for taxpayers, and keeps America leading in space,” a Vast spokesperson said. “Misusing litigation against a competitor works against all of that.” So what happened? Let’s unpack this further to understand what we do and don’t know at this point. The dispute revolves around a program called Private Astronaut Missions, launched in 2019, through which NASA allows private companies to organize missions of up to four astronauts to visit the International Space Station. The missions are part of NASA’s effort to increase commercial activity in low-Earth orbit and allow private companies to gain some operational experience as they develop their own commercial space stations. As such, these missions are viewed by some as a stepping stone toward private station contracts with NASA. Axiom became the early leader in the program, winning contracts to organize the first four private missions. The first, Axiom-1, launched in April 2022, while the most recent, Axiom-4, launched to the International Space Station in the summer of 2025. Then, in January, NASA awarded the fifth private astronaut mission to Axiom, followed by the sixth to Vast Space in February and the seventh to Voyager Technologies in April. As part of these agreements, private companies generally purchase mission services from NASA, including food, storage, and other supplies. In return, NASA buys the capability to return scientific samples to Earth that must remain cold during transit. The private astronaut mission agreements include contractual deliverables that providers must submit for NASA’s review. According to Axiom’s lawsuit, one such deliverable under its contracts is a Mission Integration and Operations Management Plan, or MIOMP, essentially the operational blueprint for the entire mission. Axiom alleges that Vast’s contract contains a similar requirement and that NASA’s acceptance of the document is a condition for a milestone payment. According to the lawsuit filed by Axiom, prior to June 3, Vast submitted its mission-planning document for the private astronaut mission to NASA. Then, on June 3, NASA notified Axiom “that its sensitive information had been improperly included in a deliverable submitted by Vast.” On page 11 of the Axiom lawsuit, the company alleges: “NASA advised that Vast’s submission in some locations substituted ‘the PAM Provider’ for Axiom’s name, while in other instances retained Axiom’s name and company details instead of replacing it with Vast’s name. That the document retained residual references to Axiom in certain sections demonstrates that the underlying text originated with Axiom and was not independently authored by Vast.” Axiom said its mission planning documents are “sensitive and proprietary” information. These documents represent years of work and significant investment, a company spokesperson said. “A Mission Integration and Operations Management Plan is not paperwork,” the Axiom spokesperson said. “It is the document that demonstrates to NASA the company understands and can execute every step required to keep a crew safe from launch to return. When human lives depend on a plan, the company submitting it must have developed it, understood it, and be able to stand behind it.” Axiom filed a lawsuit in a Houston federal court, where the company is based, on June 19. The False Claims Act complaint seeks civil penalties, treble damages, litigation costs, and a statutory whistleblower award. There are plenty of unanswered questions at this point. Principal among them is how Vast obtained Axiom’s planning document and how it was inserted into Vast’s submission to NASA. What does seem notable is that Axiom sued under the False Claims Act rather than bringing a conventional trade-secret misappropriation case. The False Claims Act imposes a demanding burden of proving fraud against the government, but successful cases can produce substantial civil penalties and damages. A finding of fraud could also complicate Vast’s ability to compete for future NASA contracts, potentially including the commercial space station competition. Additionally, by filing a False Claims Act case in federal court and essentially framing the issue as one of fraud against NASA, Axiom triggered a federal review that gave the US Department of Justice the opportunity to investigate the allegations and decide whether to take over prosecution. However, the Department of Justice declined to intervene on October 23, according to the court documents. Axiom is therefore pursuing the allegations independently. “The Department of Justice’s decision not to intervene does not mean that what happened was acceptable,” the Axiom spokesperson told Ars. However, Vast officials said the government’s decision not to intervene reflects the merits of the case. “The Department of Justice reviewed Axiom’s allegations and declined to intervene,” the spokesperson said. “We think that decision speaks for itself. Axiom’s complaint does not describe a fraud on the government, and it does not meet the basic requirements of the False Claims Act.” The Justice Department’s decision does not establish whether Vast improperly used Axiom’s materials. In False Claims Act cases, federal prosecutors may decline to intervene for a variety of reasons, including doubts about the case’s legal merits, the availability of evidence, or the potential recovery. The government provided no explanation for its decision in the court filing. Axiom remains free to pursue the lawsuit on its own. And the company appears poised to do so. Vast said it would defend itself “vigorously” in legal proceedings. The company also said its work with NASA on the sixth Private Astronaut Mission—for which customers have already been announced—is proceeding without difficulty or interruption. NASA now finds itself the central witness in a case involving two of the leading companies chasing private space station contracts that could ultimately be worth billions of dollars. Other potential contenders include Voyager, Blue Origin, and possibly SpaceX. The agency declined to provide a substantive comment for this article. “NASA is aware of the matter but does not comment on litigation,” agency spokesman George Alderman told Ars. The commercial space stations program, which dates back half a decade, has been a rough-and-tumble contest as private companies have jostled for favor with NASA. Ultimately, the space agency is expected to pick only a few winners to finish their space stations in low-Earth orbit and pay handsomely for NASA astronauts to visit there in the 2030s. At times, this contest has turned personal. In September 2025, for example, far-right political activist Laura Loomer shared an in-depth post on the social media site X purporting to link Vast chief executive Max Haot, who was born in Belgium, and Vast with Chinese interests. These allegations, which raised national security concerns, were wholly unsubstantiated. A recent Washington Post investigation reported that Loomer has received payments for some political posts on X. It is also not the only lawsuit Axiom has filed this year. In April, after policy advisor Ramzi Masri-Elyafaoui left the company to work for rival Vast, Axiom sued to stop him, alleging he violated a non-compete agreement. In June, a Houston federal judge dismissed the case with prejudice.

Delaware’s federal court forced litigation funders into the open. Its most important court still lets them hide

Fortune | FORTUNE | 2026-11-10

Visit Original Website
Sonnenfeld is one of the nation’s most recognized experts on corporate governance. Sonnenfeld and Tian  have co-authored Trump’s Ten Commandments (Worth/Simon & Schuster) We warned in the pages of Fortune last week that the corporate flight from Delaware would only accelerate on the heels of Delaware Chancery Court Vice Chancellor Travis Laster’s controversial and widely criticized decision in the highly watched Cloudflare case. Since then, Virtu Financial has announced its own relocation from Delaware to Texas on Nov. 4, following the recent exits of at least 30 companies over the last two years. Virtu’s move comes as it too is embroiled in pending litigation in Laster’s court, after Laster denied Virtu’s motion to dismiss. As we’ve written previously, the boards of companies moving out of Delaware most often cite the same concern: they can no longer predict how the state’s Chancery Court, once prized for its consistency and predictability, will treat them, amidst a series of decisions widely construed as judicial overreach. The legislature has tried to correct this problem through SB21, but certain Chancery judges continue to seemingly defy the will of the legislature, and the Delaware Supreme Court has inadvertently undermined their own efforts to overturn erroneous decisions by remanding cases back to the same judges. But a second blind spot gets far less attention, and it may matter just as much. A Delaware company can be hauled into the Court of Chancery today with no way of knowing who is paying for the lawsuit against it, whether that backer controls settlement, or whether it is a competitor or a foreign state amidst a surge in litigation backed by litigation finance firms. The legal philosopher Jeremy Bentham wrote that “publicity is the very soul of justice.” One of the quiet strengths of the American legal system is how much of that ideal it has honored. Our courts function in the open, with public proceedings, published rules, and disclosure requirements designed to ensure that judges, litigants, and the public can understand who is driving a case—and why. Those features are not procedural niceties; they are cornerstones of trust in the judiciary itself. Delaware’s century-old promise to corporate America rested on the same idea: boards and litigants could see the rules and the players before they acted. Undisclosed third-party litigation funding cuts against that promise in the one court that matters most to American business. A growing, mostly invisible market of litigation finance which has become perverted away from original noble intention The global litigation finance market was estimated at $20.6 billion in 2025, with forecasts that it will more than double over the next decade, and U.S. commercial funders alone managed roughly $16 billion in assets at the end of 2024. This is a market which has already grown exponentially, as it barely existed merely two decades ago. At its best, litigation funding expands access to justice for plaintiffs who could not otherwise afford to be heard, a noble founding intent as it was envisioned by the widely admired founders of the field. But as litigation finance firms have proliferated exponentially over recent years, the actual practice of litigation finance has frequently become perverted from those noble founding aims, with key challenges including that too often, it is opaque, undisclosed, and strategically deployed. It obscures the real parties who have an interest in dragging out litigation and obfuscates what ulterior motives they may have, distorts the incentives that are supposed to drive a case toward resolution, and risks undermining the integrity of the courts themselves. Unlike the contingency-fee arrangements familiar to American law, many third-party investments by litigation finance firms involve opaque layered structures of who gets paid, and complex ownership that remain invisible to judges and opposing parties. No federal statute or rule requires disclosure of even the existence of a litigation funder—let alone whether that litigation funder holds sway over litigation strategy or settlement decisions, as they sometimes do. Real risks of undisclosed hidden litigation finance, including most alarmingly to national security The potential harms are concrete from undisclosed litigation finance firms underwriting litigation. First, an outside litigation funder’s investment return thresholds can push it to resist settlement and prolong disputes, at real cost to litigants and to judicial resources. Second, a funder may not be motivated by financial profit at all. An undisclosed entity can underwrite suits to impose burdensome costs on a competitor for competitive harm, and when the money answers to a foreign state, the concern becomes one of national security. A December 2024 Government Accountability Office report found stakeholders pointing to funders from China, Saudi Arabia, and France, while conceding that the full extent of such funding is unknowable given how little is disclosed. The industry’s ties to foreign capital are not speculative, as one firm has announced a “strategic capital relationship” with a sovereign wealth fund, while another is now majority-owned by a consortium led by Mubadala, Abu Dhabi’s sovereign investor. Our courts are open by design, but openness should not mean naïveté. This isn’t hypothetical. A Shenzhen-based firm led by the general counsel of Chinese electronics giant TCL was revealed to be financing patent suits against Samsung in U.S. courts , and Samsung later alleged that the funder had misused its confidential information to compete unfairly. America’s famously broad civil discovery magnifies the danger. Justified by the principle that both sides should have access to the facts, discovery is estimated to account for 50% to 90% of the cost of litigating a case. That lets a funded plaintiff run up a defendant’s bill as an end in itself, or as a way to operationally hinder a competitor by requesting a document dump of sensitive strategic files of limited relevance to the actual litigation at hand, but of huge importance to sensitive industry and strategic competition. Indeed, when a lawsuit is secretly financed by an entity with strategic or geopolitical motives, discovery can become nothing more than a mechanism for extracting sensitive information under the protective cover of judicial process, with that sensitive information subsequently used by hostile foreign powers to undermine American companies unfairly. Delaware’s federal court has already proved disclosure works Patent litigation shows the pattern at scale. Patent cases absorbed 32% of new litigation-funding commitments in 2024, up from 19% a year earlier and the largest single category, and roughly 30% of patent suits are now estimated to be funded. Most of the large technology companies the GAO interviewed said that more than half the patent suits filed against them involve confirmed or suspected third-party funding. The suits increasingly come from non-practicing entities that make no products but acquire patents to monetize them in court: in 2025, NPEs filed 55% of all district-court patent cases and 90% of those in the high-tech sector. Because the U.S. Supreme Court held in TC Heartland that a corporation “resides” for patent-venue purposes only in its state of incorporation, Delaware’s federal district court is a default venue for patent suits against Delaware corporations. In April 2022, Chief Judge Colm Connolly ordered parties before him to disclose any litigation funder and whether it controls litigation or settlement decisions. The results were revealing. Patent filings in the district fell 41% over the following two years, against a 15% national decline, and funded patent cases in his courtroom virtually disappeared. It was Connolly’s order that surfaced the profoundly disturbing Chinese TCL-linked funder behind the Samsung suits mentioned previously. Transparency, it turns out, discourages the litigation that least wants to be seen. Delaware’s state courts have been slower to follow. To their credit, on Sept. 30, 2026, the Superior Court amended its rules to permit discovery into a funder’s existence, identity, and control rights while shielding the funding agreement itself absent a showing of good cause. But the Court of Chancery—the most important court in the nation for corporate disputes—has adopted no corresponding rule and proceeds case by case. Its judges have taken notice of the problem. In Burkhart v. Genworth, a putative class action decided in 2024, Vice Chancellor Nathan Cook ordered the plaintiffs to produce their funding agreement in full, warning that such cases are “especially ripe for a third-party funder to exercise improper control over the litigation.” That ruling shows the court understands the stakes, but a single order in a single case by one of the court’s seven judges, won only after a motion to compel, falls well short of a universal disclosure requirement. The gap matters, because Chancery is where corporate America’s stockholder litigation lives, as the single most important court for American businesses in the entire country. When a court and defendants do not know who stands behind a complaint, it cannot fully assess conflicts of interest, police litigation conduct, or tell whether decisions are being driven by the named plaintiff or by an undisclosed backer with very different and ulterior objectives. None of this is a criticism of judges, who can only manage what is disclosed to them. The problem is structural – and easily fixable by the Delaware Chancery Court. The case for reform is increasingly difficult to ignore. Members of Congress have pressed the judiciary to move faster. The chairman of the House Oversight Committee urged the Judicial Conference in 2024 to consider mandatory disclosure, citing foreign money in U.S. lawsuits; a bipartisan Senate bill introduced in 2023 would require any foreign funder to disclose its role and bar sovereign wealth funds from financing U.S. litigation; and four senators introduced legislation this February requiring disclosure in federal class actions and multidistrict litigation. Regulators and states are moving too. The International Trade Commission proposed in April to require disclosure of funders in Section 337 proceedings, the arcane but consequential cases that determine whether foreign goods can be blocked from the U.S. market. Ohio enacted a law in July requiring funders to register and disclose their agreements, barring them from influencing how a lawsuit is handled or settled, and prohibiting foreign funders outright. North Carolina on June 22 became the first state to prohibit third-party litigation investment altogether. More than 20 states have now enacted laws regulating the practice, and New Jersey’s Assembly passed a disclosure bill in June. Transparency is the remedy. Full, universal disclosure simply ensures that judges and litigants know who the real stakeholders are and what their motives may be —just as they already do when funders back class actions in the Northern District of California, when creditor groups organize in a bankruptcy, and when an investor accumulates more than 5% of a public company. Capital markets function on disclosure, not secrecy. Shareholders disclose ownership. Political donors disclose contributions. Judges disclose conflicts. The notion that litigation funders should be uniquely exempt makes no sense. Boards leaving Delaware cite the increasing unpredictability of the Delaware Chancery Court and the cost of stockholder litigation, but undisclosed litigation funding feeds both. It should let everyone see who is paying to litigate under them. Doing so would be a departure from American impeachment practice. On this side of the Atlantic we have not hitherto chosen to use impeachment this way, preferring instead to control erring presidents with legislative authorities like the power of the purse, or, failing that, by evicting them by election or attempting to impeach them directly. Recall that the War Powers Act was adopted in the wake of the Vietnam War as a response to the fact that successive presidents—John F. The act sought to balance the need for congressional approval of the grave choice to send Americans to war with the exigencies of the modern era in which genuine military challenges might arise and require response before Congress could assemble to judge the matter. The act’s provisions are complicated, but in essence they enjoin the president from involving the military in hostilities without either A) a declaration of war or other expression of congressional approval, or B) a national emergency. If the president does send American forces into combat without prior congressional approval, the act requires that he notify Congress within 48 hours and that hostilities be terminated within 60 days (with a possible 30-day extension) unless Congress acts to approve their continuation. Moreover, the act provides that Congress can force termination of American engagement at any time by concurrent resolution of both houses. The article based on the boat strikes plainly implicates Trump inasmuch as the president has publicly embraced these extrajudicial killings. Nonetheless, removing Hegseth without going straight at the president himself would establish that a Cabinet officer who does not merely execute unpopular policy but follows illegal orders and commits impeachable “high crimes and misdemeanors” can and will be removed from office. The deterrent effect of such a decision on future senior government officials tasked with doing wrong would be immensely desirable. During a press event in Las Vegas last week, AMD spilled the beans on its latest Zen cores, which promise an average 27 percent uplift in instructions per clock (IPC) across a variety of AI and server workloads, along with boosted clocks ranging from 4.1 to 5.3 GHz. We expect to see even higher frequencies on consumer chips before long, but AMD isn’t quite ready to talk about Ryzen just yet. CPU performance is a function of power, IPC, and clocks, and with gains across all three, the cores are shaping up to be one of the company’s biggest generational jumps ever. As in past Epyc launches, Zen 6 isn’t one core. It comes in two flavors: a frequency-optimized variant (Zen 6) and a density-optimized version (Zen 6c). This split dates back to the launch of AMD’s Bergamo Epycs in 2023, which packed 128 compact Zen 4c cores into just eight chiplets. The chip’s debut drew comparisons to Intel’s still relatively new Efficient (E-cores) for obvious reasons. But under the hood, AMD’s Zen 4c was nothing like what Intel was doing. The core itself was nearly identical to Zen 4; it simply traded clocks and a bit of cache per core for a more densely packed CPU die. The only difference between running an application on Bergamo or Genoa was how quickly it executed. The same could not be said of Intel’s P and E cores, which used different ISAs. This remained a defining characteristic and advantage of AMD’s compact core architecture through Zen 5, which allowed it to ship Turin parts with up to 192 cores without users having to worry about whether their app was running on a compact core or not. With Zen 6c, at least as we understand it, this philosophy is largely unchanged. Both Zen 6 and Zen 6c share the same ISA, with clock being the main differentiator. There are other micro-architectural differences between the two cores designed to balance IPC and clock speeds more effectively. Looking at the core’s block diagram, this isn’t immediately obvious. Zen 6 and Zen 5 share a very similar front and backend. We'd be tempted to call the core iterative if it weren't for the 20 to 31 percent IPC uplift for cloud and AI workloads that AMD claims over Zen 5. This suggests that core for core, a 4.1 GHz Zen 6c-based Venice Epyc should deliver performance on par with a Zen 5-based Turin Epyc running at 5 GHz. That's a pretty sizable jump, but just because AMD is seeing generational performance uplift in enterprise server and cloud workloads doesn't mean we'll see the same gains on the client side. Many will recall the roughly 5 percent performance gains gaming PC enthusiasts reported with the Ryzen 9000-series, leading to the rather unfortunate turn of phrase "Zen 5 percent." That happened because game engines at the time simply weren't CPU-bottlenecked, and so faster cores could only raise the ceiling so much. Even so, AMD fellow and "Zen daddy" Mike Clark tells El Reg that PC buyers expect more than a 6 percent gain from Zen 6. We're glossing over a bunch of low-level architectural optimizations here, but according to Clark, one of the biggest drivers of performance was the front end. Zen 5 introduced a dual front end, which for simple workloads worked great, but lost a lot of steam when confronted with more dynamic loads. "The Zen 6 team did a really good job for those larger footprint workloads that have a bipolar nature," Clark said. "It's not something that comes through. You're not going to see it in a block diagram very well. It's sort of subtle... but it's a big lever." Another contributor was the move to a distributed scheduler in the out-of-order pipeline. One of the biggest performance drivers in modern CPU cores is that they don't execute instructions in the order they receive them. Instead, they reorder the instructions so as to keep the core's backend execution units fed with a constant stream of instructions. This is where we see the biggest difference between Zen 6 and Zen 6c. To account for the slower clocks on the latter core, AMD gave the core's scheduler deeper queues, which Clark tells us helps with higher-latency workloads. Meanwhile, from what we understand, Zen 6's shallower queue enables it to clock higher on less power. Finally, on the backend, AMD made several subtle changes which should have major implications for vector heavy workloads, including AI. One of the more significant was the ability to perform four AVX2 or two AVX-512 loads per cycle. While this doesn't improve AVX-512 performance much, for applications that haven't been updated to use it, the core is now able to perform twice as many loads, which should improve throughput for legacy applications. This should come in handy, particularly on PCs, where Intel's outright lack of AVX-512 support has made AVX2 the lowest common denominator for a lot of software. That's not to say AVX-512 didn't get some love this generation. For those running AI workloads on CPUs, Zen 6 now natively supports FP16 datatypes. Previously FP16 was supported, but required padding the data to FP32, which meant the lower precision didn't buy much in terms of performance. That's no longer the case in Zen 6. Normally, boosting L3 capacity would have required larger chiplets with lower yields, but that's not how AMD has gone about it for the past four years. That consumer desktop chip stacked a 64 MB SRAM tile on top of an 8-core chiplet, boosting L3 capacity to 96 MB. The extra cache made the chip one of the most potent gaming CPUs of the time. Later that year, AMD brought the same technology to its Milan generation of Epycs as the company expanded its portfolio to address a wider range of workloads. The extra cache was popular enough to justify a second generation of X-series Epycs, which launched in 2023. But then, nothing. Venice-X marks the return of AMD's X-chips, and for the most part, the processor largely follows the same recipe as its predecessors. Each of the chip's up to eight CPU dies is now bonded to a 96 MB SRAM tile using advanced packaging, for a total of 144 MB of L3 per chiplet or 1,152 MB per socket, which you might have noticed is the same as in the Genoa-X parts they replace. The metals industry has experienced transformational moments before: the Bessemer process, flash smelting, electric arc furnaces, and modern continuous casting all changed what was possible. Yet, for more than a century, most extraction processes rely on techniques and pathways that would be familiar to metallurgists from generations ago. Processes have become larger and control systems smarter, but the fundamental science behind separating metals from ores has changed less than many realize. Today’s materials challenges are different from those of the 20th century, but why are critical metals still produced using largely the same scientific foundations? EvoMaterials, a technology platform developed by Outokumpu—the global leader in sustainable stainless steel and the producer of ferrochrome with 67% lower carbon footprint than the industry average—is exploring that question by rethinking how metals can be extracted: “We decided to go beyond the current constraints and look fundamentally at what’s the best way to do this with the benefit of the scientific engineering knowledge and hindsight that we have today,” says Caspar Stinn, technical director for extractive metallurgy at Outokumpu. “We’re looking at how we can reimagine metals production.” As economies expand and the world becomes increasingly electrified, data and scientific models point to rising demand for many minerals, driven by technologies ranging from data centers to aerospace, water treatment, energy systems, and defence. According to Antoine Allanore, professor of metallurgy at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) this coincides with many Western economies having reduced their domestic processing capacity for the very metals those industries depend on. Metallurgy is now strategically important, shaped not only by cost and availability but also by carbon footprint, geographic origin and supply-chain resilience. That reality is particularly relevant for chromium and other specialty metals used in high-temperature and extreme environments. Chromium-based materials play a critical role in aerospace systems, power generation, corrosion-resistant infrastructure and defense technologies, where materials must perform under demanding conditions. For decades, metallurgical innovation largely focused on improving yields, energy efficiency and process control. EvoMaterials is based on the idea that some metallurgical processes may benefit from entirely different chemical pathways. The platform originates from research into sulfidation-based processing technologies, initially pursued to reduce carbon emissions associated with ferrochrome production. Laboratory work revealed broader potential. Lab-scale trials demonstrated that these alternative process chemistries could potentially do more than lower emissions. They could recover valuable metals from lower grade resources and industrial side streams while fundamentally altering how separation processes are performed. The opportunity is to rethink how mineral resources are used and how much value can be extracted from them, bringing in greater resource efficiency and stronger industrial competitiveness. Development of the technology began in the United States in 2021 for chromite from Outokumpu’s Kemi mine in Finland, the only chrome mine in the European Union and one of the few such operations across the Western world. Since then, Outokumpu has evolved the technology through extensive research and development, combining new process chemistries with proprietary metallurgical expertise, supported in part by a non-exclusive patent license agreement from MIT. “The technology is a new application for separating metallic elements from materials,” explains Allanore. Historically, that separation has been achieved using carbon- and energy-intensive approaches. The new method creates a different chemical environment that enables more efficient extraction with fewer losses and fewer unwanted by-products, recovering critical materials from resources considered waste. EvoMaterials sits at the intersection of several scientific disciplines, a combination that strongly resonates with Stinn, who completed his PhD in Materials Science and Engineering at MIT, where his research focused on low-carbon metals processing and recycling technologies. Modern materials innovation draws on a growing mix of chemistry, materials science, physics, computational modeling, and engineering. With advanced simulation tools and AI helping researchers identify promising directions, scientific discovery still depends on experimentation and testing in the lab—on getting your hands dirty and doing the hard work. “Science today is much more interdisciplinary than it was, and the collaboration across disciplines reveals new scientific opportunities,” says Stinn. Engineering can translate those opportunities into more efficient and cost-competitive metals production. A technically significant aspect of EvoMaterials is that it’s not a single process, but a technology platform. It’s accepted wisdom in Big Tech that the war against data centers must be about something bigger than just data centers themselves. Two days after the midterms, OpenAI’s chief global affairs officer, Chris Lehane, wrote that data centers “have become a proxy for public unease about affordability, corporate power, and people losing control over their futures.” It’s a culture war, in other words. But spending a few days in Data Center Alley left me wondering if it’s really the proxy that Lehane describes. I came away convinced that the data center backlash is more about electricity than Big Tech admits. The industry can improve its image, but it can’t escape the physical consequences of its enormous appetite for electricity. Here’s why. There are two ways to power the sort of data center that AI companies want: connecting to the grid, or generating electricity onsite. In Virginia’s Loudoun County, where data centers are ubiquitous but have been built at a steadier pace than the breakneck expansion now underway elsewhere in the country, most are connected to the existing grid. One facility I went inside, operated by Equinix, has massive diesel generators onsite for backup but gets its power from the local utility, Dominion Energy. Much of Dominion’s electricity is generated by natural gas (which is cleaner than coal or oil, but worse than renewables). The average data center operating in this part of Virginia today has a power capacity of 54 megawatts. That’s more than the power draw of the Denver International Airport. Elena Schlossberg, a prominent activist against data centers in Virginia, told me she’s “always banging my head against the wall” when the industry paints the fight as a culture war. “It was always about power,” she says—that is, electricity. Some Virginia protesters appear more animated by a broader anti-tech populism than Schlossberg, to be fair. But it’s the particular consequences of drawing so much power from the grid that have done the most to shape how the backlash has played out. Recent protests have led to new rules from the state that data centers must pay fully for the cost of new transmission lines to the grid and make sure they’re not spread among everyone else. Data center developers told me they’re preparing for new rules that would cut them off from grid power during peak demand. Schlossberg also worries about the downstream effects of a strained grid—for example, that data centers will run more diesel generators or that Dominion Energy will expand fossil fuel inputs rather than renewables to meet demand. The overwhelming majority of AI data centers being built elsewhere in the country are not designed this way. The scale desired is so massive that companies are setting up in rural areas where there is virtually no grid infrastructure to connect to. Building there means bringing your own power (so-called “behind the meter” power), in the form of natural-gas-powered turbines. Companies also build diesel generators for backup. Anthropic, for example, has reportedly agreed to pay $3.4 billion for gas turbines in what’s described as the largest power generation purchase by an AI company. Even if grids were nearby, they’d be unlikely to serve the amount of power demanded. The smallest data centers attractive for AI training are 300 megawatts. It’s a lot of power for one industrial site, but not unprecedented: One data center developer told me he’s eyeing a former steel mill already equipped to draw that much electricity from the grid. But the ideal facilities are more like 6,000 megawatts. That’s the estimated draw of the city of Chicago, which is powered by more than 20 power plants within the city alone (and dozens farther out). The notion of data center companies’ bringing their own power might seem attractive at first, because they wouldn’t be straining the grid. But generating electricity at this scale inevitably affects surrounding communities, and when public utilities build infrastructure of this magnitude, communities have traditionally expected a say in where it goes. Now, private companies are undertaking projects on a similar scale. Natural-gas turbines of any scale release air pollutants. When diesel generators are used, it’s even worse. And in the unprecedented rush to get these facilities operating, companies have become horrible neighbors (recall that Elon Musk violated air quality laws when he set up dozens of turbines to power a data center in Memphis, according to the Environmental Protection Agency). Big Tech’s tendency to chalk the backlash up to a culture war obfuscates one simple fact: There’s only so pleasant you can make a small city of computers to live around. Sure, companies can pursue better deals that raise tax revenues, hire local workers, and get more community involvement, as the protests are leading them to do. But if a small town is asked to welcome a Walt Disney World, an international airport, a steel plant, and the power plants to run them all, should we be surprised by its trepidation? Again, that’s not to say this is the sole reason for opposition to data centers. Fears of job losses, existential risks, and concentrated corporate power all fuel the backlash, as does misinformation (most facilities use far less water than many people think). Nor is consuming enormous amounts of electricity inherently bad: Many of the advances that have improved modern life have required just that. But as one developer recently told me, industry insiders see their ambitions as constrained less by public opposition than by a lack of electricity. That should tell us something. So what happens next? It’s possible that newly elected politicians’ actual stances on data centers won’t be as negative as the ones they campaigned on. But what seems true regardless is that if new data centers need their own power generated onsite, they’ll go where there are the fewest people able to resist them. That might mean the Permian Basin, where a network of natural-gas lines can be easy for developers to connect to and populations are sparse. Companies might also try their chances abroad; Google, in fact, announced the day after the elections that it will spend $15 billion on data center projects in Malaysia.
