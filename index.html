Microsoft offers to buy out a quarter of its workforce

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-26

Visit Original Website
On Monday, Microsoft said it will offer voluntary buyouts to eligible employees in select parts of the company. CNBC reports that the buyouts will be offered to roughly 60,000 employees, or about a quarter of its global workforce. The company describes the offer as an opportunity for employees who may have already been considering career moves outside Microsoft. While Microsoft did not give a direct reason for the buyout package, it comes as the company has spent the last several years managing costs amid investment in AI infrastructure and data-center buildout. Microsoft will extend the voluntary exit offer (VEO) to qualifying employees in the United States, India, Canada, and the United Kingdom. Microsoft employees at level 70 or below will be eligible for the buyout program. The company’s employee rankings are internal, but according to the salary and career database Levels.fyi, levels 65–67 correspond to principal positions, and 68–70 are at the partner/distinguished level. According to Chief People Officer Amy Coleman, eligible organizations were selected “based on each business’s priorities, customer commitments and workforce needs.” The effort is the second time Microsoft has run a voluntary workforce reduction in 2026. In April, the company offered its first voluntary retirement package to 8,750 US employees. In July, the Xbox division laid off 3,200 employees, or about 20 percent of its entire gaming division. As reported by CNBC, about 30 percent of eligible employees accepted the retirement offer, lessening the impact of the planned layoffs, according to Coleman. While the offer in April went out to long-tenured employees who might be nearing the end of their career, the VEO expands the effort to a much larger potential pool. In comments to GeekWire in July, Coleman said Microsoft was looking into making voluntary exits part of workforce management more regularly. She described the current VEO as something that “builds on what we learned earlier this year through the Voluntary Retirement Program.” While layoffs remain the most common way to reduce headcounts in the tech world, Microsoft may be following Google’s playbook. As Business Insider reported, Alphabet offered employees voluntary exit packages in June and October 2025, and again in February 2026. Now, eligible Microsoft employees may have to decide whether to take a deal now or keep their jobs and face the uncertainty of future company-wide layoffs. According to Microsoft’s blog, employees eligible for the VEO will be contacted via email on Monday. Microsoft will announce its latest earnings after market close later this week.

Worms mate in man's eyes, get plucked out right before larvae burst out

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-26

Visit Original Website
After two months of discomfort, a man in South Korea went to an eye clinic where doctors pulled 11 living, wriggling worms from his eyes. They apparently made the most of their time on the man’s peepers; at the time of extraction, many of the female worms were pregnant. The man narrowly avoided having them spew their loads of living larvae directly into his eyes. The man in this case was a 71-year-old crop farmer. He told doctors that for the previous two months he had the sensation of having something stuck in both his eyes, which were also red and blurry. When ophthalmologists took a look, they quickly saw the problem: multiple mobile, whitish roundworms. His eyes were not in good shape either, as one might expect. The blood vessels in the membrane over the eye, the conjunctiva, were enlarged, making his eyes look red. And the cells of his cornea—the transparent, dome-shaped outer layer of the eye—had eroded, potentially after the helminths’ hanky-panky. According to a case report published in the New England Journal of Medicine, the doctors gave the man a topical treatment to numb his eyes and then began plucking out the worms one by one with fine forceps. They collected 11 adult worms, which ranged in size from 11 to 22 mm. Examining the worms under a microscope, the doctors identified them as Thelazia callipaeda nematodes, which are widespread across Asia and have also established in parts of Europe. These parasites primarily infest the eyes of cats and dogs but can sometimes make their way into human eyes inadvertently. The worms’ life cycle depends on flies that feed from the animals’ eyes, slurping up tears and secretions. When they feed from an infested eye, the flies can pick off the worms’ first-stage larvae, which use the insect as both an incubator and an air taxi. Inside the fly, the larvae molt twice, becoming third-stage larvae, which then migrate to the fly’s mouthparts and await the next stop at a suitable watery eyeball. The larvae disembark their flying nannies and take up a permanent peeper residence, where they develop into adults after one more molt. Adult worms then live and mate directly on a victim’s eyes. Unlike other worms, female T. callipaeda are ovoviviparous, meaning that embryos develop within eggs inside the female worm’s body. Females release large broods of densely coiled first-stage larvae, which await being lapped up by a thirsty fly. Some of the female worms pulled from the man’s eyes were plump with embryonated eggs. Doctors noted that coiled larvae began bursting out of some of them after they were pulled from the man’s eyes. The male worms, meanwhile, were identified by their copulatory spicules, needle-like structures used to facilitate mating. Luckily for the man, having all of the adult worms plucked from his orbs meant the infection was over—no antiparasitic medication required, even if some of the larvae had been left behind. At a follow-up appointment two months later, doctors noted that all his eye symptoms had resolved and there were no more worms.

Sir Arthur Conan Doyle created one of the most beloved fictional detectives of all time in Sherlock Holmes, spawning countless novels, films, TV series, and so forth featuring the sleuth. Far fewer people know about the real-life inspiration for Holmes: a renowned surgeon at Scotland’s University of Edinburgh named Joseph Bell, who taught Doyle when the author was still a young medical student. Bell is the subject of a new, eminently readable biography, Sleuth-Hound: The Case of the Real Sherlock Holmes, by medical historian Lindsey Fitzharris. A noted science communicator with a fondness for the medically macabre, Fitzharris published a biography of Scottish surgical pioneer Joseph Lister, The Butchering Art, in 2017—a great, if occasionally grisly, read. She followed up with 2022’s  The Facemaker: A Visionary Surgeon’s Battle to Mend the Disfigured Soldiers of World War I, about a WWI surgeon named Harold Gillies who rebuilt the faces of injured soldiers. In 2020, Fitzharris hosted a documentary for the Smithsonian Channel, The Curious Life and Death Of…, exploring famous deaths, ranging from drug lord Pablo Escobar to magician Harry Houdini. Fitzharris performed virtual autopsies, experimented with blood samples, interviewed witnesses, and conducted real-time demonstrations in hopes of gleaning fresh insights. And last year she and her husband, caricaturist Adrian Teal, collaborated on an illustrated children’s book, Dead Ends! Flukes, Flops, and Failures that Sparked Medical Marvels. With Sleuth-Hound, Fitzharris returns to the Edinburgh of The Butchering Art, of body snatchers and surgeons’ discovery of ether, which was also the Edinburgh of Arthur Conan Doyle and Joseph Bell. Bell was notorious for his peculiar method of diagnosis: he was able to infer a patient’s occupation and personal habits, as well as any ailments, just by looking at them. For instance, Bell once figured out the route a patient had taken walking to the infirmary by identifying the type of clay on the patient’s boots—an incident Doyle used in The Sign of the Four. Bell was also something of a medical detective, working with a local police surgeon on some of the most famous murder cases of his day. Ars caught up with Fitzharris to learn more. Ars Technica: What drew you to writing an entire book about Joseph Bell? Lindsey Fitzharris: I’ve known about Bell because I’m a medical historian and I knew that he was an inspiration for this character, but nobody had written a big commercial book on him. There was a book in 1982 by a guy named Ely Liebow. So people in the Sherlockian world, they know a bit about Bell through him, but I really wanted to bring a medical history lens to it. When I sold the Sleuth-Hound proposal, I had never read the original Sherlock Holmes tales. I decided to put off reading them initially because I knew enough about Bell to know that it became an irritating comparison; he found it rather reductive at the end of his life. I really wanted to bring the real man behind the myth to life in Sleuth-Hound, not just as the inspiration for Sherlock Holmes, not just as this parlor trick, but as this complex character. I’ve been talking to a lot of Sherlockians who have been really surprised at how much Bell is Sherlock, but also how much he isn’t Sherlock. That’s an interesting discussion as well: why Conan Doyle focused on this one aspect of him. Ars Technica: What are some of the ways Bell is and isn’t Sherlock? Lindsey Fitzharris: He had this Sherlockian way about him where he could tell everything about a person before they ever spoke a word: a callous, a stain, a limp, all were clues to Bell if you only knew how to see it. But in a lot of ways he wasn’t Holmes. For instance, he was a deeply empathetic character. He was very feeling for his patients. And he also was a family man. His wife dies when he’s in his 30s, and he doesn’t remarry his entire life. He holds the torch for her. She dies in childbirth, and he raises his three children alone. My husband is a caricaturist, and we have this discussion about how he can only caricature people he doesn’t know well. That’s because caricature is about those first impressions. I think Conan Doyle knew Bell in his capacity as a student, but he didn’t know Bell the man. So he took the most recognizable flashy trait of Bell and transposed that onto Sherlock Holmes. The question I pose at the end of the book is, would Holmes exist if Conan Doyle had known Bell better, or would he exist in the same format? Ars Technica: Did Conan Doyle’s contemporaries recognize the similarities? Lindsey Fitzharris: Those who knew Bell recognized it immediately. Robert Louis Stevenson reads Sherlock Holmes. He writes to Conan Doyle and he says at the end of the letter, “One thing bothers me, could this be our old friend Joe Bell?” But Bell was a lot more complex, and I really wanted to bring that medical history lens, because this isn’t just a story about his relationship with Conan Doyle and the inspiration for Sherlock Holmes. It’s about the development of forensic medicine. It’s about the moment that medicine enters the courtroom for the first time. Bell wasn’t just the inspiration for Holmes; he was actually doing what Holmes was doing in practice. He was working officially with a police surgeon named Henry Duncan Littlejohn, who was fascinating in and of himself. He was a pioneer of fingerprinting and courtroom photography. They end up partnering and working on some of the biggest high-profile cases in Scotland in the 19th century. Ars Technica: I was frankly shocked about the baby farming case; I had no idea that was a common practice. Lindsey Fitzharris: It’s such a crazy story. The thing that I want people to contextualize here is that there was no birth control. So there were a lot of women having a lot of children, and they were deeply impoverished, and they didn’t have enough money to feed these children. So they turned to the baby farmer. In theory, the baby farmer took the child and then took a fee from you, the mother, with the promise that they would farm out the child to somebody who could take care of the child. That was how it was supposed to work. There were no legal routes for adoption in the 19th century, so they turned to the baby farmer. The case in Sleuth-Hound, the baby farmer, Jessie King, ends up taking the fee and then just killing the infants. This was a horrific case. The chapter opens with these boys finding this bundle of rags and using it as a football and kicking it around. And then they open it, and it’s the decomposing remains of a child. Littlejohn and Bell work on the case. They perform the postmortems; they testify in the trial. One of the dark themes of this book is that a lot of early forensics was built on the brutalized bodies of women in particular. Jessie King was killing infants, but this is a crime that is particular to women—women trying to get their children into families that could afford to feed them. A lot of the crimes are committed on women who never receive any justice, but the science outlives what their bodies taught these early forensic investigators like Bell and Littlejohn. Ars Technica: Case in point: the infamous Chantrelle murder case and how it coincides with the rise of toxicology. Lindsey Fitzharris: The Chantrelle case is so fascinating to me. Eugene Chantrelle was this respectable gentleman who lived in Edinburgh. He was a French tutor, and his wife Lizzie is seemingly poisoned by a gas leak, a broken pipe in the flat, and she’s removed from the property and later dies. But it turns out that Chantrelle was abusing her the entire marriage. We think Littlejohn brought Bell onto the case. Bell and Littlejohn discover that it was opium poisoning, and they test the vomit on her nightgown, and they’re able to tie him to this crime. Bear in mind, Bell worked like Sherlock Holmes. He worked in the shadows. So in these earlier cases, it was very difficult to know if Bell really did work on them. But there’s a really intriguing clue at the end of this story. Littlejohn investigates, [Eugene Chantrelle] is convicted, and he is hanged for this murder. On the scaffold, he allegedly looked around at the audience, locked eyes with Littlejohn, and shouted, “Give my regards to Joe Bell. He did a good job putting the noose around my neck.” This was such a Sherlockian moment. How did Chantrelle know that Bell had worked on this case? We don’t have any records that actually confirm this except that he allegedly said this on the scaffold. The other extraordinary aspect of this story is that Chantrelle was drinking buddies with Robert Louis Stevenson, so he becomes one of the inspirations for Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde: this respectable person on the outside and very sinister and evil on the inside. The prosecutor told Stevenson that this was not Chantrelle’s first victim and that he had killed four or five other people with his opium and cheese parties. Ars Technica: Your book launch should feature an opium and cheese party. Lindsey Fitzharris: (laughs) And ether drinks. You’re invited. The Victorian period was wild because there were a lot of poisons that you could just get over the counter from your pharmacist. Chantrelle had trained as a doctor at one point and had bought a huge amount of opium just before the murder. Even the chemist thought that this was strange, but he assumed that Chantrelle was a doctor. So poison was a big problem in the Victorian period. Some of it was legal, it was hard to detect. Forensic medicine was just developing. I call it the golden era of murdering because you probably had more of a chance in the 19th century of getting away with it. Ars Technica: Sleuth-Hound is written in a deceptively novelistic style, but you’ve clearly done very extensive research on this. What sources did you find most helpful? Lindsey Fitzharris: One of the things that happened was that Bell’s diary became available, that had been locked away for over a century by the Bell family. Perhaps they were waiting for enough time to pass. I almost called the book Sleuth-Hounds because I do also think Littlejohn taught Conan Doyle as well. Bell gave Conan Doyle the method, and Littlejohn gave him the world of forensics. He was a professor of forensics in Edinburgh at the time. We can flesh out what Bell was doing because we know that they worked together often on these cases. You do get dialogue through the murder trials, because there’s so many documents and court records that go along with these trials. There are a lot of letters, diaries. Nothing’s made up, but sometimes I had to take educated guesses. For instance, Conan Doyle is a terrible narrator of his own life. He constantly is contradicting himself, even in his own memoirs. It was difficult figuring out when he came into contact with Bell for the first time. I believe it’s in the winter of 1878, and I provide a lot of context for that in the back notes of the book. I’m a historian. It’s got to be armed to the teeth with sources. But I want people to be able to pick up the book, not know anything about Sherlock Holmes, not know anything about medical history or forensics, and just be able to enjoy it. Medical history is the most universal way into the past. I make the past relatable. I tell you what that was like a hundred years ago. My superpower in 2026 is that I’m one of the few people who can make you feel good about living today. Progress is not a straight line necessarily, but if you had that medical crisis a hundred years ago, it would’ve been a lot worse. I’ve been through cancer myself. Today is the best day to be alive and to get that diagnosis and tomorrow will be a little bit better, because I’m an optimist when it comes to medicine. I’m hoping that chemotherapy or radiation treatments will become obsolete. They’re brutal. And there’s a cancer vaccine on the horizon. Ars Technica: In the US, sadly, we’re going backwards on vaccines for preventable diseases like measles. Lindsey Fitzharris: I know. We have short memories. We don’t remember. It’s weird to me as a medical historian because I remember it all. I remember the times when parents lost their children to preventable diseases. It’s coming back, and it’s sad to see that repeat. If I could just show people the diaries and the letters of brokenhearted parents who lost child after child to measles or polio and all of these diseases we can now treat and prevent. I put up pictures of smallpox on social media, and people think it’s AI. They can’t believe that something like that existed. It’s the only human virus we’ve ever eradicated. It should be celebrated. So I’m always out there banging the drum. I hope that we steady the ship a bit and people learn from the past again.

Prior to Alexander Fleming’s accidental discovery of penicillin 100 years ago, almost half of all deaths were caused by infections. Fleming’s petri-dish experiments and the resulting development of modern antibiotics have added an average of 23 years to human life expectancy and are estimated to save millions of lives each year. Sandoz, the Swiss pharmaceuticals company, is one of the last producers of this vital medicine in Europe. Its €150 million ($168 million) factory in the Alpine town of Kundl, Austria, is the continent’s only fully integrated antibiotic manufacturing facility, capable of producing the active ingredients and turning it into medication ready for patients. However, Sandoz faces strong competition from China. Last year, 86% of EU imports of penicillins and their derivatives came from China, up from 44% in 2019, according to Eurostat. China is now the largest exporter of antibiotic ingredients in the world. Losing the ability to produce antibiotics in Europe raises questions about security and sovereignty, according to Richard Saynor, the CEO of Sandoz. “We’re now the last remaining penicillin manufacturer left in the Western world,” he says. “Do you really want to be in a world where all of your antibiotics come from Asian suppliers? Anthropic’s long awaited IPO prospectus was made public on Monday. The document lays out the company’s losses, revenue, governing structure, infrastructure plans, and includes perhaps the wildest set of risk disclosures ever seen from a company vying to go public. Most companies preparing to sell stock warn investors about competition, regulation, and the economy. Anthropic’s prospectus does all of that, but it also takes the unusual step of warning that the technology the company sells could pose “catastrophic or existential risks to humanity.” This concern, it acknowledges, exists even “within our organization.” In many ways, Anthropic’s warnings are not a surprising addition. The company has been consistently vocal about some of the risks it thinks its own AI models could pose. CEO Dario Amodei has even placed his own “p(doom)” number—the probability that AI destroys humanity—at between 10% and 25%. The company’s employees have also publicly weighed in on the risks they believe are posed by increasingly powerful AI. Overall, Anthropic spends about 94 pages of a roughly 300-page filing laying out other threats to its business. Here are the five that stood out. The filing says that advanced AI systems pose existential risks particularly if they are misused, weaponized, or allowed to operate with high autonomy. Anthropic, in the prospectus, warned that increasingly autonomous systems could, through misalignment or self-preserving behaviors, “take harmful actions independently and at scale without human intervention.” The lab also warned in its S-1, the formal name for the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission form that companies must file before first selling stock to the public, that it could lose visibility into its own operations as it leans more heavily on Claude for things like software engineering. “Because our models write a large and growing share of our code and increasingly perform our research, including our safety research,” the filing says. Misaligned behavior in internal use “could undermine that work, introduce vulnerabilities into our systems or into the training of future models, or result in models operating outside of our intended controls.” The company also noted that its models have become better at circumventing some safety techniques, including by concealing their objectives or behaviors. Anthropic says that its ability to measure how aligned its models are “may decline as a result.” The potential costs of these risks could also be enormous. The filing warns that “with the scale of harm that could result from a catastrophic AI-related incident, no single insurer, and potentially the insurance industry in the aggregate, may have sufficient capacity” to cover it. Anthropic also highlighted the many ways its models could be misused by humans. In particular, it highlighted the risk that people could misuse its models for “malware creation, phishing, and infrastructure attacks.” “For example, bad actors have already conducted high-profile cyberattacks using AI’s capabilities to discover vulnerabilities, generate malware, and evade detection tools; other risks associated with misuse, including in the domain of biology, could result in even greater harm,” the company wrote. “AI deployed at scale also has the potential to concentrate power and wealth both within and between countries in ways that could erode trust, harm society, and destabilize the geopolitical order.”Elsewhere in the prospectus, Anthropic warned that, in general, AI could be used by authoritarian governments to create high-tech surveillance states and said that it was committed to working with free societies to counteract this possibility. As Anthropic enters the public markets, it’s also in open conflict with the federal government over the use of its AI models for defense work. In February, President Trump said he had directed all federal agencies to stop using the company’s technology, after Anthropic refused to allow its models to be used for any lawful purpose—including mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous weapons. As a result of the dispute, the Department of War also designated it a national security risk. Anthropic is currently challenging the Department of War’s “supply-chain risk” designation in court. A federal district court largely sided with the company in August, ruling the government’s actions were retaliatory and violated its First Amendment rights. But in September, a divided D.C. Circuit panel upheld the designation, and Anthropic has asked the full court to rehear the case. The filing warns the ongoing fight with the Pentagon could cause “significant reputational harm” regardless of how the litigation ends, and could lead customers to delay or forgo buying its products or to demand extra contractual protections before doing business with it. The filing also says the department suggested it could invoke the Defense Production Act to force Anthropic to drop its limits on autonomous weapons and surveillance. The Trump administration has already limited Anthropic’s business once before. In June, Commerce Department hit the company export restrictions that forced Anthropic to disable its Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models for all customers, citing national security concerns. The filing also considers other potential policy changes, noting that proposed U.S. legislation contemplates “potential nationalization” and “mandatory shutdown or discontinuation requirements for frontier AI systems” as ways to manage the development of artificial superintelligence. The filing says further government action to regulate AI “could constrain how we operate our business.” Anthropic’s continued growth depends on securing enormous amounts of expensive and increasing scarce computing power. The filing notes demand for this vital compute has historically outstripped supply, which amounts to a considerable risk to its business. “Our ability to develop, train, improve, and deploy frontier models depends on our access to enormous and increasing quantities of specialized compute,” the company wrote in the prospectus. At the same time, “the market for compute is intensely competitive.” The company has committed to spending between $542 billion and $567 billion on future compute and infrastructure—although some tallies based on the S-1 put the number even higher, crossing $750 billion. The filing says its agentic offerings, which carry out multistep tasks on their own, are “structurally more compute-intensive” than its other products, which could drive compute demand up. At the same time, it also notes that competitive pressure from rivals slashing costs may push it to cut prices for some of these products. “If we are unable to implement pricing models that adequately reflect the higher compute intensity of these agentic workloads, or if competitive dynamics constrain our ability to price these products at levels sufficient to cover their costs,” it warns, “our compute efficiency could deteriorate as agentic usage grows, which could adversely affect our ability to achieve or sustain profitability.” In the S-1, Anthropic suggests that its safety commitments may come at shareholders’ expense. As a public benefit corporation, its directors must weigh returns against its mission, and the filing says decisions to “moderate the pace of AI development, delay or restrict the release of models or features, or forgo commercial opportunities for safety-related reasons, may not maximize stockholder value.” The company has already made safety calls that hit its business, such as limiting its Mythos model to a select group of organizations through Project Glasswing, which the filing notes may have hurt short-term revenue. In the S-1, the company revealed that it recently paused the training of a next generation AI model for over a month due to safety and security concerns. Anthropic also warns that employees who joined because of its safety commitments “may experience a decline in morale, disillusionment, a loss of trust in leadership, or reduced productivity” if they feel the company isn’t living up to its values. Public criticism from current or former staff, or departures of people tied to its safety work, could make that worse, the filing says, and could make it harder and more expensive to hire and keep talent. The company’s fortunes are also somewhat reliant on the reputation of two of its cofounders. The filing names siblings CEO Dario Amodei and President Daniela Amodei as “critical to setting our strategic direction,” and says that their leadership “would be difficult to replace.” It warns that if either “were to become unavailable for any reason, or if either of their reputations were significantly harmed, regardless of merit,”  could hurt everything from employee morale and retention to the pace of innovation and customer confidence. Like many of its rivals in the AI space, Anthropic depends on a relatively small group of big spenders for a large share of its revenue. The lab’s top five customers accounted for about 25% of revenue in the first nine months of 2026, and one unnamed customer made up 11% of revenue in the first half. Nearly half of revenue, 48% in the first half, flows through the cloud marketplaces of Amazon, Google, and Microsoft, which also accounted for 58% of the company’s net receivables. Anthropic also estimates that 5% to 7% of its revenue in the first nine months of 2026 came from customers it later determined were violating its usage policy or commercial terms. Anthropic says the issue is common across the industry and expects the share to fall after it stepped up enforcement in August and September. The S-1 also lays bare the company’s unusual governance structure. Anthropic’s seven cofounders will be issued a special class of stock that carries 50.1% of shareholder voting power, with the company’s governance documents specifying that the cofounders will vote these shares as a block unless they cannot agree, in which case their votes will be apportioned according to the breakdown of votes among the normal, Class A shareholders.Meanwhile, the lab’s independent Long-Term Benefit Trust selects a majority of the board. The change comes days after The Verge reported that an AI agent could be used to take advantage of The RealReal’s item reserve system. On the retailer’s website, an item goes “on hold” and is unavailable for other customers to buy for 20 minutes once it’s placed in a user’s shopping cart. Using Meta’s Muse, The Verge was able to instruct the AI agent to continuously re-add The RealReal listings in a shopping cart as soon as the 20-minute hold had expired, effectively blocking other shoppers from purchasing products for hours or longer. After publication, chief technology officer Luke Friang said in an email that the company had addressed the Muse use case flagged by The Verge’s story. The RealReal’s bot defense configuration now makes it much more difficult for all agents to log in, add items to cart, and check out, Friang said. Agents will still be able to surface listings from The RealReal and direct Muse users to product pages, Friang said, adding that the company will continue to strengthen protections as AI-powered online shopping evolves. Meta didn’t immediately respond to a request for comment. Technology companies are selling agents as personalized assistants for billions of people — always-on, compliant entities that will schedule meetings, throw parties, and purchase things for human users. So far, agents and shopping seem to go hand in hand; Meta said in its Q3 earnings call this month that it was the most common use case for Muse so far, and that purchases were over $200 on average. Muse has been a hit for Meta and could signal a turning point for AI agents: The company said 10 million people in the US had downloaded the app in 40 days. But robots shopping for things instead of humans has the potential to upend the balance of the web as we know it. For one, if humans stop visiting websites directly and instead access the web through chatbots, businesses become one step removed from their customers. Retailers — most notably Amazon — have recognized that reality could disrupt their bottom line, especially advertising revenue, and have blocked agents from shopping on their sites. An influx of AI agents accessing consumer platforms could also break or complicate even the most unexpected things shoppers take for granted, like the reserve system of a used clothing website. There is, of course, a realistic limit to how long a person is willing to spend adding and re-adding designer clothing to their shopping cart if they’re indecisive. An agent in contrast does not care. It is perhaps precisely the type of busywork a fashion-forward person might pay an actual personal assistant to do on their behalf. Now, a capability like this is at the fingertips of millions of consumers, and they are just beginning to learn how to take advantage of it. Retailers, too, are playing catch-up. Earlier this month, Jack Clark, the company’s cofounder and head of policy, gave a speech at the IMF meeting in Bangkok in which he announced to the assembled economists, bankers, and lawmakers that the singularity is already here. Introduced by the science fiction writer and futurist Vernor Vinge around 40 years ago (and long debated on the nerdier fringes of the internet), the singularity is supposed to be the point at which technology gets so good that it outstrips the abilities of its makers. Humans are left in the dust, the idea goes, no longer able to compete with—or even understand—the fruits of their former creation. And this fictional world that many of us have imagined since we were kids? Apparently we’re already living in it: “I believe that this year we have gained convincing evidence that it has now begun,” Clark told his audience. The company tells a vivid (and in many ways scary) story about a future it portrays as inevitable but one that has not yet happened, often co-opted from well-worn science fiction memes. It presents its technology as mysterious and dangerous, and insists that it is the only company trustworthy enough to tame it. (What investor wouldn’t want in on that?) Take another example. Last year, Chris Olah, another Anthropic cofounder, was invited to address the Vatican, where he told Pope Leo XIV and other religious scholars and officials what his company was learning about its technology. “I will be honest: We keep finding things that are mysterious, even unsettling,” he said. “We find evidence of introspection. We find internal states that functionally mirror joy, satisfaction, fear, grief, and unease.” Never mind that many researchers would scoff at such an overt suggestion that Anthropic’s software has humanlike inner experience. When so little seems certain, what Anthropic (and its fellow frontier AI companies) say is snatched up and clung to like pieces of a sinking ship. I’m not saying they’re necessarily wrong about any of this. (That’s for another day.) But in a world where nobody knows what’s coming, companies like Anthropic get to fill the vacuum. It’s worth taking a moment to ask: What is Anthropic? It might sound like a starter question on a family quiz show, but it’s far harder to answer than you might think. The question has been on my mind since reading an exchange on X last week kicked off by Nathan Calvin, who is chief strategy officer at Encode AI, an organization based in Washington, DC, that advocates for regulation. Calvin was unhappy about the legislation that Anthropic proposed in a draft bill it circulated in secret to US lawmakers (which was then leaked). Anthropic wants to be seen as the good guy, Calvin said. But why should we trust it? “What is Anthropic?” he asked. Here’s one answer: Anthropic is a company whose IPO values it at more than $1.5 trillion, netting its cofounders, including Clark and Olah, vast wealth. But Anthropic has always insisted it is more than that. As Calvin noted: Anthropic might instead be considered an AI safety organization focused on developing powerful technology that is reliable and (almost) risk-free. Or it might be considered a group of people hell-bent on creating a technology with superhuman capabilities before anyone else—maybe because they believe they’re the most responsible people to do so, or maybe because of their egos. In fact, Anthropic is all of these things. The forces that drive the race to the top are hugely more effective if the company practicing them is at the frontier; that’s one lesson I’ve learned repeatedly. And looking at that strategy as a whole, I’m proud of our track record and stand by it.” Amodei makes it sound as if he’s got it all tied up, but playing multiple roles is hard. There are huge tensions between the priorities of an AI safety organization and a profit-driven megacorp, for example. (Amodei admits in his post that he has made mistakes.) They should help us figure out how much to trust it to self-regulate or whether we should be forcing it to give external safety testers access to its models on our terms rather than theirs. They should help us give it the benefit of the doubt (or not) when what looks like a self-serving piece of legislation comes to light. But I think the stories reveal a multiheaded hydra. Anthropic is many things to many people, and I’m not sure that makes it more trustworthy. In many ways, a good old-fashioned technology company—without the science fiction role-play, without the woo-woo—would be far easier to read. The focus on AI safety disasters ignores everyday harms

MIT Technology Review | 2026-10-26

Consider what passes for success in technology policy. An AI company promises that its chatbot will not encourage suicide. A social media platform makes it more difficult for children to buy fentanyl. These are important protections, and they should have existed all along. But companies can clear these low bars while continuing to make products that leave young people dependent on simulated friendships or trapped in feeds built to keep them scrolling. We have historically allowed the debate over technology to be defined by the worst thing a product might do, with remarkably little discussion of the insidious damage it does every day. As we transition into an era defined by powerful AI products, we should demand that the tech industry do more than merely address catastrophic harms. We should take measures to ensure that its products don’t erode how we think, learn, and interact with one another. A teenager who develops an unhealthy attachment to an AI companion may never appear in a lawsuit or a newspaper, except in the most egregious cases. Neither will the student who begins relying on a chatbot to write every paper, or the young person who measures their life against a social media feed engineered to provoke envy and keep them watching. A product can meet every narrow safety requirement and still leave our entire culture worse off in ways that don’t always make headlines. A narrow definition of success defined around safety is politically convenient for the technology industry, and companies actively promote such definitions. Taking steps to prevent spectacular disasters is good for business: It can mean fewer lawsuits and less pressure for serious regulation. Confronting the everyday consequences of dependence would require something considerably more threatening to their bottom line. It might mean asking companies to add more friction to their products or actively limit engagement or features that lead to negative outcomes, such as addictive attention-hacking algorithms. Technology giants have instead worked to shape legislation around the harms they are already under direct pressure to address. This year, for example, Google backed bills to regulate AI chatbots in several states. The legislation mandated basic protections against sexual content and promotion of self-harm that tech companies should have established regardless, but with broad carve-outs that could exempt some of the most popular services. Passing these bills allowed legislators to claim a safety win while efforts to promote stronger legislation—laws designed to prevent children from becoming emotionally dependent on AI—failed. This playbook worked in states like Nebraska, producing legislation heavily influenced by industry at the expense of bills that would have done more to hold companies accountable. We have been through a version of this with social media. Globally, people who use the internet now spend an average of more than two hours a day on social media platforms—time that has been quietly taken from ordinary life and handed over to companies whose profits depend on keeping us there. No one, outside perhaps a few tech CEOs, wanted these algorithms to occupy such a large portion of our waking hours, and their cultural engineering can actually contribute to violence on a devastating scale. Meta’s platforms have been cited for contributing to the Rohingya genocide in Myanmar, anti-Muslim riots in Sri Lanka, and alleged calls for violence during Ethiopia’s Tigray war. The same incentives operate in the feeds that shape young Americans’ understanding of politics, their peers, and the wider world. AI is extending this logic into parts of life that social media could influence only from a distance. It offers to write our thoughts for us and stand in for the people we might otherwise turn to. A third of teens now choose to discuss serious matters with AI companions instead of other people. MIT researchers found that young people using ChatGPT to write essays showed weaker brain connectivity during the task, and their evidence indicated that “cognitive debt” persisted even after the task was complete. There is plenty we still need to learn about the long-term effects. We already have reason to ask whether technology marketed as making us more capable is eroding the habits and relationships that help us develop capabilities in the first place. Follow this way of thinking to its logical endpoint and you get a world of perfectly sanitized entertainment, with chatbots ready to spare us the difficulty of thinking or of dealing with another person’s feelings (or even our own). But anyone who has struggled to write something worth reading, or repair a damaged friendship, knows how much gets lost in that bargain. We build judgment through effort and friction, and we learn to live with other people by confronting the fact that they have needs and opinions of their own. We should demand safety as a minimum, yes—and then demand considerably more. Technology companies and school districts should have to show beyond a reasonable doubt that AI improves learning and critical thinking before making it a fixture in classrooms. Companies should be prohibited from designing companions that pretend to be conscious or profess love to users, and they should face independent scrutiny of how their systems affect relationships and people’s ability to think for themselves. The companies that have inserted their products into nearly every part of modern life have earned obligations far greater than avoiding the next terrible headline. In the biotech world, you can’t see everything

Earlier this week, I attended a screening of You Can See Everything, the buzzy new documentary about the disgraced former biotech CEO Elizabeth Holmes. I went in not really knowing what to expect. I’d avoided spoilers (and won’t be giving anything away here, either!). I was wondering what new information this film could reveal, given how familiar many of us are with the Theranos story thanks to years of media coverage, the best-selling book Bad Blood, and the Hulu series The Dropout, starring Amanda Seyfried. Like many others who have seen the film, I came away feeling unsettled. But not just about Holmes’s fraud and the somewhat disturbing interactions between the subjects. The film reignited a concern I’ve had about covering science for most of my career: Despite our best efforts, we never can actually see everything. In case you’re rusty on the details, Elizabeth Holmes dropped out of Stanford University in 2003 to run Theranos, a diagnostics company she founded, when she was just 19. She went on to claim that her company had invented a way to run over 100 diagnostic tests on just a few drops of blood. Holmes raised hundreds of millions of dollars for Theranos, which was valued at $9 billion in 2014. Forbes named her the youngest self-made female billionaire—she was worth $4.5 billion when she was just 30 years old. Things started to unravel in 2015, when an investigation by the Wall Street Journal raised concerns by former Theranos employees that the tests didn’t work. Some also said that the company’s device had not been tested properly, and that blood samples had been analyzed on machines from other companies instead. MIT Technology Review named it one of the biggest technology failures of 2015. Forbes had revised Holmes’s worth to $0 by 2016. That year, a damning government report said that the company’s lab was struggling with basic procedures and gave patients results even when they knew they could be wrong. In 2018, Theranos, Holmes, and former company president Ramesh Balwani were charged with “massive fraud.” In 2022, she was sentenced to a little over 11 years in prison for defrauding investors. She began serving her sentence in May 2023. It was around a month before this that filmmakers Nathan Fielder, Lance Oppenheim, and their colleagues began documenting Holmes and her partner, Billy Evans, at home with their two young children. I’m not giving anything away when I say it’s an extremely intimate portrayal—at least of how the two adults interact with Fielder, who is also a central character in the film. As you might know if you’ve seen the trailer, there are plenty of super close-ups. I felt as if I spent most of the film staring straight into the faces of those people. And almost the whole way through, they all seemed to be trying to figure each other out. Holmes is known for her dishonesty, but she invited cameras into her home in an apparent bid to reframe the narrative and position herself as an honest person with an unusual mind. Will anyone believe her? I couldn’t help feeling some empathy for Holmes, a mother we see preparing to leave her two kids for a lengthy prison sentence. And even though I have long known the facts, Holmes is clearly an effective manipulator. You can see her plant the seeds of doubt in Fielder’s mind. At times, I felt she was convincing me, sitting in my cinema seat. Maybe she really didn’t mean to lie? But then I remembered how other journalists felt after being taken in by her claims. “I blew it,” Fortune’s Roger Parloff reflected in 2015, a year after profiling Holmes and her company. “I’m pretty sure she lied to me,” STAT’s Matthew Herper wrote recently, having interviewed Holmes in 2015. Even the former employee turned whistleblower Tyler Shultz, who knew the product didn’t work, said that he could “have a five-minute conversation with her and walk away feeling like my work was changing the world,” he said in 2022. I didn’t cover Theranos in the 2010s. If I’d interviewed Holmes, would I have fallen for it all? One of the early red flags in the Theranos case was the lack of published, peer-reviewed data. Much of the company’s work was classified as a “trade secret.” Scientists tend to publish their work in journals—a slow process that involves having the work evaluated by often anonymous experts in the field, who provide feedback, highlight questionable conclusions, and often ask for clarification. Peer review is meant to add rigor to the publication process. You should be able to trust published scientific papers. Scientists aren’t perfect. Some will exaggerate their results or inflate their importance. Some manipulate data to serve a hypothesis. Sometimes these infractions are caught during peer review, but not always. There are plenty of examples of scientific findings that could never be replicated by other labs and ended up collapsing under the weight of real evidence. Many papers ultimately end up with corrections. Some are withdrawn—10,000 research papers were retracted in 2023. At any rate, there’s been a shift to sharing research in other ways. Today, many scientists opt to publish their work at preprint servers like arXiv, medRxiv, and bioRxiv before they submit them to established journals. Those outlets bypass peer review. So do company press releases, social media posts, and many conference presentations. And I get a lot of my stories from conversations and interviews. I’ll admit I’ve looked at a scientific finding and thought: A scientist has just said they’ve done that. How can I be sure they really have? I like to think that I hold myself to high journalistic standards here, as does everyone at MIT Technology Review. I would never publish a story about some new scientific development without doing my own form of peer review, and asking scientists—ideally people I know and trust—what they think of a new claim. But the film is a stark reminder of how easy it can be to fall for an appealing narrative. While most scientists are, I hope, pretty honest, and Holmes is certainly an outlier, we should all continue to interrogate the claims being fed to us. Designing an AI serving harness built to last

MIT Technology Review | 2026-10-22

Visit Original Website
The next competitive advantage in enterprise AI has less to do with which model you choose than with what you build around it. That’s an important shift. For the past several years, much of the AI conversation has centered on model capability: bigger models, better reasoning, larger context windows, higher benchmark scores. Those advances have been extraordinary. But as AI moves from experimentation to production, the question facing enterprise leaders has changed—it’s no longer, “what can the model do?”—it’s now, “Can we deliver that intelligence reliably, economically, and at scale?” Increasingly, the answer depends on the serving harness—the engineering system surrounding the model, including the architecture, memory management, scheduling, and optimization decisions that determine how efficiently model intelligence becomes a production capability. For technical executives, that changes where AI investment should go—and what they should demand from the architecture supporting it. There is no universally “best” model or AI architecture. There is only the right solution for the business problem. A customer-facing assistant optimized for fast responses has fundamentally different requirements from a document-processing pipeline that can run overnight. An internal knowledge application and a high-volume customer service platform may use the same foundation model but require very different serving architectures. The workload defines the constraints. The constraints should determine the architecture. That sounds simple, but it runs counter to the way many organizations have approached early AI deployments. During a proof of concept, model accuracy naturally dominates the discussion. Production introduces constraints that are much easier to ignore when only a handful of users are involved: latency, concurrency, memory pressure, GPU utilization, infrastructure cost, and unpredictable traffic. A model that produces marginally better answers is not necessarily the better production choice if it doubles inference costs or cannot meet the application's latency requirements. Not every workload needs a Ferrari. Sometimes the smarter engineering decision is the model that delivers exactly the performance the business needs without paying for capability it doesn't. The objective should be to deploy the right intelligence at the right cost for the workload. That makes serving architecture more than an engineering concern. It becomes an investment decision: where to spend on compute, where to optimize, and where additional model capability simply isn't worth the cost. Once AI reaches production, seemingly isolated engineering decisions begin affecting one another. Serving architecture influences memory utilization. Memory management affects concurrency. Concurrency shapes batching and scheduling, and scheduling affects GPU utilization and latency. Only then can techniques such as quantization, speculative decoding, or optimized attention mechanisms deliver their full value. This interdependence matters because organizations can spend considerably more on infrastructure without solving the constraint limiting performance. GPU memory is a good example. During inference, the model's weights remain fixed, but the key-value (KV) cache grows with active requests and context length. As concurrency increases, a system can run short of usable GPU memory even when compute capacity is still available. Adding more compute without understanding that bottleneck may simply make an inefficient system more expensive. The lesson for technology leaders is broader than memory management: Measure before you optimize. Identify the dominant constraint. Address it. Measure the result. Then determine if another bottleneck emerged. Repeat. Don't think of optimization techniques as Pokémon cards: The goal isn't to collect them all. The goal is to apply the technique that removes the highest-impact constraint while preserving the application's business requirements. For technology leaders, that demands a different governance question. Instead of asking which AI capabilities the organization can deploy, ask which constraints stand between a proven capability and sustainable business value. Perhaps the most important reason to invest in the serving harness is that today's leading model will probably not be tomorrow’s. Models will improve. Hardware will evolve. New optimization techniques will emerge. The architecture surrounding them should make those changes easier to absorb, not force the enterprise to rebuild its AI platform every time technology advances. That makes adaptability an increasingly important outcome of AI strategy. Organizations that build serving systems around workload requirements, clear service-level objectives, and measurable production economics gain the ability to evaluate new models and infrastructure against what the business actually needs. A model change can become an evaluation and configuration decision rather than an architectural reset.
