Ukraine’s drone strikes spur hasty Russian plans for underground data centers

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-03

Russian tech companies are scrambling to harden and hide their data centers underground after Ukrainian drone strikes damaged facilities housing AI supercomputers and disrupted cloud services for millions of Russian users. Meanwhile, the Kremlin is requiring data center owners to reserve computing capacity for emergency state needs and has even authorized possible use of foreign data centers. These consequences of Russia’s war against Ukraine come as the Russian military has bombarded Ukrainian cities with drones and missiles nearly night and day in recent months, along with specifically targeting Ukrainian data centers since late September. But Ukraine has retaliated with its own drone strikes on military and infrastructure targets across Russia, degrading or knocking out data centers operated by Russian tech giant Yandex, the state-owned bank Sberbank, social media site VKontakte, and the Russian Ministry of Finance. Now, state-owned telecommunications company Rostelcom has raised the possibility of moving some Russian data centers underground in talks with the nickel and palladium mining company Norilsk, according to Russian newspaper Vedomosti. The industry publication Data Center Dynamics also pointed to a recent proposal by a Russian regional governor to build data centers within open-pit coal mines while planting green public parks above as camouflage. On November 2, Putin also signed a decree ordering Russian data center operators to set aside 10 percent of available space and capacity in case the Russian government requires it, according to independent Russian news publication Meduza. That may indicate growing Kremlin concerns about the vulnerability of Russian cloud computing infrastructure during its war with Ukraine. Putin’s decree also allows a special Russian government commission to authorize use of foreign data centers in consultation with Russia’s Federal Security Service. That marks a notable policy change because Putin and the Russian parliament previously enacted Russia’s “Sovereign Internet Law,” which has blocked Russian companies from using cloud computing infrastructure outside Russia’s borders. Russia’s push for a sovereign and increasingly closed Internet has resulted in a highly centralized system under a few Russian tech companies like Yandex. That hyper-centralized system has now proven vulnerable during wartime, as Ukrainian drone strikes on key Russian data centers created widespread disruptions for popular payment and digital banking apps, e-commerce platforms, video streaming services, and ride-hailing and food delivery apps, among others. Yandex has also been forced to relocate all three of its main supercomputers after Ukrainian drone strikes targeted the AI data centers where they were originally housed, Meduza reported. By comparison, Ukraine’s decentralized digital services have proven much more resilient despite years of Russian drone and missile attacks, especially as the government has taken more steps to disperse crucial energy and telecommunications nodes and bury more supporting infrastructure underground. A big boost came from US tech giants Amazon and Microsoft, which helped Ukraine migrate government resources and other crucial digital services to cloud regions elsewhere in Europe shortly after Russia launched its full-scale invasion in February 2022. The Ukrainian government has also used SpaceX’s Starlink satellite terminals—paid for by the United States and European countries such as Poland and Germany—as a backup communications system.

The New York Times and other newspaper companies sued Brave Software yesterday, alleging that the browser maker illegally bypasses paywalls and sells stolen news content to AI companies. “Brave is the supplier, dealer, and distributor of stolen copyrighted content for its own users and many of the most prominent GenAI companies, including Anthropic, Perplexity, Mistral, xAI, and Cohere,” the NYT alleged in a lawsuit filed in US District Court for the Southern District of New York. Citing third-party revenue estimates, the suit claimed that “Brave has made more than $100 million by copying and reselling publishers’ work without consent or permission,” while “publishers have received nothing.” The lawsuit said, “AI companies pay Brave to provide them with real-time online content from the Brave search index,” and use content from the Brave index “to train their own AI models” and “to respond to specific user queries.” The NYT lawsuit points to Brave’s website, which says the Brave Search API is “used by some of the biggest names in AI, including Cohere, Mistral, Perplexity, and You.com.” The lawsuit accused Brave of all the major types of copyright infringement: direct infringement, contributory infringement, and vicarious infringement. The lawsuit claims Brave infringes copyrights directly by scraping articles for its search index, for its “Ask Brave” AI search feature, and for the Brave API that “serves as an illicit source of unlicensed copyrighted material, including Times journalism, to other AI companies.” The NYT accused Brave of being “a contributory and vicarious infringer when it deliberately induces, facilitates, controls, and profits from its users’ and GenAI customers’ copying of the copyrighted content.” Finally, the lawsuit said Brave violated the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) by circumventing technological measures the NYT and other content owners use to restrict access to their articles. “The Brave Browser includes a default setting that, when activated, causes the browser to go behind website paywalls and provide the paywalled content to the user,” the lawsuit said. The browser “blocks advertising that would otherwise appear on The Times’s website, and instead shows its own ads, offering its users a cut of the revenue even as it deprives The Times of digital advertising revenue that helps it fund the expensive effort of producing journalism,” the lawsuit said. When testing the Brave browser for Mac today, we found that the optional “paywall blocker” feature made it possible to read a paywalled New York Times article. The feature was enabled in the default settings and can be turned off. The NYT asked for a permanent injunction to stop Brave from continuing the allegedly illegal conduct. The lawsuit seeks statutory damages, actual damages, restitution of profits derived from alleged copyright infringement, and “restitution and disgorgement of all revenue and profits Brave obtained through its ad-substitution scheme.” A similar lawsuit was filed against Brave in the same court yesterday by the New York Daily News, Chicago Tribune, and Orlando Sentinel companies. We contacted Brave and will update this article if it provides a response. Perplexity, which is not a defendant in the Brave lawsuit but is facing a different lawsuit filed by the NYT, accused the news organization of “disinformation” in a statement provided to Ars and other news outlets. Perplexity’s statement did not identify any specific inaccuracies in the lawsuit against Brave. “The New York Times is overpaying its attorneys and underpaying its journalists. (Who could’ve easily identified the disinformation in this suit.),” Perplexity’s statement said. The NYT sued Perplexity in December 2025, alleging that its AI tool gives users “outputs that are identical or substantially similar to” NYT articles, including “verbatim or near-verbatim reproductions, summaries, or abridgements of the original content.” Perplexity’s response to that lawsuit said its answer engine only fetches content from copyrighted articles in reply to user queries. Perplexity argued that “developing an AI tool that can allegedly automatically fetch and provide specific content in response to user requests based on its pre-configuration and access to materials available on the Internet” is not “volitional conduct.” The NYT also sued OpenAI and Microsoft in December 2023 in a lawsuit alleging that OpenAI trains its AI with stolen news content. The lawsuit is heading toward a trial, with OpenAI arguing that its use of news articles is fair use under copyright law.

As with interior design for houses, interior design for vehicles is also subject to fashion trends. And like any fashion trend, even the best ideas can become stale and played out with overuse. Some ideas weren’t the best, either, like demonstrably worse human-machine interfaces that add to driver distraction; yet, they still proliferated throughout the industry and across the cars offered for sale. But the good news is that the pushback seems to be working. “When the trend of the Piano Black and the switchless interior was happening, it was during a time when designers and companies and engineers and everybody were thinking that, ‘Hey, we can just get rid of all these switches on the interior. We can put it all in the software. It’ll save money. It’ll save us tooling up all these extra parts for unique cars and all this stuff and make the interior more like a software experience.’ And it was done with those intentions,” said Hiren Patel, senior design manager at Nissan. That resulted in cars with minimalist interiors that looked good in the dealership. But people drive their cars on roads; they don’t just sit inside them, parked inside a dealership. “So lots of people were complaining about it and saying, ‘Hey, it takes my eyes off the road to change the temperature or to adjust my seat or do whatever.’ It’s not as easy as doing something on your phone where it’s just right at your fingertips and intuitive,” Patel said. In part, blame the increasing complexity of modern cars, which have multiple subsystems that need to be controlled. “It’s really like I’ve got to go in all these menus and do stuff, and all the car companies have a different way of doing it, too. So maybe nobody’s really figured out a great way of doing it.” Most recently, Patel was responsible for the new Rogue, Nissan’s bestselling family SUV. And its interior is a good one. Less minimalist than the all-electric Ariya, the new Rogue Hybrid has buttons and switches for most of its controls and a useful amount of storage built into the interior. Yet it’s not a boring design—the seats are a standout, taking inspiration from puffer jackets. And no, there’s no Piano Black in sight. “People want easy, intuitive controls,” Patel said. “They want wireless charging. They want surfaces that are easy to manage and clean, keep clean and clutter-free, and they want lots of places to store things. Oh, and the last thing is, they want easy access.” So the new Rogue’s rear doors were engineered to open to 90˚ to make it easier to put in a kid’s car seat or help an elderly parent into the back. That would be less of a priority for a sedan being marketed at a more image-conscious customer, but vitally important for this segment. “The target customer for this product… they have families and they have a lot of stuff. So you’ll notice that you open the door, and immediately there’s places to put stuff,” Patel said. Utilitarian need not mean prosaic, however. As designers since the Bauhaus movement have proved, “everyday” doesn’t have to be ordinary. Patel shared that sentiment. “One of the exciting things and one of the really big challenging things of doing a car or doing design for a product that is a usable everyday product is when you can make that product beautiful,” he said. “There’s so much more that goes into our everyday usable products in terms of cost, engineering, usability, feasibility, everything. And then on top of that, we’re struggling and really trying our best to fight for every little millimeter that we can get that we feel is important to making sure that that product is appealing from a visual standpoint.”

While Google is trying to move beyond Chromebooks, the ubiquity of these devices has made the company’s education products huge in schools. Now, Google says it will let educators choose whether to allow its AI tools in the classroom. However, blocking AI tools also means giving up search, leaving schools with a difficult decision. Google’s new overarching approach to AI in K-12 classrooms sounds good on the surface. After consulting educators, researchers, and lawmakers, Google has decided that schools using its education products will be opted out of AI tools by default. It will be up to administrators to manually enable them when these changes roll out in the coming months. When you look at what Google considers to be an AI tool, the nature of the Faustian bargain becomes clear. The affected products are Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom, and Google Search. There’s no picking and choosing—you either allow all the AI or none of it. This is actually consistent with Google’s approach in the consumer space. Google executives have said in no uncertain terms that Google Search is AI search. Most searches now provide an AI Overview at the top, and the company is constantly coming up with new ways to nudge people toward the conversational AI Mode chatbot function. Google sees this as the future. In a statement to The New York Times, Google education VP Chris Phillips reiterated this approach to web search. “Search and AI are deeply integrated,” he said, adding that if a school chooses not to enable AI, the students “will not have access to search, and they’ll see a message from their school that indicates it’s not available.” Google could make a version of search for schools that doesn’t have AI tools, but it’s not doing that. Instead, it’s creating two different versions of AI in search. When schools enable AI for student accounts, they’ll have two options: the standard Gemini-based AI and the new “Guided Learning” version. The latter, available in Classroom as well as search, is supposed to provide one-on-one tutoring and assistance without giving away all the answers. Google notes all of its generative AI products can make “mistakes,” though, so it may not be difficult to talk the chatbot into doing your homework. Google is betting that schools won’t want to give up Google Search and will therefore accept AI tools. This will expose students to AI early and often, possibly changing the way they interact with technology. Schools that don’t like Google’s approach could switch to alternatives like DuckDuckGo, which lets you disable AI features. But will Google’s new Guided Learning be enough to assuage their concerns?

A child in New York has died of measles, health officials in the state announced late Monday. It marks the first reported measles-associated death in New York in more than 30 years. It is the seventh measles-linked death reported in the US this year; all six of the other deaths were reported in Pennsylvania, which is experiencing a dramatic measles outbreak that has stricken over 1,430 people this year. Prior to the deaths, Pennsylvania hadn’t seen a measles-related death in 35 years. The boom in vaccine-preventable outbreaks, cases,  and tragic deaths is being fueled by slipping vaccination rates nationwide as health misinformation and anti-vaccine sentiments have taken hold. One of the most prominent anti-vaccine activists in the US currently serves as the US health secretary; Robert F. Kennedy Jr. spent decades prior to taking office attacking vaccines and spreading misinformation. He and his anti-vaccine organization, Children’s Health Defense, have been one of the most prolific sources of anti-vaccine content. Since taking office, Kennedy has waged a multi-front war on vaccines that has included dismantling and obstructing federal vaccine recommendations; launching dubious studies to look for alleged, unspecified harms of vaccines; promoting alleged vaccine injuries; and continuing to spread misinformation about vaccines and vaccine-preventable illnesses. In a statement, New York Governor Kathy Hochul directly tied the state’s death to Kennedy’s policies and anti-vaccine activism. She called the child’s death “a devastating reminder of what’s at stake when Washington deliberately erodes decades of progress in protecting Americans from preventable illnesses.” She also said she was “heartbroken” by the death, sending her condolences to the family. “As a mother and grandmother, I cannot imagine what this family is going through,” Hochul said. Overall, New York has reported 170 measles cases in over a dozen counties. In a statement emailed to Ars Technica after publication, a spokesperson for the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) said that “Secretary Kennedy and CDC Director Dr. Erica Schwartz continue to make clear that the MMR vaccine is the most effective way to prevent measles.” Days before the seventh preventable measles death, The Washington Post reported on Kennedy’s latest push to thwart public health efforts: Career scientists at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention—which is under Kennedy’s control—were blocked from publishing an urgent health alert to clinicians regarding the risk and management of measles cases during pregnancy. Given the previous success of vaccines and vaccination campaigns, many clinicians have never seen such cases in their careers. But measles infections during pregnancy pose severe, life-threatening risks to both pregnant people and fetuses. As such, CDC scientists wanted to issue an informational advisory through the CDC’s Health Alert Network. But the CDC Director’s Office declined to provide the required approval for the alert, sources told the Post. The scientists have since moved to trying to publish the information in the CDC’s publication, The Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (MMWR). But the peer-review process for the publication is longer than that of an advisory. Further, vaccine-related studies already approved for publication in MMWR have been suspiciously delayed, the Post reported. The scientists are now considering publishing the information outside the agency to avoid onerous clearance. In response to the Post’s reporting, the HHS spokesperson told Ars Technica that CDC is closely tracking measles and “providing clinicians and public health officials with guidance to protect mothers and babies.” Information on measles is “moving through CDC’s scientific review and clearance process, which informs publication decisions and timing.” Health officials in Pennsylvania, meanwhile, released their own alert on measles in pregnancy and congenital measles on October 19. New York has not released the age of the child who died or any other details about the case. The other sixth deaths reported this year have included babies, including one with congenital measles. The six deaths in Pennsylvania included a newborn with congenital measles, whose mother was severely ill at the time of birth; a 6-week-old who was too young to be vaccinated; a “perfectly healthy” 18-year-old; a 20-year-old with leukemia; a 40-year-old with underlying conditions; and a 39-year-old with underlying conditions. All the deceased were unvaccinated. Pennsylvania’s health department is currently only reporting five deaths; the department confirmed to Ars Technica on Monday that its investigation into the sixth death, reported by the Franklin County coroner on October 12, is still pending. A Franklin County judge has ordered that information about the death be sealed from public review, and The Philadelphia Inquirer has taken legal action to obtain certain death record details. The CDC, meanwhile, has reported only three of the seven deaths this year. Kennedy reportedly ordered the CDC to delete some of the early deaths and has since changed how the CDC counts deaths. Instead of the long-standing practice of relaying reports from states, the CDC now relies on federal death records, which can take weeks to come through and are not designed to capture the real-time data needed to respond to an active outbreak. Overall, the CDC reports over 4,850 measles cases across 47 states and jurisdictions. At least 511 people have been hospitalized. This year’s tally is more than double the total from all of 2025, which was 2,289 cases with three deaths. The 2026 tally is also the largest the country has seen since 1991, prior to measles being declared eliminated from the US in 2000 amid high vaccination rates. Google dismantles botnet secretly running on 1.3 million Android TV boxes

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-03

Visit Original Website
Google’s Threat Intelligence Group says it has shut down a massive botnet called x.cpe. At its height, x.cpe ran on more than 1.3 million Android TV devices, and unusually, these were Google-certified boxes. Google says its screening process missed the malware due to “obfuscation techniques” employed by Shenzhen-based Videostrong Technology. In a detailed blog post, the Threat Intelligence Group explains how the x.cpe botnet evolved quickly from a single-stage dropper into a modular three-stage malware platform that sold access to third parties. Manufacturers must have their devices certified if they want access to Google apps and branding, a process that is supposed to catch threats like this. However, Videostrong’s compromised boxes, mostly sold under the MECOOL brand, evaded detection by using a backdoor to remotely install the malware after the devices were set up and online. After devices appeared online, a stub application called x.cpe or com.oemservice would phone home. This package did not have any malicious capabilities of its own, but it had the system-level permissions necessary to install malware modules at the operator’s behest. It also disabled Play Protect, which would have otherwise flagged the silently installed packages as malicious. Google found most of the boxes were running residential proxies for routing large volumes of nefarious traffic or modules that engaged in ad fraud schemes. Selling access to this botnet was probably a big business. Videostrong also had full access to the devices, allowing operators to run terminal commands, capture the screen, and probe other devices on the local network. Google reportedly observed Videostrong doing this in real time. Google worked with several other organizations to disrupt the x.cpe network, including Japan’s National Police Agency, Synthient, and Trend Micro. Google says it has taken legal action to shut down the domains and servers Videostrong used to operate and install its malware. It also went after command-and-control servers operated by Videostrong that were connected to other malware campaigns. Google estimates Videostrong has been cut off from about 10 million compromised devices. This action should make the affected devices safe to use, but probably not as safe as they should be. Play Protect is still disabled, and the inert malware modules are still installed. Since they are loaded as system apps, users cannot easily remove them. Synthient has published a list of 182 device model names known to be included in the botnet. Anyone who has purchased a MECOOL or white label Android TV box of unknown origin should check it. Trend Micro counted a total of 1,472,554 infected devices, somewhat higher than Google’s estimate. The overwhelming majority were in Brazil, Malaysia, Iraq, Mexico, and Taiwan, but tens of thousands were also online in the US, Germany, the UK, Australia, and Canada. Google determined the operation was not the work of a rogue actor, so it has ended its relationship with Videostrong. The company, as well as any subsidiaries, will not be permitted to use Google’s Android apps and services. Google also notes that it is updating its certification process to close the loophole that allowed Videostrong to operate its botnet on certified devices. Android is open source, though, and there are countless uncertified Android-based TV boxes out there. Videostrong could continue to sell malicious hardware under other brands without Google apps. But it would still have to rebuild its malware operation from scratch.

The new job comes two months after Driscoll left the Trump administration after reportedly clashing with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. In the role, Driscoll will advise OpenAI’s public and private sector partners on how to modernize policy and strategy in support of improving national security with AI, according to OpenAI. Each printer can create a rigid, cylindrical beam by extruding coils of resin that are cured by an ultraviolet light, which the company analogizes to cement and water. This technology matters because future spacecraft will need to generate a lot more electrical power and radiate a lot more heat into the vacuum of space, particularly if they’re going to serve as orbital data centers. They’ll also still need to fit into the tight confines of the fairing on top of the rocket that carries them into orbit. Today’s large structures in space are origami projects — large solar arrays or telescope mirrors are folded up and then deployed on orbit. That’s a tricky engineering project, and so the idea of making and assembling those structures in space is appealing. NASA has funded some efforts at the idea, notably a mission with the company Redwire that 3D printed structural beams in a vacuum chamber in 2023 but never got off the ground. Naturally, it needed a name, and she thought coming up with one together would be part of the fun. One roommate, however, promptly recruited some outside help, pulling out her phone to ask ChatGPT for suggestions. To Green, a professor of psychology at Georgetown University who studies creativity, the roommate had missed the point. “Who the hell cares what the name of the basil plant is?” he says. “We’re supposed to have fun together.” Coming up with something themselves would have given the roommates more than a name: They’d have a shared memory and a story to tell whenever they referred to their leafy housemate. Instead, he says, “we just lost this experience.” You don’t have to be naming a houseplant to recognize the temptation. A birthday gift, a tricky email, a problem at work: Why wrestle with it yourself when a chatbot is happy to get you started? Yet before you hand over the task, experts suggest one small change: Write down three ideas of your own. They can be rough, impractical, or destined for the recycling bin. The point is to give yourself a chance to think before someone—or something—else supplies the answer. AI platforms can be enticing; no one likes to stare into a void of ideas. “We’re all scared of the blank page,” Green says, “and AI is offering to fill that page for us and take that fear away.” Yet working through the discomfort of having to think for yourself offers rewards: figuring something out, expressing something meaningful, or realizing you’re capable of doing something difficult. You get to tell yourself: “I’m actually proud of this because it was hard, because I failed and I didn’t give up, because I learned something in order to succeed,” he says. “And now I’ve got this new skill and this new capability.” Ask AI to spare you that struggle, and you might find it starts to feel harder to come up with your own ideas afterward. AI offers “a narrow set of possibilities,” says Sabrina Habib, a creativity researcher at the University of South Carolina, which can lead to what researchers call anchoring or fixation. “You’re going to have a hard time thinking of ideas beyond that narrow set of possibilities that was given to you.” Plus, rely on AI too much, and everything you produce might start to sound like everything else everyone else produces. In research comparing human-written college admissions essays with those generated by ChatGPT, Green and his colleagues found that the human essays explored a wider range of ideas; the AI essays covered much of the same ground. “When we adopt AI’s ideas as our own, our own ideas become similar to each other,” he says. “We lose our unique voice.” Your own ideas have the advantage of being squarely yours. Only you know the inside joke that belongs in your sister’s birthday card, or what would make an overworked colleague’s day easier. Taking a first pass lets those details shape your approach. “We’re bringing our own thoughts. We’re in charge. We have agency. We’re the authors,” Habib says. AI can help you build on what you’ve started, but “humans have to be in control of the vision, the direction.” Before opening a chatbot, spend a moment figuring out what you actually need, Habib suggests. If you’re choosing a gift, what would make it a good one for this particular person? If you’re swamped at work, what would actually take something off your plate? Giving yourself some criteria helps you generate possibilities—and, later, judge whether AI’s suggestions are useful. Then take your first pass. Write down three gifts your sister might like, three ways to adjust your workload, or three points you want to make in a difficult conversation. If you’re tempted to ask AI to write an email to your boss about that workload, try putting those points into your own words first—even if they come out clunky. Digital communication expert Erica Dhawan, author of the forthcoming book Use Your Brain, calls this the “naked draft rule”: “Write your own raw draft before you ever use AI,” she says. And remember: It doesn’t have to be perfect. Your birthday-card draft can have an awkward opening and a sentence you’ll eventually delete. Your gift list can include something too expensive. Late last month, the Justice Department did something unusual, unnecessary, and capable of being explained only if one considers the political context. This sentence could refer to any number of actions undertaken by the department in recent months. However, what occurred on Oct. 23 went mostly under the radar, as political considerations clearly hijacked federal law-enforcement strategy in a disturbing new way. I am referring to the DOJ’s charging of two former FBI officials, Nikki Floris and Bradley Benavides, with, in 2023, making false statements to the House Judiciary Committee about their involvement in and knowledge of the FBI’s investigation of Hunter Biden—charges that, on Monday, a federal magistrate judge dismissed. My focus is not on the substantive merits of the charges, the government’s exercise of its prosecutorial discretion to pursue these charges, or the close-to-an-election timing of the initiation of the prosecution. Rather, I am struck by the way the government began the case—by presenting a 27-page affidavit (sworn by an FBI agent) to a magistrate judge and thus obtaining a criminal complaint. Using this procedure—one normally reserved for the most urgent cases—was a grossly political maneuver. This action is a troubling signal of how the DOJ might operate going forward. Most federal criminal cases start when a prosecutor calls one or more witnesses before a grand jury, establishes probable cause, and persuades the grand jury to return an indictment. After an indictment is returned, the case is set for trial. Before trial, the defendant has no right to challenge the grand jury’s probable-cause determination. Unlike in civil cases, a criminal defendant’s first chance to test the evidence itself comes before the jury. Occasionally, prosecutors will need to initiate charges more quickly than the grand jury’s schedule allows. A grand jury, however, might convene only once a month, requiring the need for an expedited procedure. If, for example, someone detonates a bomb at the federal courthouse, there is an obvious need to ensure that the culprit is immediately detained (and thus not able to flee or cause further injury). We don’t want prosecutors to have to wait until the grand jury’s next meeting to get the person off the street. The law fills this gap by authorizing prosecutors to seek criminal complaints. A complaint is a sort of temporary charging document. A prosecutor obtains it by presenting to a federal magistrate judge a probable-cause affidavit signed by an investigator. Based on the affidavit’s content, the judge finds probable cause and signs the complaint. The agents then arrest the defendant, bring them before the judge, and, almost always, ask that the defendant be detained. After all, the whole point of a complaint is to get off the street someone who is potentially dangerous or likely to disappear. If neither consideration is present, then prosecutors generally wait to present the case to the grand jury. Even if a prosecutor obtains a complaint, the complaint is valid for only, in most cases, 30 days. After 30 days, unless a plea agreement has been negotiated, the prosecutor still must go to the grand jury. Aside from adding an extra step and thus more to the prosecutor’s workload, complaints can also be strategically costly. This is because, unlike an indictment, a complaint entitles a defendant to a preliminary hearing. At that preliminary hearing, the defendant can challenge the government’s probable-cause evidence by cross-examining the government’s witnesses. Even if the magistrate judge finds probable cause, the fact that the preliminary hearing occurred can pose problems down the road. This is because cross-examination can result in a key witness making a minor misstatement or offering a defense-friendly admission. If this occurs, the preliminary hearing transcript will be valuable when that witness testifies at trial. As I noted earlier, the government initiated the Floris and Benavides case by obtaining such a complaint. To my knowledge, neither posed any threat or flight risk. This is evidenced by the fact that the government did not seek to detain either defendant. Nevertheless, because it obtained a complaint, the government had to endure a multiday preliminary hearing during which the investigating agent was subjected to cross-examination by each defendant’s attorney. The plan then backfired in embarrassing fashion when the judge determined that the evidence the government mustered was insufficient to establish probable cause to support the charges. Why, then, did the government charge the defendants in the way it did? There appears to be no basis to support this strategy. Perhaps the government’s attorneys feared they might fail to obtain an indictment from a grand jury, as has occurred in other politically charged cases. However, this fear cannot explain their decision. As explained above, the complaint here merely hastened the defendants’ first court appearance. It did not negate the need for the government to go before the grand jury. Had the government skipped the complaint step and gone straight to the grand jury and there succeeded in obtaining a grand jury indictment, absent some government misconduct, a judge would have been obligated to allow the case to proceed to trial no matter how grave the judge’s concerns about the merits of the case. However, if the overriding goal of this endeavor was scoring political points—or pleasing an audience of one—then the government’s choice may make sense. As noted above, the government’s attorneys obtained the complaint by presenting a magistrate judge with a 27-page affidavit. The affidavit describes, in painstaking detail, the FBI’s investigation of the former president’s son, the defendants’ involvement in that investigation, and the defendants’ various interactions with congressional committees. The granularity is typical for a complaint affidavit, but it goes far beyond what would traditionally be included in an indictment returned by a grand jury. An indictment charging a false-statement offense would usually contain nothing more than an identification of the defendant, the date of the offense, the location where the offense occurred, and the specific false statement. In contrast to the 27-page affidavit, an indictment would be no more than a page or two—and would provide no color for television commentators, candidates for office, and the president. In short, the department made an unusual and likely unwise strategic decision so that it could issue what was, in effect, a press release harping on one of the president’s favorite themes. Similar motivations may have led to the most recent effort to prosecute former FBI Director James Comey. On Oct. 5, government prosecutors filed an information charging Comey with violating and conspiring to violate a criminal provision found in the Espionage Act. An information, unlike a complaint, can be filed by the government without any judicial approval and without the vote of the grand jury. However, it can form the basis of a prosecution only if the person charged waives his or her right to have the grand jury make a probable-cause determination. Comey, needless to say, is unlikely to do so. Why, then, did the government file the information? Ostensibly, the prosecutors acted to prevent the statute of limitations from expiring. They apparently did not think their case was yet strong enough for a grand jury presentation but recognized that if they did not do something, the 10-year statute of limitations would have expired on Oct. 5 of this year—hence the filing of the information. The problem with this explanation is that it is doubtful that the government’s information prevented the statute-of-limitations period from expiring. As Comey’s attorneys argued in a motion to dismiss the information, the applicable statute of limitations, found in the Espionage Act, requires an indictment to be returned within 10 years of the crime and does not contemplate an information doing the trick. The government countered that the general federal statute of limitations, applicable whenever a criminal provision does not specify a limitations period, appears to permit an information to toll, or pause, the limitations clock. However, this argument runs counter to the plain language of the act’s offense-specific limitations provision. Once again, why would the government use an unconventional method to commence a high-profile prosecution, particularly when the method rested on legally shaky grounds? Perhaps the information, like the complaint affidavit, had been written with a public audience in mind. These events remind me once again that this is not the department for which I proudly served. If the DOJ is nothing more than a mouthpiece of the White House, then it is unclear to me who is left to do the essential work of evenhandedly enforcing our nation’s criminal laws. Record-breaking amounts of money have once again poured into our country’s elections, taking place on Tuesday. An old story is that the Supreme Court opened the floodgate with a number of recent decisions, most notably the 2010 Citizens United decision that laid the groundwork for our system of super PACs. That’s not nearly the whole story, though. Even the Supreme Court itself underestimates how much money it let into our federal elections. As a commissioner at the Federal Election Commission, I’m professionally obliged to let the public, and the court, know the true extent to which its most recent major campaign finance decision has opened the door to millions of dollars in additional funds that risk further corruption in our federal campaign finance system. In June, the court’s majority in National Republican Senatorial Committee v. Federal Election Commission effectively allowed political parties to give unlimited amounts to their preferred candidates. In an opinion by Justice Brett Kavanaugh, the court’s conservative majority struck down “coordinated party expenditure limits,” which were caps set on spending by a national party committee in coordination with, and to benefit, the party’s preferred federal candidate. In other words, each political party committee (such as the Democratic National Committee or the Republican National Committee) may now give unlimited amounts to their preferred federal candidate, including by paying for television advertisements that support the candidate. And that’s why we’re seeing so many more campaign ads on TV and social media this election cycle. While the majority and dissenting opinions disagreed about the risk of corruption, all nine justices agreed that striking down the limits would increase money in politics. In her dissenting opinion, Justice Elena Kagan illustrated the consequences of eliminating these limits with the example of a single check from one individual for $551,300. With this check, the individual contributes $7,000 to a candidate’s campaign committee, $44,300 to the national party committee, and $10,000 to each of the 50 state party committees. Justice Kagan further explains that $544,300 of that check will go to the national party committee because the state parties can give their $500,000 to the national party committee. Therefore, that single $551,300 check, from a single person, can now be used by the political party to help a single candidate win an election. Unfortunately, Kagan’s example didn’t go far enough. That $551,300 number does not actually come close to the maximum amount an individual can give to the political party committees in a two-year election cycle. First, Kagan’s example includes the contribution limits for only one year. But an election cycle is two years, so one person can give twice the annual limit, so we can immediately double the amount of Kagan’s check. But there’s even more money on the table here. In fact, one person can actually give their preferred political party $3.1 million. And now, thanks to the court’s recent decision, that entire $3.1 million can be spent on one candidate. Here’s the breakdown of the permissible contributions to the parties for 2025–2026: One person gives $265,800 total to the three party committees for general expenses. This includes the DNC or the RNC (the national committees that help elect the presidential candidate and support candidates nationwide), the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee or the National Republican Senatorial Committee (the Senate campaign committees that help elect candidates of the party to the U.S. Senate), and the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee. That same person also gives $797,400 to the three national party committees for expenses for legal proceedings, including recounts and contests, or for paying the committees’ lawyers. And that person gives another $797,400 to the three national party committees for expenses for party headquarters buildings, like a local office in a candidate’s district. That one person also gives $265,800 to the national party committee only, like the DNC or RNC, for expenses related to presidential nominating conventions. Finally, that same person gives $1 million to the state party committees because an individual can give $20,000 to each of the 50 state party committees, which the state party committees can then transfer to the national party committees. In all, that’s $265,800 plus $797,400 plus $797,400 plus $265,800 plus $1 million for a total of $3,126,400, all of which could be spent by a political party in support of a single federal candidate, who just may happen to be the original donor’s preferred candidate. The court’s majority didn’t think striking down coordinated party expenditure limits would be a problem. Justice Kagan noticed the problem, but underestimated it. Based on my nearly 20 years of experience at the FEC, I’m concerned the FEC’s remaining rules and enforcement tools are ill-equipped to address the corruption risks from one wealthy donor contributing to a party to evade contribution limits to a single candidate. At minimum, the FEC needs its quorum restored so we can resume using what means we have left to maintain our federal campaign finance system, regardless of how much money floods our elections. Amicus’ “By the People” series concluded last month with a capstone conversation taped live in front of a virtual audience of Slate Plus members. Host Dahlia Lithwick convened an expert panel—political organizer Stacey Abrams; constitutional law scholar Daphna Renan; and movement strategist Ilyse Hogue—to grapple with a question that has become impossible to ignore: What happens when the Supreme Court has ceased to function as a backstop for democracy, and what would it take for the American public to actually reclaim the Constitution from a court claiming sole ownership over its meaning? Portions of their conversation are excerpted below, edited and condensed for clarity. Dahlia Lithwick: I would love to hear from each of the three of you—what was the moment when it became clear to you that the Supreme Court is not going to save us, and further, the moment the court stopped behaving like the stewards of American constitutional democracy? When did you realize it was time to stop believing that these nine justices can claim sole ownership over the Constitution? Stacey Abrams: I structure my thinking about this around DEI. We tend to dismiss DEI as a sort of corporate HR issue, but in the United States—and the right has understood this for a while—DEI is the container for all of the rights, privileges, and defenses that we’ve created since the birth of this nation, that guarantees access to citizenship and to the rights and prerequisites of being an American, and not just being an American, but of being in America. If you look at the arc of history, the things that go into that container are the 13th, 14th, and 15th amendments, the 19th Amendment, also the Americans with Disabilities Act, and the Immigration and Nationality Act. All of these things are DEI. There are three things DEI protects in our democracy: education—what you know, the economy—what you do, and elections—who’s in charge. The first attack on DEI under this regime happened when they went after the Voting Rights Act in Shelby County v. Holder in 2013. Education was Students for Fair Admissions, and the economy was their attack on Roe v. Wade, because it stopped women from controlling their bodily autonomy. But all of that was predated in 2013 with the attack on voting rights, which underpinned the ability of Americans to have a voice in how this country works. Daphna Renan: I started to think about this at the beginning of the Obama administration. I was a young lawyer in the Justice Department. President Obama and members of Congress had just won a landslide election on the argument that our healthcare system was broken, and we needed to fix it, and had spent all of the political energy, momentum, and capital they’d gained in enacting a landmark Affordable Care Act. And I remember seeing that all of a sudden, everything that mattered was what five justices were going to say about this statute when it was challenged for its constitutionality in federal court; and that it no longer mattered that there had been this massive political coalition-building and compromise and debate in enacting the law. It could all collapse under very controversial theories that five justices held.And at the same time the court was starting to really aggressively build out its unitary executive theory—the idea that the president could violate federal laws long understood to rein in abuses of executive power. Ilyse Hogue: My point of no return was in the months I spent between July and October of 2018 fighting Justice Kavanaugh’s Supreme Court nomination. We had almost never seen a citizen uprising opposing a nomination as powerful, durable, and sustained. And yet no one stood up—not Justice John Roberts, not Senate Majority Leader Mitch McConnell—and said, “Actually, the investment of faith in the American public in this backstop of justice is more important than this one man achieving a life goal.” I felt a total collapse of trust in all of the systems that are the pathways for ordinary citizen engagement in our democratic processes. Without that trust, we cannot function as a democracy. I have spent the better part of the last year on the road in communities around the country. We’re hearing from the fastest-growing segment of the American population that political scientists call “double-haters.” They don’t like either party. They don’t have a lot of faith in the institutions that are supposed to serve us. What we’re experiencing in these rooms with people from all walks of life, all political persuasions, is that they still have a deep belief in the promise the constitutional principles outlined: separation of powers, separation of church and state, equality, freedom, rights. So that abiding investment in those principles, coupled with the work you’re doing here with “By the People” to help citizens understand their own power in reclaiming those principles, gives me the hope that powers my work for the future of this democracy. Ilyse Hogue: I think it’s a yes/and. People understand the court needs to be reformed and transformed to reflect the will of the people and not the handful of electeds and corporations that influence it. I do not hear unity around one solution, and I think we need to couple any proposals around court reform with popular education so it becomes less scary and less weaponized and more of a wholesale vision for how the court serves the people, and is representative of the people it serves. Stacey Abrams: I actually start with the district court level. I live in the South. I’ve spent most of my life in the South, where the capture of the courts is not a Supreme Court issue, it’s a 5th Circuit, 11th Circuit, issue. How do we expand the court to reflect the composition of the country we currently have? We have not done that update in decades. We do not have a court that’s reflective of the needs and values or the lived experiences of the people they are adjudicating. If you were to ask someone on the other side of the ideological aisle, “Do you think things are moving fast enough?” Depending on what their lived experiences are—they may not necessarily think it’s the Supreme Court, but they will have a conversation about expanding the lower courts. I’m in favor of expanding the Supreme Court, expanding the lower courts. I’m also in favor of thinking about a term limit conversation at the Supreme Court level. But you cannot have those conversations unless you walk people through the entirety of the process. When people know more, they demand more, and the capture of the courts has relied on ignorance—on people not knowing what they should be entitled to. It’s largely reliant on the fact that people are so used to things being broken, they don’t know what it feels like when it works. Daphna Renan: When we talk about court reform, I think the question is, to what end? What is the proper role we want the court to play in American constitutional democracy? If you look at Callais this past summer, you have a court that believes it can decide that a landmark law enacted and reauthorized over and over by overwhelming votes in Congress, signed into law by presidents of both parties, a law vindicating a vision of the 15th Amendment—five justices get to say whether that can be our vision of the 15th Amendment, whether that can be the Constitution that belongs to us. This judicial supremacy puts the court’s understanding of the Constitution over any other law the people may elect representatives to enact, no matter how misguided, how dangerous, or how problematic that interpretation of the Constitution is. So when we talk about court reform, we should also be talking about the tools available to Congress, through federal laws, to restrict that totalizing power of the Supreme Court. One idea that’s been on the table over and over again throughout history, and actually passed the House of Representatives during Reconstruction, is a statute that would say you need a consensus among the justices before they can say a federal law is unconstitutional. It shouldn’t be that five or six justices on a thin, controversial reading of the Constitution can reject what a whole movement has built in coalition to transform into a landmark federal law. Are the people still in charge? Is our grand experiment in democracy still working?” Stacey Abrams: We live in a deeply divided country, and the divisions are so visceral that at this moment, a constitutional convention is not actually a solution set. We should not be so myopically focused on one solution set. We should look at all the tools we have. The challenge with all these tools is that they don’t necessarily look like they will build what we want. My work is really focused on how we excavate and investigate those tools. Then you wrap that together with Daphna’s work that says, “Here’s what we’ve done before,” and Ilyse’s work that says, “Here’s what people want to build.” I’ve spent much of this election season working on state and local elections. State elections can have more impact in this moment than a constitutional amendment, because we have the ability at state and local levels to start to tweak the things we need. Instead of waiting for a federal solution set, which is what the Constitution provides, I’m looking at how to reverse-engineer what opponents to democracy have often utilized. They don’t fight at the federal level alone. They go to the state and local level and dismantle democracy at its base and let the infection and the rot rise. That means we can start the planting at the base. It does not mean we don’t have a democracy. It does mean we have to fight harder for it. We are in a competitive authoritarian regime. That’s what we are. Where we stay depends on how hard we’re willing to fight and our willingness to try different things. Daphna Renan: The Constitution is not what blocks our ability to pass laws that limit the role of dark money in our elections. What stands in the way of that is the current interpretation by the Supreme Court, which is a deeply controversial interpretation. We can limit the court’s power to decide those kinds of questions, and its power to reject an alternative constitutional judgment that’s reflected in a federal statute like the long-standing bipartisan campaign finance reform legislation the court has been gradually gutting. During Reconstruction, the first Black member of the House, Joseph Rainey, argued we should bring to life these new constitutional guarantees just enacted in the 13th, 14th, and 15th amendments, and we should do it not through a kind of narrowly legalistic frame. He argued for the need to construe these amendments in the light of humanity, “in the light of progress and civilization” that we want to see. The abolitionists built out this idea that if you give the people the power to define the Constitution through our politics, through our organizing, through our coalition-building, through our building political parties, through our voting, and through our legislating, that there’s an alternative vision of the Constitution we can bring into life. Ilyse Hogue: We are not nearly as divided as the authoritarians want you to believe. Division is their fuel. Unity is our fuel. People have not given up. They may have given up on the two-party system. They may have given up on the idea that politics as usual and the status quo is going to deliver what they want. But they are resilient, and they want to fight if we’re offering them pathways to fight for this vision of universal rights, freedoms, and opportunity. Perfectly spherical asteroids are rare, and their irregular shapes present an additional challenge for those keeping an eye on the skies to avert a potential asteroid apocalypse. To overcome the computational problem and enable better modeling of irregular asteroid shapes, Moore and his team combined two high-fidelity simulation codes already in use at Sandia: ALEGRA, which models radiation heating and high-energy material dynamics, and SPARTA, which simulates the behavior of low-density gases. As Moore explained to us in an email, both codes are good at one particular part of asteroid impact modeling, but bad at the other. “ALEGRA … can very accurately simulate the vaporization of the rocky surface, but the calculation becomes more inefficient for matter at low densities,” Moore told us. “This is a problem for asteroid deflection simulations, because as the gas expands around the asteroid, the calculation time goes up.” Earlier asteroid simulations using ALEGRA typically modeled asteroids as spheres to reduce calculation times. “SPARTA is more efficient at low gas densities,” Moore said. “The problem is, conversely, SPARTA gets more difficult to run at high gas densities. The result is two codes handling the parts of the problem they're best suited to solve, reducing the overall computational burden. The combined approach is more than 1,600 times faster than earlier methods, Moore said in a Sandia press release, while retaining sufficient accuracy to predict deflection in irregularly shaped asteroids. A new approach known as “reverse game theory” aims to control powerful AI models by designing systems that incentivize them to stick to the rules. As AI models become more capable, safety researchers are scrambling to ensure they are “aligned” with human desires—to make sure, in other words, that rogue agents don’t break the law, hack into unauthorized servers, or release poison gas into major cities and kill us all. Until now, this work has mostly focused on trying to peer into an AI’s “brain” to understand what it is “thinking,” or training models to hew to values that we hold dear. Claude, for instance, has its constitution—a sort of moral code. The trouble is, these techniques don’t seem to work particularly well: Models have been shown to deliberately underperform on tests, hide their intentions, and mislead their evaluators. Now some alignment researchers believe that reverse game theory, also known as mechanism design, might get better results. Where game theory tries to predict how rational actors will behave when given a particular set of rules, reverse game theory attempts to design rules that will elicit the kinds of behavior we want to encourage. An example of mechanism design in the real world might be the classic strategy for splitting a piece of cake in two: One person cuts the cake and the other gets the first choice of pieces. This incentivizes the cutter to make the pieces as even as possible. Other examples include surge pricing in ride-hailing apps, which encourages drivers to be available at busy times, and second-price auctions, where the winning bidder pays the price of the second-highest bid instead of the highest. Platforms use these auctions to sell online advertising because they encourage buyers to bid close to the maximum they are willing to pay. In AI, mechanism design starts from the premise that we may never be able to understand enough about the internal workings of frontier models to make sure they are aligned. This means we should design mechanisms that incentivize agents to behave in ways that we want, regardless of what’s actually going on under the hood. A recent preprint paper by Stephen Morris, an economist at MIT, along with colleagues from Yale and Columbia suggested some potential mechanisms that could stop agents from “sandbagging” tests (pretending to be worse than they actually are—for example, to avoid triggering safety mechanisms). Instead of being rewarded only for how well it does on a test, a model could be rewarded for how well its answers predict what a second model will say. Deliberately playing dumb becomes a losing bet. “AI agents have a very good understanding of what other AI agents can achieve,” Morris says. “So it does seem like a context where this cross-checking could work more practically. The paper also suggests pitting agents against each other, creating a competitive dynamic that incentivizes them to perform their best, or pairing agents that are known to be biased in opposite directions and rewarding them depending on how closely their outputs match, thus incentivizing them to reach an optimal answer. A second approach, as envisioned by Elad Hazan, a professor of computer science at Princeton, is to design mechanisms that take inspiration from the ways human society keeps people “aligned”: social norms, the justice system, the monetary incentives of free-market capitalism. “We want to align superintelligent AI,” he says. “Why don’t we first talk about humans? How do we align humans?” Hazan’s suggested mechanism involves having AI models take turns as either “solvers” that tackle problems or “auditors” that check the others’ work. “One agent acts like law enforcement; the other agent acts like the citizen,” he says. During training, the models are rewarded depending on outcomes: Solvers that get caught cheating are punished, but so are auditors that fail to catch the cheaters. In situations where there’s a disagreement between auditors and solvers, the case can be sent to “trial”—perhaps a human expert will decide who is right. There is, Hazan admits, a risk that agents will collude with each other—as auditors have colluded with the businesses they’re meant to be auditing, or corrupt police officers have colluded with criminals. But that problem can be solved by rewarding the auditors well enough, so that being caught isn’t worth the risk (a lot easier in the world of AI training than the real one, where money for police salaries is tight), and by auditing the auditors using techniques akin to employing mystery shoppers. Hazan points to Nobel Prize–winning work by the economist Gary Becker, who introduced the idea of the “rational offender” in the 1960s. Criminals don’t break the law because they’re inherently bad people, he said. Instead, they do it when it’s the most rational choice given the incentives on offer. Crime makes sense only when the proceeds outweigh the potential punishment. Alignment shouldn’t be thought of as a property of a model, he argues, but as a descriptor of its behavior at a particular moment. “You are, like, a nice person,” he says. “Are you aligned? I don’t know. Probably you are aligned most of the time, but there could be a situation where you’re not.” One potential issue with applying mechanism design to AI: It relies on the assumption that AI agents behave rationally. Mechanism design has a mixed record with humans, who often fail to behave the way economic models say they should. It’s ironic: Economists have spent decades trying to reduce human behavior to mathematics, and now, in large language models, we’ve built mathematical systems that run on human language and could be just as inscrutable. AI models exhibit behaviors that seem more human than machine, and they can be swayed by subtle factors that are difficult to understand and explain. “Is it even reasonable to assume that they will respond to incentives?” asks Helen Qu, a research fellow at the Flatiron Institute, a nonprofit dedicated to advancing scientific discovery through computational methods, who has written about mechanism design and AI safety. “Obviously, AIs are not rational,” Morris says. “They’re just algorithms at work.” However, as economists have found with humans, treating them as if they’re rational could still be useful if it helps to predict and control their behavior. Qu sees mechanism design as just one layer in a “defense in depth” strategy for keeping AI models aligned—alongside interpretability, regulation, and training, not instead of them. But there are a lot of questions to be answered before the approach can be implemented in actual frontier models. It’s unclear, for instance, how these theories will apply to real models, or whether mechanisms designed for one task will carry over to another in practice. Models could find loopholes that no one anticipated. (Anthropic and OpenAI did not respond to requests for comment on whether they are working on this.) Whatever these mechanisms look like, they will ultimately have to slot into the biggest mechanism of all: the free market. “We play by the rules of economics,” says Hazan. A more aligned model may be good for the world, but if it’s slower or more expensive than a less aligned one, does our current system of rules and regulations actually incentivize any of the frontier labs to release it? “It’s a mechanism that’s responsible for a lot of prosperity for humankind,” he says of the market, “but it could be ultimately responsible for something very terrible.” Perhaps the entities really in need of a better mechanism design are the labs themselves. But blanket limits can restrict the workflows delivering value. They also optimize the wrong number. Cost per token measures consumption, not results. Some spending reflects waste, including retries and runaway loops. Some represents the work of shipping software. A meter cannot distinguish them, and neither can a spending cap. A better measure is cost per merged pull request: total AI-assisted development cost divided by the number of changes that pass review and are merged. Track rework and defects alongside it so cheaper changes do not conceal lower quality. If that cost falls as AI usage rises, without compromising quality, developers are converting more consumption into useful software. Agentic coding does not require the most powerful model at every step. Use a frontier model, or an on-premises flagship when policy requires it, for difficult planning and novel reasoning.
