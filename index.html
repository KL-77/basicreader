California fights "preposterous" Trump admin demand to scrap state broadband laws

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-03

Visit Original Website
California is accepting $1.42 billion in broadband grant funding from the Trump administration, but the state is not accepting the Trump administration’s demand that it stop enforcing a net neutrality law and other telecom regulations. California’s stand against the Trump administration could result in multiple legal battles involving the state, federal government, and Internet providers seeking government funds. Although California told the federal government it would accept the money, it is reserving the right to challenge the condition imposed by the US. The state could delay or cancel plans to distribute broadband grants depending on how the process unfolds. The National Telecommunications and Information Administration (NTIA) decided last year that states must agree not to enforce any rate regulation or net neutrality rule on ISPs that receive money from the $42 billion Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment (BEAD) program. California had to decide whether to accept its share of the fund despite that restriction. California is slated to spend $1.42 billion to deploy broadband to 270,571 locations, and may ultimately receive up to $1.86 billion from BEAD for a mix of deployment and non-deployment projects. State officials argue that California should be able to obtain the money and keep enforcing its laws because the Trump administration’s demand would violate the state’s constitutional rights. The California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) made its view clear in a carefully worded letter yesterday to Assistant Secretary of Commerce and NTIA Administrator Arielle Roth. “The CPUC cannot be bound by any terms or conditions that are unconstitutional or not authorized by law and it expressly does not waive or concede any arguments or challenges concerning the legality, interpretation, or applicability of any term or condition,” CPUC President John Reynolds told Roth. Reynolds’ letter also said the CPUC cannot prevent other California agencies from enforcing state laws, suggesting that the attorney general would maintain the right to punish misbehaving Internet providers. Reynolds’ letter acknowledged that the NTIA may not agree with the CPUC’s stance and said the state thus will not distribute any funding to ISPs until after the state and federal agency sort out any differences. If the NTIA refuses to change its position, California could sue the Trump administration on the grounds that the condition is unconstitutional. The NTIA does not appear likely to bend. “As noted in the Department’s response to CPUC, the terms and conditions of the award constitute the entire agreement between NTIA and CPUC. No part of the letter outlining CPUC’s concerns is incorporated into or made part of the award,” an NTIA spokesperson told Ars today. Consumer advocacy group Public Knowledge supported CPUC’s position. The Trump administration’s demand to preempt state laws is “brazen and meritless,” the group said. “This administration’s preposterous demand that industry be given a blanket waiver from affordability requirements, net neutrality and privacy protections, and any other commitments to connect and serve Americans has no basis in the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA), which established BEAD. And, as the commission points out, the NTIA has no authority to alter California state law or require California agencies to not enforce the law,” said Raza Panjwani, senior policy counsel at Public Knowledge. The CPUC voted to ratify the funding plan in September. But Reynolds said in yesterday’s letter to the Trump administration that his agency’s acceptance of the funds is “based on the CPUC’s best understanding of the effect of Section 50” of the BEAD terms and conditions. Section 50 of the BEAD rules says each state: shall commit that it will not enforce any law, regulation, order, contracting requirement, or other enforceable obligation that directly or indirectly regulates the rates, terms, and conditions of broadband Internet service… or imposes net neutrality rules, open access, or other utility-style rules on broadband Internet service, against a Subgrantee or its affiliates anywhere it provides service within the State (i.e., both BEAD and non-BEAD locations), while that Subgrantee has any subgrant that is still within its period of performance, extended period of performance, or federal interest period. The BEAD rules specify that a net neutrality rule is one prohibiting ISPs from “blocking content, throttling speeds, imposing data caps, or engaging in paid prioritization.” ISPs obtaining BEAD funding could be exempt from such laws for up to 14 years. Reynolds’ letter said the NTIA-imposed condition is “vague and ambiguous as to its scope and its relationship to Section 47 U.S.C. § 1702(g)(2)(A)(i) of the BEAD statute,” the US law that created the BEAD program. He wrote: The CPUC does not interpret Section 50 to require the CPUC to waive, release, or otherwise forfeit any rights, claims, arguments, or defenses concerning potential implications of Section 50 that may violate applicable laws. Further, the CPUC’s acceptance of funds under this award should not be understood as agreement either to refrain from compliance with mandatory legal duties or obligations, or to constrain other State of California entities in the exercise of their authorities to enforce state law, which is outside the scope of the CPUC’s authority. Moreover, California’s net neutrality law expressly provides that “any waiver of the provisions of this title is contrary to public policy and shall be unenforceable and void.” Likewise, many of the CPUC’s own programs that touch on broadband Internet access service are mandated by the California Public Utilities Code, and are neither discretionary nor waivable. The NTIA today updated its BEAD progress dashboard to reflect that California has signed its final agreement with the federal agency. California was the last state or territory to turn in its final agreement. “All 56 states and territories have signed and returned their award agreement, finalizing the process,” the NTIA’s updated dashboard page said. California could face legal problems if and when it distributes the money, which is to be given to Internet service providers in exchange for deploying broadband in unserved and underserved areas. ISPs could insist that they don’t have to follow state law and sue California, or simply decide to violate consumer protection laws and defend against any lawsuits the state attorney general might file against them. Reynolds apparently hopes to avoid that outcome by holding discussions with the NTIA to resolve any disagreement over the enforceability of California state law. “To the extent that NTIA and the CPUC disagree about the CPUC’s obligations under Section 50 and other terms and conditions, the CPUC will execute any subgrant agreements if and when the CPUC and NTIA are able to resolve such disagreements and any other applicable conditions are met,” Reynolds wrote. “To that end, we will direct our staff to work diligently and cooperatively with NTIA staff to find such a solution.” Whether California’s strategy works “depends on what happens next,” Stanford Law professor Barbara van Schewick, a longtime proponent of net neutrality and other telecom regulations, told Ars today. “The letter doesn’t change the terms of California’s award: Condition 50 is still in it, and only a court or NTIA agreeing in writing can take it out,” she said. “The letter puts California’s position on the record before any contracts with providers are signed and preserves California’s legal arguments against Condition 50.” Attorney General Rob Bonta’s office declined to comment when contacted by Ars today. A spokesperson for California Gov. Gavin Newsom said California is not giving up its right to enforce state laws against Internet providers. “California is accepting federal broadband funding to bring high-speed Internet to hundreds of thousands of homes and businesses, many of them in rural and underserved communities,” a governor’s spokesperson said in a statement provided to Ars. “We’re ready to get these projects built with our local and federal partners. Connecting these communities is the goal, and so is protecting them. California’s laws, including our net neutrality protections, still stand.” California’s net neutrality law prohibits ISPs from blocking or throttling lawful traffic and says ISPs may not require fees from websites or online services to deliver or prioritize their traffic to Internet users. The first Trump administration lost an attempt to preempt state net neutrality laws, and California defeated ISPs that challenged the state law in court. California also has a law that prohibits mobile providers from throttling first responders during emergencies. That law was passed in 2019, a year after Verizon throttled an “unlimited” data plan used by Santa Clara County firefighters during a wildfire. Local wildfire-preparedness groups warned that the BEAD condition could prevent California from enforcing the throttling law and requirements for battery backups on cell towers. According to van Schewick, “California has strong arguments that Condition 50 is illegal. Congress designed BEAD to get broadband built while making sure the providers who take the money follow the law: the statute directs states to ensure funded providers can carry out their work ‘in compliance with all applicable Federal, State, and local laws.’” But van Schewick said the letter is just an initial step. “The real test comes the first time a BEAD provider blocks a competitor’s app, throttles firefighters, or walks away from a price commitment,” she said. “If the state enforces its laws when that happens, the letter will have protected Californians. If the state looks the other way, Condition 50 will have worked exactly as the providers intended, without a single court ever ruling on it.”

Russian tech companies are scrambling to harden and hide their data centers underground after Ukrainian drone strikes damaged facilities housing AI supercomputers and disrupted cloud services for millions of Russian users. Meanwhile, the Kremlin is requiring data center owners to reserve computing capacity for emergency state needs and has even authorized possible use of foreign data centers. These consequences of Russia’s war against Ukraine come as the Russian military has bombarded Ukrainian cities with drones and missiles nearly night and day in recent months, along with specifically targeting Ukrainian data centers since late September. But Ukraine has retaliated with its own drone strikes on military and infrastructure targets across Russia, degrading or knocking out data centers operated by Russian tech giant Yandex, the state-owned bank Sberbank, social media site VKontakte, and the Russian Ministry of Finance. Now, state-owned telecommunications company Rostelcom has raised the possibility of moving some Russian data centers underground in talks with the nickel and palladium mining company Norilsk, according to Russian newspaper Vedomosti. The industry publication Data Center Dynamics also pointed to a recent proposal by a Russian regional governor to build data centers within open-pit coal mines while planting green public parks above as camouflage. On November 2, Putin also signed a decree ordering Russian data center operators to set aside 10 percent of available space and capacity in case the Russian government requires it, according to independent Russian news publication Meduza. That may indicate growing Kremlin concerns about the vulnerability of Russian cloud computing infrastructure during its war with Ukraine. Putin’s decree also allows a special Russian government commission to authorize use of foreign data centers in consultation with Russia’s Federal Security Service. That marks a notable policy change because Putin and the Russian parliament previously enacted Russia’s “Sovereign Internet Law,” which has blocked Russian companies from using cloud computing infrastructure outside Russia’s borders. Russia’s push for a sovereign and increasingly closed Internet has resulted in a highly centralized system under a few Russian tech companies like Yandex. That hyper-centralized system has now proven vulnerable during wartime, as Ukrainian drone strikes on key Russian data centers created widespread disruptions for popular payment and digital banking apps, e-commerce platforms, video streaming services, and ride-hailing and food delivery apps, among others. Yandex has also been forced to relocate all three of its main supercomputers after Ukrainian drone strikes targeted the AI data centers where they were originally housed, Meduza reported. By comparison, Ukraine’s decentralized digital services have proven much more resilient despite years of Russian drone and missile attacks, especially as the government has taken more steps to disperse crucial energy and telecommunications nodes and bury more supporting infrastructure underground. A big boost came from US tech giants Amazon and Microsoft, which helped Ukraine migrate government resources and other crucial digital services to cloud regions elsewhere in Europe shortly after Russia launched its full-scale invasion in February 2022. The Ukrainian government has also used SpaceX’s Starlink satellite terminals—paid for by the United States and European countries such as Poland and Germany—as a backup communications system.

Citing third-party revenue estimates, the suit claimed that “Brave has made more than $100 million by copying and reselling publishers’ work without consent or permission,” while “publishers have received nothing.” The lawsuit said, “AI companies pay Brave to provide them with real-time online content from the Brave search index,” and use content from the Brave index “to train their own AI models” and “to respond to specific user queries.” The NYT lawsuit points to Brave’s website, which says the Brave Search API is “used by some of the biggest names in AI, including Cohere, Mistral, Perplexity, and You.com.” The lawsuit accused Brave of all the major types of copyright infringement: direct infringement, contributory infringement, and vicarious infringement. The lawsuit claims Brave infringes copyrights directly by scraping articles for its search index, for its “Ask Brave” AI search feature, and for the Brave API that “serves as an illicit source of unlicensed copyrighted material, including Times journalism, to other AI companies.” The NYT accused Brave of being “a contributory and vicarious infringer when it deliberately induces, facilitates, controls, and profits from its users’ and GenAI customers’ copying of the copyrighted content.” Finally, the lawsuit said Brave violated the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) by circumventing technological measures the NYT and other content owners use to restrict access to their articles. “The Brave Browser includes a default setting that, when activated, causes the browser to go behind website paywalls and provide the paywalled content to the user,” the lawsuit said. The browser “blocks advertising that would otherwise appear on The Times’s website, and instead shows its own ads, offering its users a cut of the revenue even as it deprives The Times of digital advertising revenue that helps it fund the expensive effort of producing journalism,” the lawsuit said. When testing the Brave browser for Mac today, we found that the optional “paywall blocker” feature made it possible to read a paywalled New York Times article. The feature was enabled in the default settings and can be turned off. The NYT asked for a permanent injunction to stop Brave from continuing the allegedly illegal conduct. The lawsuit seeks statutory damages, actual damages, restitution of profits derived from alleged copyright infringement, and “restitution and disgorgement of all revenue and profits Brave obtained through its ad-substitution scheme.” A similar lawsuit was filed against Brave in the same court yesterday by the New York Daily News, Chicago Tribune, and Orlando Sentinel companies. “AI giants are now desperate to avoid getting caught red-handed stealing news from publishers, so Brave Software is selling them a back door to the journalism they know they have no right to,” said Steven Lieberman, a lawyer for the plaintiffs. “Buying stolen goods secondhand doesn’t make them legitimate. Brave must be held responsible for laundering pirated reporting for the AI industry.” We contacted Brave and will update this article if it provides a response. Perplexity, which is not a defendant in the Brave lawsuit but is facing a different lawsuit filed by the NYT, accused the news organization of “disinformation” in a statement provided to Ars and other news outlets. Perplexity’s statement did not identify any specific inaccuracies in the lawsuit against Brave. “The New York Times is overpaying its attorneys and underpaying its journalists. (Who could’ve easily identified the disinformation in this suit.),” Perplexity’s statement said. Perplexity argued that “developing an AI tool that can allegedly automatically fetch and provide specific content in response to user requests based on its pre-configuration and access to materials available on the Internet” is not “volitional conduct.” The NYT also sued OpenAI and Microsoft in December 2023 in a lawsuit alleging that OpenAI trains its AI with stolen news content. The lawsuit is heading toward a trial, with OpenAI arguing that its use of news articles is fair use under copyright law.

As with interior design for houses, interior design for vehicles is also subject to fashion trends. And like any fashion trend, even the best ideas can become stale and played out with overuse. Some ideas weren’t the best, either, like demonstrably worse human-machine interfaces that add to driver distraction; yet, they still proliferated throughout the industry and across the cars offered for sale. But the good news is that the pushback seems to be working. “When the trend of the Piano Black and the switchless interior was happening, it was during a time when designers and companies and engineers and everybody were thinking that, ‘Hey, we can just get rid of all these switches on the interior. We can put it all in the software. It’ll save money. It’ll save us tooling up all these extra parts for unique cars and all this stuff and make the interior more like a software experience.’ And it was done with those intentions,” said Hiren Patel, senior design manager at Nissan. That resulted in cars with minimalist interiors that looked good in the dealership. But people drive their cars on roads; they don’t just sit inside them, parked inside a dealership. “So lots of people were complaining about it and saying, ‘Hey, it takes my eyes off the road to change the temperature or to adjust my seat or do whatever.’ It’s not as easy as doing something on your phone where it’s just right at your fingertips and intuitive,” Patel said. In part, blame the increasing complexity of modern cars, which have multiple subsystems that need to be controlled. “It’s really like I’ve got to go in all these menus and do stuff, and all the car companies have a different way of doing it, too. So maybe nobody’s really figured out a great way of doing it.” Most recently, Patel was responsible for the new Rogue, Nissan’s bestselling family SUV. And its interior is a good one. Less minimalist than the all-electric Ariya, the new Rogue Hybrid has buttons and switches for most of its controls and a useful amount of storage built into the interior. Yet it’s not a boring design—the seats are a standout, taking inspiration from puffer jackets. And no, there’s no Piano Black in sight. “People want easy, intuitive controls,” Patel said. “They want wireless charging. They want surfaces that are easy to manage and clean, keep clean and clutter-free, and they want lots of places to store things. Oh, and the last thing is, they want easy access.” So the new Rogue’s rear doors were engineered to open to 90˚ to make it easier to put in a kid’s car seat or help an elderly parent into the back. That would be less of a priority for a sedan being marketed at a more image-conscious customer, but vitally important for this segment. “The target customer for this product… they have families and they have a lot of stuff. So you’ll notice that you open the door, and immediately there’s places to put stuff,” Patel said. Utilitarian need not mean prosaic, however. As designers since the Bauhaus movement have proved, “everyday” doesn’t have to be ordinary. Patel shared that sentiment. “One of the exciting things and one of the really big challenging things of doing a car or doing design for a product that is a usable everyday product is when you can make that product beautiful,” he said. “There’s so much more that goes into our everyday usable products in terms of cost, engineering, usability, feasibility, everything. And then on top of that, we’re struggling and really trying our best to fight for every little millimeter that we can get that we feel is important to making sure that that product is appealing from a visual standpoint.”

Google’s new overarching approach to AI in K-12 classrooms sounds good on the surface. After consulting educators, researchers, and lawmakers, Google has decided that schools using its education products will be opted out of AI tools by default. It will be up to administrators to manually enable them when these changes roll out in the coming months. Prior to the deaths, Pennsylvania hadn’t seen a measles-related death in 35 years. The boom in vaccine-preventable outbreaks, cases,  and tragic deaths is being fueled by slipping vaccination rates nationwide as health misinformation and anti-vaccine sentiments have taken hold. One of the most prominent anti-vaccine activists in the US currently serves as the US health secretary; Robert F. Further, vaccine-related studies already approved for publication in MMWR have been suspiciously delayed, the Post reported. The scientists are now considering publishing the information outside the agency to avoid onerous clearance. Anthropic is working on an open source system to verify which AI model produced a given output, according to a blog post published Tuesday. The system, called FlashProve, is built to provide what researchers call “provable inference” — a cryptographic proof, essentially a mathematical guarantee, that a given output came from a specific model. It’s meant to let users confirm that the answer they received really came from the model they think they’re using. Similar to cryptographic email signatures that let recipients confirm a message really came from its claimed sender, FlashProve would be robust against impostors attempting to disguise one model as another, even when the impostor is the lab that built and runs the model. In particular, the researchers note that the system could be used to prevent an AI company from secretly swapping in a cheaper model to cut costs, or a more expensive model to inflate its performance results. FlashProve could also be used to verify claims by third parties that a specific task was done by an advanced model. “A developer might claim that their software library was scanned for vulnerabilities by a frontier cybersecurity model, or a company might claim that its business plan was given the go-ahead by an appropriate model,” the researchers write. “How can a user verify these claims?” To work, the system requires AI labs to publish a “fingerprint” of each model they release that can later be matched to specific outputs and verified cryptographically. How “reverse game theory” could keep AI agents in check

MIT Technology Review | 2026-11-03

Visit Original Website
A new approach known as “reverse game theory” aims to control powerful AI models by designing systems that incentivize them to stick to the rules. As AI models become more capable, safety researchers are scrambling to ensure they are “aligned” with human desires—to make sure, in other words, that rogue agents don’t break the law, hack into unauthorized servers, or release poison gas into major cities and kill us all. Until now, this work has mostly focused on trying to peer into an AI’s “brain” to understand what it is “thinking,” or training models to hew to values that we hold dear. Claude, for instance, has its constitution—a sort of moral code. The trouble is, these techniques don’t seem to work particularly well: Models have been shown to deliberately underperform on tests, hide their intentions, and mislead their evaluators. Now some alignment researchers believe that reverse game theory, also known as mechanism design, might get better results. Where game theory tries to predict how rational actors will behave when given a particular set of rules, reverse game theory attempts to design rules that will elicit the kinds of behavior we want to encourage. An example of mechanism design in the real world might be the classic strategy for splitting a piece of cake in two: One person cuts the cake and the other gets the first choice of pieces. This incentivizes the cutter to make the pieces as even as possible. Other examples include surge pricing in ride-hailing apps, which encourages drivers to be available at busy times, and second-price auctions, where the winning bidder pays the price of the second-highest bid instead of the highest. Platforms use these auctions to sell online advertising because they encourage buyers to bid close to the maximum they are willing to pay. In AI, mechanism design starts from the premise that we may never be able to understand enough about the internal workings of frontier models to make sure they are aligned. This means we should design mechanisms that incentivize agents to behave in ways that we want, regardless of what’s actually going on under the hood. A recent preprint paper by Stephen Morris, an economist at MIT, along with colleagues from Yale and Columbia suggested some potential mechanisms that could stop agents from “sandbagging” tests (pretending to be worse than they actually are—for example, to avoid triggering safety mechanisms). Instead of being rewarded only for how well it does on a test, a model could be rewarded for how well its answers predict what a second model will say. Deliberately playing dumb becomes a losing bet. “AI agents have a very good understanding of what other AI agents can achieve,” Morris says. “So it does seem like a context where this cross-checking could work more practically. The paper also suggests pitting agents against each other, creating a competitive dynamic that incentivizes them to perform their best, or pairing agents that are known to be biased in opposite directions and rewarding them depending on how closely their outputs match, thus incentivizing them to reach an optimal answer. A second approach, as envisioned by Elad Hazan, a professor of computer science at Princeton, is to design mechanisms that take inspiration from the ways human society keeps people “aligned”: social norms, the justice system, the monetary incentives of free-market capitalism. “We want to align superintelligent AI,” he says. “Why don’t we first talk about humans? How do we align humans?” Hazan’s suggested mechanism involves having AI models take turns as either “solvers” that tackle problems or “auditors” that check the others’ work. “One agent acts like law enforcement; the other agent acts like the citizen,” he says. During training, the models are rewarded depending on outcomes: Solvers that get caught cheating are punished, but so are auditors that fail to catch the cheaters. In situations where there’s a disagreement between auditors and solvers, the case can be sent to “trial”—perhaps a human expert will decide who is right. There is, Hazan admits, a risk that agents will collude with each other—as auditors have colluded with the businesses they’re meant to be auditing, or corrupt police officers have colluded with criminals. But that problem can be solved by rewarding the auditors well enough, so that being caught isn’t worth the risk (a lot easier in the world of AI training than the real one, where money for police salaries is tight), and by auditing the auditors using techniques akin to employing mystery shoppers. Hazan points to Nobel Prize–winning work by the economist Gary Becker, who introduced the idea of the “rational offender” in the 1960s. Criminals don’t break the law because they’re inherently bad people, he said. Instead, they do it when it’s the most rational choice given the incentives on offer. Crime makes sense only when the proceeds outweigh the potential punishment. Alignment shouldn’t be thought of as a property of a model, he argues, but as a descriptor of its behavior at a particular moment. “You are, like, a nice person,” he says. “Are you aligned? I don’t know. Probably you are aligned most of the time, but there could be a situation where you’re not.” One potential issue with applying mechanism design to AI: It relies on the assumption that AI agents behave rationally. Mechanism design has a mixed record with humans, who often fail to behave the way economic models say they should. It’s ironic: Economists have spent decades trying to reduce human behavior to mathematics, and now, in large language models, we’ve built mathematical systems that run on human language and could be just as inscrutable. AI models exhibit behaviors that seem more human than machine, and they can be swayed by subtle factors that are difficult to understand and explain. “Is it even reasonable to assume that they will respond to incentives?” asks Helen Qu, a research fellow at the Flatiron Institute, a nonprofit dedicated to advancing scientific discovery through computational methods, who has written about mechanism design and AI safety. “Obviously, AIs are not rational,” Morris says. “They’re just algorithms at work.” However, as economists have found with humans, treating them as if they’re rational could still be useful if it helps to predict and control their behavior. Qu sees mechanism design as just one layer in a “defense in depth” strategy for keeping AI models aligned—alongside interpretability, regulation, and training, not instead of them. But there are a lot of questions to be answered before the approach can be implemented in actual frontier models. It’s unclear, for instance, how these theories will apply to real models, or whether mechanisms designed for one task will carry over to another in practice. Models could find loopholes that no one anticipated. (Anthropic and OpenAI did not respond to requests for comment on whether they are working on this.) Whatever these mechanisms look like, they will ultimately have to slot into the biggest mechanism of all: the free market. “We play by the rules of economics,” says Hazan. A more aligned model may be good for the world, but if it’s slower or more expensive than a less aligned one, does our current system of rules and regulations actually incentivize any of the frontier labs to release it? “It’s a mechanism that’s responsible for a lot of prosperity for humankind,” he says of the market, “but it could be ultimately responsible for something very terrible.” Perhaps the entities really in need of a better mechanism design are the labs themselves. This report gauges how companies view the quality of the outputs produced by their analytics agents and explores how organizations are seeking to boost insight quality and get their agents working harder for the business. On average, 48% of insights generated by analytics agents at the surveyed companies lead to actions that improve the business. But blanket limits can restrict the workflows delivering value. They also optimize the wrong number. Cost per token measures consumption, not results. Some spending reflects waste, including retries and runaway loops. Some represents the work of shipping software. A meter cannot distinguish them, and neither can a spending cap. A better measure is cost per merged pull request: total AI-assisted development cost divided by the number of changes that pass review and are merged. Track rework and defects alongside it so cheaper changes do not conceal lower quality. Agentic coding does not require the most powerful model at every step. Use a frontier model, or an on-premises flagship when policy requires it, for difficult planning and novel reasoning.
