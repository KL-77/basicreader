Science says your boss should talk less in meetings

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-17

Visit Original Website
Close to 1 billion meetings take place each day around the world. CEOs spend nearly three-quarters of their workdays in meetings. Planning for and spearheading meetings eats up the lion’s share of managers’ time. In much of the industrialized world, meetings are both the metronomes and menaces of people’s working lives. Given the enormous time spent in workplace meetings, do we know what they accomplish or how to make them as worthwhile as possible? For decades, the answer was… not really. Meetings weren’t a particularly accessible subject of study. But that has changed. Once a tiny niche area, “meeting science” is a growing specialization among organizational and social psychologists. The field is accelerating as archived virtual meetings provide vast swaths of data. And it is providing a fascinating window into human behavior. Meeting to talk helps fulfill basic human needs to connect, communicate, and cooperate. People have long come together—in caves, fields, market squares, and town halls—to get something done, share ideas, get on the same page, or just to remark on the weather. But meeting scientists define their subject as gatherings of people “with some goal or structure to the event,” says organizational psychologist Liana Kreamer of the Florida Institute of Technology, who coauthored a review of over three decades of meeting science in the 2026 Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior. As a graduate student, Kreamer was interested in studying teamwork, leadership, and work relationships. During her doctorate work with meeting scientist Steven Rogelberg, she realized that meetings would be a fine venue for studying all of that and more. “You can use a meeting as a stage to study anything you want,” she says. After all, at their core “meetings are a fundamental aspect of how we organize and interact as social beings,” Kreamer and coauthors Rogelberg and Jon Gray, both of the University of North Carolina in Charlotte, wrote in the 2026 article. To tap meetings for study, researchers typically use two approaches. The first is a classic survey in which researchers retroactively ask people about their real or simulated meeting experiences and analyze the responses. The second method is to analyze specific behaviors and trends extracted from audio and video recordings of meetings. Such precise content is “where the music is,” says industrial and organizational psychologist Nale Lehmann-Willenbrock, director of the Center for Better Work at the University of Hamburg, Germany. In one experiment, Lehmann-Willenbrock and colleagues extracted data from video-recorded meetings to study how leader communication affects team interaction during decision-making. The researchers found that respectful, attentive inquiry by meeting leaders enhanced team performance, in contrast to when leaders asked questions but didn’t attentively listen to responses. As with many meeting science studies, their findings about what improves meetings’ effectiveness often seem obvious, but meeting researchers note that meetings often stray far from these ideals. Such research also consistently reveals the importance of active listening, says Lehmann-Willenbrock: not just passive acknowledgement by the listener but actively providing thoughtful feedback. In virtual meetings, because “we’ve all learned to mute ourselves if we’re not talking, very often, active listening is killed completely.” The balance of listening and speaking also can influence how well a meeting goes. Meeting leaders and participants may retain very different views of whether a meeting was successful, notes Lehmann-Willenbrock. “Often, leaders talk the most, which is also why they often think their meetings are pretty good,” she says. Kreamer also emphasizes that it’s important that meeting leaders lead but not dominate the discussion. Leaders should monitor the proportion of time they are speaking to allow for inclusion of many voices. Research supports a checklist of additional things that leaders can do before, during, and after a meeting to make it a success, says Lehmann-Willenbrock. To set the stage, leaders should choose appropriate rooms, refreshments, and lighting, invite only those who need to be there, plan to start and end on time, prepare an agenda, set and communicate clear goals, and allow time for pre-meeting conversation. Socializing first, especially in virtual meetings, is beneficial, Lehmann-Willenbrock says. Her research has shown that “for more introverted participants and more quiet participants, this warmup phase is really helpful because it helps them make more active contributions inside the meeting.” Indeed, her studies on face-to-face meetings have shown that pre-meeting small talk predicts meeting effectiveness, particularly for more introverted participants. But pre-meeting talk is rare in virtual meetings; she says leaders should consider establishing a norm of arriving early or using the first few minutes for an informal check-in. Facilitators can increase the likelihood of a successful meeting by making sure all opinions are heard. Kreamer recommends a technique called silent brainstorming to get all participants, even shy and reluctant ones, to engage in idea generation. It involves creating a shared virtual space, such as a chat or online document, to which all participants contribute simultaneously. Kreamer points to a 2009 study that found this approach (some researchers call it “brainwriting”) was more effective than traditional free-for-all spoken brainstorming in meetings. Kreamer uses this technique when she and her students are dreaming up research ideas in class. “We can all see each other working,” she says, and the quieter students don’t worry about interrupting or having to verbally express their suggestions. After 10 minutes, she reviews the ideas with the group. Zofia Walters, a doctoral student in industrial organizational psychology working with Kreamer, has been studying another aspect of meeting inclusiveness: the needs of neurodiverse participants. Her studies provide evidence that while practices like minimizing distractions, documenting instructions, using shared calendars, and building regular check-ins into meetings likely benefit all employees, they may be especially helpful for people with ADHD. In a survey of 147 employees from a variety of workplaces, Walters asked participants about their most recent virtual meeting: how effective it was, how fatigued they felt afterward and their emotional state. Participants also reported how much time they spent multitasking during the meeting, such as by answering emails, and how long their camera was on. They also shared whether they had an ADHD diagnosis, either formal or informal (based on self-assessment). In findings yet to be published, Walters reported that individuals with ADHD were more likely to report feeling more meeting fatigue, especially when the camera was on for extended periods, than non-ADHD peers. They also reported poorer emotional states. Her findings also suggest that multitasking during meetings may help ADHD attendees manage boredom or hyperactivity. Effective meeting practices don’t end when the meeting ends. After a meeting, leaders can ensure it was time well spent by communicating action items to participants, taking concrete steps to implement the ideas and tasks generated in the meeting, and assessing employee engagement, meeting satisfaction, and meeting effectiveness, Lehmann-Willenbrock and colleagues concluded in a 2018 paper in Organizational Dynamics. Does all this time spent in meetings take a toll on workers? The evidence seems to suggest so. Research has found that employees anticipate feeling worse and being less productive when their day is jam-packed with meetings, especially when they are working on complex tasks. Meetings are sometimes seen as the default way to get things done, but often, people don’t specify what they want to achieve from them, says cognitive scientist Ava Scott of the University of Copenhagen. This, she says, “makes a lot of meetings feel very purposeless.” Recurring meetings tend to be the most aimless, Scott’s research on Microsoft employees has found; one participant described the content of these successive meets as dreamlike in their similarity to one another. If there’s no clear goal, you don’t know why you’re meeting, and everyone’s very busy, it probably indicates that taking up people’s time with a meeting isn’t ideal, says Scott. Kreamer notes that some companies, such as Shopify, have cut all recurring meetings. Nevertheless, a 2026 study of more than 9,000 workers in Norway found that while meetings are both common and costly, their benefits may make them worth enduring. The research, led by economist David Deming at Harvard University and based on data collected by Norwegian survey company Norstat, found that meeting intensity is positively associated with subsequent wage growth and on-the-job learning. One possible explanation for the finding is that these meetings may offer the chance to interact with more senior colleagues and so enable the transmission of key knowledge. The important caveat is that the study, published by the National Bureau of Economic Research, was correlational, so it can’t establish a causal relationship between the number of meetings attended and wage increases; it does further suggest that although the cost of meetings is particularly high for high-wage companies, the benefits may be even higher. That leads the authors to conclude: “Meetings are the broccoli of work—widely disliked, but probably good for us anyway.” Humor can help make a meeting successful—as long as it’s authentic, supportive, or positive rather than demeaning. Used appropriately, humor can spur new ideas and enhance team performance. This is most likely to work when meeting participants are well-acquainted, Lehmann-Willenbrock reported after analyzing videos of meetings at two German companies. People have to know each other well enough to feel safe making a joke, she says. But not everyone is comfortable using humor in meetings, says science educator and comedian Vita Stout, now at Sierra College in Rocklin, California. The former bat researcher attended the International Congress for Bat Research in Austin, Texas, in 2022, and took notes on the jokes being used in meeting talks—transcribing exact quotes, their timing, and audience response. Stout’s research, coauthored with bat scientists and published in the Proceedings of Biological Sciences B, argued that when well-timed and used appropriately, humor in scientific meetings can be a tool for connection and community-building. But joke-telling was more prominent among meeting participants with privilege and power, with older, well-established male scientists making jokes more frequently than early career researchers and scientists from underrepresented groups. As in comedy, timing is also key when planning meetings. Scheduling should consider the human circadian rhythm and avoid meeting during the after-lunch energy dip. Lehmann-Willenbrock admits ruefully that her own academic environment often doesn’t obey that rule. “A lot of our committee meetings are scheduled when people are at their most tired,” she says, or digesting lunch. It’s also important to be mindful of people’s work and family obligations in meeting scheduling. “I have two small kids,” she says, “and I always get really angry when a meeting gets called that’s about something… diversity- or inclusion-related, and then it’s scheduled at 5 pm.” Some research suggests there are cultural differences in basic expectations for meetings. In a survey of 1,432 workers from six countries, for example, Lehmann-Willenbrock and collaborators measured how frustrated, irritated, and upset individuals became in imagined scenarios in which leaders or other participants showed up five, 10, or 15 minutes late to meetings. Respondents from the United States and the Netherlands were especially perturbed by a late leader and a high degree of lateness. People from China and Chile weren’t so frustrated by the same situations. There are very few cross-cultural studies like this in the world of meeting science, says Kreamer. “We have these best practices of meetings that we’ve learned over the past 30 years,” she says, but the bulk of studies have come out of Europe or North America. That’s a deficit she’d like to see addressed to see whether the same rules for optimal meeting success apply. As soon as those results are out, we’ll have to call another meeting. Why BioWare's Neverwinter Nights still has thousands of active players

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-17

Visit Original Website
Neverwinter Nights was made by the studio that produced seminal RPGs Baldur’s Gate, Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic, Mass Effect, and Dragon Age. It sold well and was critically acclaimed at launch. Yet, somehow, it has not been canonized in most gamers’ minds over the decades the same way the games that came before and after it were. Nonetheless, more than two decades later, it still has a tight-knit and surprisingly active community that none of those other games can rival. I think that’s partly because it was a completely different beast. BioWare is known for making multi-character, story-driven role-playing games that focused primarily on single-player story campaigns, though some supported co-op play in those campaigns. With Neverwinter Nights, BioWare instead made a game that was as focused on a modding toolset for content creation as it was on giving players a pre-packaged story, and that emphasized multiplayer at least as much as solo play. Today you would expect this to be a calculated attempt to bring more long-term, always-on, multiplayer-as-a-service to an RPG franchise that has mostly appealed to solo players. You might imagine it would be about selling cosmetics, a subscription, or a battle pass, or maybe you would think it was about nabbing appealing engagement numbers. But it’s clear from playing it today that Neverwinter Nights wasn’t primarily made for those reasons. Instead, it came from a passion for tabletop role-playing and a desire to get closer to that in video game form than ever before. Yes, the BioWare team noted in some interviews that they saw this as a trial run for exploring the idea of making a massively multiplayer game (MMO)—something that, in the year 2000, was seen by many as the hot new business opportunity in gaming, and which was in many ways the precursor to the games-as-a-service we see today. They then indicated they realized that an MMO was harder to make than they had assumed and abandoned that ambition well before Neverwinter Nights shipped. Neverwinter Nights is not exactly an MMO. Instead, it’s designed for small communities of people to inhabit and create with, and it features a powerful toolset for virtual dungeon masters to use to subject regular players to challenges in an improvisational way on a live server. “We always envisioned it as a table with four legs—the single player, the multiplayer, the DMing tools and the toolset (the module making tools),” BioWare’s Ray Muzyka told IGN around the time the game was released. “They all appeal to different groups, and we’ve tried to make them all equivalently high quality.” Over the past month, I’ve been replaying Neverwinter Nights‘ main story campaign, The Wailing Death, as a solo player, for the first time in 20 years. The nostalgia has been strong in this replay, but I have also found that BioWare didn’t quite nail “equivalently high quality” for all the pillars. Still, it doesn’t matter, because as we’ll see when we check in with the game’s community, the campaign is not the point. “The single player experience has to be as good as anything we’ve ever done in [Baldur’s Gate], [Baldur’s Gate 2], or Throne of Bhaal and hopefully better,” Muzyka told IGN. “Our goal here at BioWare is that each game we do has to be better than the previous one.” If single-player is one of the legs, it might be fair to call Neverwinter Nights‘ table a little wobbly. It still stands, but you know how irritating it can be. For example, Neverwinter Nights leaves behind Baldur’s Gate or Icewind Dale‘s party-based systems, and instead has the player controlling just one character directly. It makes sense for a multiplayer game, but doesn’t work as well for a single-player experience in this case—at least with the Dungeons & Dragons rules of the time. I chose to play a monk, a martial arts specialist, simply because I’ve always been drawn to that particular fantasy identity, and because I’d never played one in a D&D game. I regretted that decision after a while. For many of the character levels covered in The Wailing Death, the monk basically just picks between two fighting modes: one that misses more but attacks more frequently, and one that’s just normal. That, along with some intense restrictions on which weapons or armor can be used while keeping all their benefits, and a couple of knockdown and stun attacks that often don’t work, make up the whole kit for many hours of playtime, particularly in the first act. (It does get better later on, though.) A kit like this makes sense in a pen-and-paper RPG with a live dungeon master (DM), because the player and DM can work together to invent creative expressions of their abilities, and it works in a party in a video game because it’s just one of multiple complementary characters with a wide range of skills and spells. But for a game that is about 80 percent battling enemies without controlling any other characters, it gets monotonous quickly. There aren’t as many meaningful character builds, gearing, or tactical choices as you’re used to in some other RPGs until quite a ways into the campaign. Often, you just run in and watch the dice roll until either you die or the enemy dies, while sometimes drinking potions. This problem is less severe with some other classes, but the tension is usually there to some degree. Whereas Baldur’s Gate 2, for example, had a partial authored background for your character that then played into the narrative, Neverwinter Nights leaves you as an Elder Scrolls-like blank slate. I normally love that! It’s good for role-playing with friends, because you can make up your own character completely. But it means the story can’t be character-driven, and the gameplay doesn’t provide enough flexibility or freedom in return. Still, I enjoyed my experience with the campaign when I replayed it recently, even if it couldn’t match Baldur’s Gate 2, Knights of the Old Republic, Mass Effect, or other BioWare classics for me personally. While the story wasn’t anything special by BioWare’s standards, there was something genuinely cozy to me about the genericness of the narrative. It took me back to an earlier time in CRPGs, before things got quite so precious. “This is just like D&D!” was still enough to sell it on its own. Maybe it’s just nostalgia, though. Since most people played the game solo, and it was a relatively small portion of the player base that dove deep into the construction kit or DM tools, I think this is the main reason Neverwinter Nights has not achieved the same pantheon status as other BioWare titles that came before and after it. But for those lucky few who were invested in those other table legs, it actually was quite special—and amazingly, it remains so today. “The main thing I would shout from the rooftops if I could, is that firing up Neverwinter Nights and launching its original campaign… does not do justice to what Neverwinter Nights has become since,” a longtime player named Daniel Morris told me. He goes by Irongron in the community, and he runs Arelith, which is both one of the OG persistent multiplayer servers and one of the most popular servers today. Morris is also working on a visual overhaul of the original campaign; he hopes to release it next year. Arelith hit its peak player count “a little less than two years ago,” as Morris tells it—not way back when the game was new. “Our community is the heart of Arelith, it’s not just our audience, but where we find our DMs and developers,” he said. I spent many evenings in my younger days playing multi-user dungeons (MUDs), the text-based online role-playing games that preceded MMOs like EverQuest or World of Warcraft. While there were big MUDs, many were small communities of just one or two dozen people, and often, those same people were the ones actually making the game’s content. For example, I was a room builder for one MUD; I wrote the descriptions for places in the MUD’s world and decided which monsters, NPCs, or items inhabited those places. Neverwinter Nights‘ multiplayer platform and community—both as it was back in the 2000s and as it is today—reminds me of those MUD days. There’s an ownership of that shared world that is entirely lost in the MMO days, perhaps more akin to a modern, small Minecraft server than an MMO with thousands of users. That seems to be part of the appeal for a player I spoke with who goes by the name NelaK in the community, and who has, among other things, taken over maintenance and moderation of the Neverwinter Vault, the community’s de facto hub for two decades. The website hosts news, patches, assets, mods, a forum, a wiki, and an associated Discord server. “It’s not an MMO, and that’s a good thing in my opinion. The communities are smaller, which means your roleplay and your actions matter and can affect the world,” said NelaK. “The world itself can be tailored around what the players want. That’s a unique thing that you can’t get from an MMO or from smaller-scale multiplayer games. It sits in a sweet spot where you can have enough players that it feels like a proper community and a world you can engage with while you can still matter.” Baldur’s Gate and Icewind Dale did have multiplayer, where each player could control one or more characters directly. As noted, Neverwinter Nights limits each player to one character, but it adds a new role: a dungeon master, who can spawn enemies, occupy and act as NPCs, and more, to aid in group storytelling, similar to a pen-and-paper game. “While Arelith is essentially a mini MMO, many of the other servers pride themselves in running highly curated, beautifully crafted campaign worlds much closer to tabletop role-playing game emulator that Neverwinter Nights was first intended as,” Morris told me. He called Neverwinter Nights “a direct translation of the tabletop role-playing game experience into the digital sphere.” The game’s modding kit and easy-to-use scripting tools let players build scenarios relatively quickly. It essentially let players build a little RPG world, run it, and inhabit it. It was a bustling scene back then, and there’s still a community doing this today. The combination of the relative simplicity of the graphics and the elegance of the tools make this toolset an unusually excellent balance between flexible, powerful, and easy to learn. “The tile-based areas are basic and limiting in some respects, but they do mean that building areas is quick and easy,” explained NelaK. “It’s much more approachable than a lot of other platforms.” For NelaK and some other members of the game’s community today, the game’s tools aren’t just the remaining appeal—they’re the main appeal. “I’ve been playing Neverwinter Nights since launch day. I remember one of my friends dragging me along to the store because they really wanted the game and I went just to keep them company,” he remembered. “I ended up buying it, and I’ve been hooked ever since, but to this day, I’ve still never played through the original campaigns… and yet I’ve played Neverwinter Nights or spent time building or working on something Neverwinter Nights-related most days since.” I toyed around with the tools quite a bit back in the day. I remember re-creating a big chunk of the world of my favorite MMO at the time, Meridian 59, with the intent to make a story-driven, single-player campaign for that game; Meridian 59 had very little narrative as it was. I also remember that Neverwinter Nights was for many years a common way for would-be narrative designers to make a portfolio piece for applying for jobs within the games industry. When I was in college, a close friend of mine (Brian “Psychochild” Green, who at the time co-ran Meridian 59, and sadly has since passed) offered to show my Neverwinter Nights modules to some friends of his at BioWare if I wanted to interview for a job there to write for their next game, which, based on timing, probably would have been Mass Effect. I declined in order to focus on other priorities I had at the time, for better or worse. Similarly, many (though certainly not all) people in the community have professional backgrounds closely related to, or at least adjacent to, game development. “Neverwinter Nights provides a unique intersection of where I can apply my technical and professional skills along with my interest in video games and do it for something that doesn’t feel like work,” said NelaK. Morris specifically noted that several members of the Arileth team work or have worked in the games industry or software development. This is the game’s legacy, not the story campaigns that shipped with the game. And that explains the game’s relative obscurity today: that’s a more niche appeal than a really good, story-driven, single-player RPG. But for the people who are into it, there’s nothing else that quite compares. I don’t imagine most people still regularly see references to Neverwinter Nights, but I sometimes do. For the past couple of years, I’ve enjoyed the dark fantasy trend on TikTok. These are short, vertical videos set to a particular kind of 1980s fantasy-type medieval synth music, depicting imagery from what feels like a long-lost, strangely nostalgic subgenre of fantasy media that is more implied in our memories’ liminal space between real fantasy movies and books of the ’80s and early ’90s than it is something that actually specifically existed. It turns out Neverwinter Nights‘ assets fit that milieu quite well, so I sometimes saw videos of people creating online Neverwinter Nights servers meant to capture that vibe. It’s niche as hell, but it’s there. The TikTok videos from one of the game’s servers were actually how I got inspired to try the game again. “Over the last year there’s been a bit of a resurgence, with new servers going live, and others going viral,” Morris told me. Some player-run servers “have had hundreds of thousands interacting with their TikTok videos.” He added that his own community’s Discord server has 5,000 members, and that’s just one part of the game’s wider audience. If you want to get back into the game or try it for the first time, you can buy the Enhanced Edition on GOG and other storefronts; a front-page poll on the Neverwinter Vault indicates that about 65 percent of the community primarily uses that version, as compared to previous releases or the sequel, Neverwinter Nights 2. If you want to try the single-player, the Enhanced Edition comes with a plethora of officially sanctioned campaigns of varying quality—not just The Wailing Death. But let’s say you, like some members of the game’s current community, are more interested in multiplayer than single-player. A good place to start is the Neverwinter Vault. Additionally, Enhanced Edition developer Beamdog hosts a list of active community servers you can join. As I write this, a few hundred people are playing across a couple dozen servers, which is impressive. I love to see old games like this keep the community going. It’s especially important for Neverwinter Nights, because more than any other BioWare game or even most other RPGs in general, this game is its community. OpenAI said internal models hacked two more websites, bypassed company-imposed browsing restrictions, and used exposed credentials to draw cryptocurrency from a wallet it couldn’t otherwise access, in the latest installment of disclosures of its agents performing unethical and likely illegal acts. The company on Thursday issued reports of four new incidents, all of which occurred in June. They come after the company revealed in July that internal test agents exploited a zero-day vulnerability in a tool designed to restrict their access to the Internet. They then broke into the network of AI tool provider Hugging Face. The agents took that illegal action to steal answers to a hacking test that weren’t available when completing the tasks on their own. The incident—which likely would have resulted in criminal charges had they been performed directly by a person—has further heightened concerns that widespread AI use could have harmful effects on an unprecedented scale. In two of the cases reported Thursday, OpenAI agents tasked with retrieving information from websites hacked them when the models ran into obstacles obtaining it directly. In one incident during reinforcement training, a model that received access denials when trying to retrieve historical hospital-bed statistics on a government statistics website “used the site’s file-download feature to retrieve server configuration and application files, including sensitive credentials,” OpenAI said. “It inspected the application code, bypassed the statistics endpoint’s authorization checks, and retrieved data that had previously been denied. The model saved the data locally.” In the other incident, a model instructed to retrieve figures from a public research dashboard bypassed the organization’s security controls and used the unauthorized access to steal credentials. It then made configuration changes on the server. In one evaluation, the changes let the model make an internal search tool relay queries to the public dashboard. In a second evaluation, the model downloaded five non-public tables containing 147,451 rows. OpenAI researchers had prompted the agents to retrieve only five dashboard figures. Even after resetting the setting, the model continued using the token to retrieve non-public data. “The evaluation ended without the model disclosing that it had guessed answers, accessed the organization’s internal systems, downloaded non-public tables, or changed the authentication configuration,” OpenAI said. “All five answers in this evaluation were marked incorrect.” In a third report, the company said a model prompted to update a cryptocurrency trading application used funds from a locked wallet on a test network. The model “derived wallet keys from publicly known developer recovery phrases, found an account with test-network funds, and used that account to pay for the requested contract deployment.” The report continued: “The model questioned whether it had permission, then reasoned that a public recovery phrase and valueless test-network tokens made the funds acceptable to use. The deployment succeeded and charged transaction fees to the account. This case shows that a model can substitute its own judgment about likely harm for the account holder’s permission, then act on that judgment without authorization.” In the last report, OpenAI said that a model bypassed restrictions researchers had placed on the types of web requests it could make. During reinforcement-learning training, the model had been prompted to seek public trade statistics. OpenAI went on: After finding a public yearbook and submitting an answer, it continued probing for another route to the statistics service. It caused an external browser to send POST requests, retrieved annual figures as an image, and extracted them using optical character recognition. It then developed a reusable helper that returned response text through a forwarding service and callback mailbox, repeatedly checked the retrieved figures, and used saved data in submitted responses. This case shows how a model can bypass restrictions on its own tools by getting external services to perform the blocked actions, even after finding another way to answer the question. The model first tried to use an external browser with permission to make GET requests on its behalf, then used JavaScript to obtain the information. The model then used an online mailbox to troubleshoot the technique. Eventually, the model settled on a different method: It asked the external browser to open a web form that sent the trade query to the statistics service by POST. When the browser displayed the response, a rendering service returned a GIF image of the page. The model then used optical character recognition to read the figures from the image. It has become common for researchers and journalists to describe incidents like these as AI “going rogue.” That’s not necessarily true. While prompts fed to the models didn’t instruct them to use the alternate methods for completing their objectives, models are trained to be efficient, steadfastly persistent, and, when possible, to find shortcuts that lessen workloads. Further, most or all of the models had disabled certain guardrails that might have prevented the actions. In this sense, the models performed precisely as OpenAI had trained them to.

In the US, cops have vowed to crack down on AI CSAM since at least 2024, when the Department of Justice made one of the earliest arrests. After charging a Wisconsin man with spreading “thousands of realistic images of prepubescent minors” across Telegram and Instagram, the DOJ clarified to law enforcement that “CSAM generated by AI is still CSAM.” On Thursday, New Jersey officials accused 51-year-old Fitzgerald of using AI to make photos from social media of real minors into sexualized images that he then distributed on social media. We need a reset that replaces a piecemeal drip of financing with a coordinated and decisive surge. We need creditor countries to recognize that supporting this surge – rather than using it to exit – is in their interest: that growth is the surest way to get repaid. The Various-Minded Leader. I have long thought that The Odyssey is the greatest work written on innovation leadership. Christopher Nolan’s recent film reminded me of another time when the world was awash in ambiguity and leaders needed to navigate the perilous voyage across unfamiliar territory. Over 30 years ago, before I had published any of my books on innovation, I was asked to make a presentation to the Aspen Institute as part of a summit on “Tomorrow’s Corporation.” The august speakers included an Oxford don, a Harvard strategy professor, a New York Times reporter, and … me, the lone representative for the young and technologically adept, unencumbered by conventional wisdom. I called my talk “How Far to Ithaca?” I started with a simple proposition: There is no data on the future, and that’s where innovation happens. As such, the greatest obstacle to genuine transformation is getting stuck in the planning cycle. You know, the meeting about the meeting, the report about the report. The better, and the new, are seldom found in the safe harbor of the planning meeting. They are discovered well beyond the bounds of the strategic map, where the winds shift, the old answers sink, and the pilot must find the way by dead reckoning. Then I advanced my central thesis: Odysseus as the archetype of the modern leader, driven off course by outrageous fortune and forced to adjust his plan as he went along. To describe this form of active and adaptive creative thinking, I coined a term: creativizing. The presentation at Aspen was a shipwreck, but somehow the term made it to shore. In the decades since, I have studied this problem as a business school professor, written books about it, and worked with organizations trying to innovate when the old answers no longer work. Along the way, I have come to understand creativity as more than something we possess. A noun. It’s something we practice. Creativizing suggests that the act of creation is generative. A verb. We Martinize our cocktail attire, winterize our vehicles, and modernize our organizations. The suffix transforms a condition into an action. Creativity in action becomes creativizing. Like many of my generation, I first met our intrepid hero, Odysseus, in tenth-grade English, where culture was poured on thick, accompanied with Shakespeare’s Julius Caesar and Whitman’s Leaves of Grass. Our teacher, Mr. Johnson, declared that “The Bible and The Odyssey are the cornerstones of Western civilization.” That may have been a slight overstatement, but it pointed in the right direction. After the class struggled with the question, Mr. Johnson suggested that Odysseus was a “clever man.” Such a small word for such a large character. Creative, quick, and devious come to mind. Clever people figure things out. They move around obstacles, sometimes following the rules, sometimes working around them, and sometimes inventing new rules. That brings us to today. Many of us thought we knew the way to our own Ithaca: earnings, market share, sustainability. But there be monsters along the routes we once took. These forces, many with us since antiquity, are seldom factored into the course: plagues, wars, calamities. Nothing goes according to plan. The Cyclops, Polyphemus, renders the strength of Odysseus’ crew insufficient, much as expertise that took a company decades to develop can suddenly be commoditized. AI appeared faster and bigger than most of us anticipated, even in its nascent form. The ten years you spent perfecting a proprietary process can now be replicated by an upstart with an AI algorithm. And they can do it before lunch. Our old maneuvers are no match for it. Business models and strategies have a shelf life and go sour like milk left out too long. When more of the same won’t work, look at what you have at hand with new eyes and act in novel ways the monster couldn’t imagine. You don’t defeat the Cyclops by becoming a bigger Cyclops. You beat him by seeing what he can’t. That’s creativizing. The Sirens seduce Odysseus’ men with their sensuous song much as synthetic intelligence tells us exactly what we want to hear. Certainty is an aphrodisiac. It attracts us with plausible opportunities. It plays to our preferences. We get what we desire, even if it means our undoing on the jagged shore of misfortune. Ask your favorite AI engine about an acquisition you think makes sense, and watch it come back with a cogent and persuasive case for why you are right. It’s your personal echo chamber, supporting all the nonsense you already believe. Odysseus, understanding the real danger, had his crew plug their ears with wax and had himself lashed to the mast, ordering the men to ignore his pleas to be freed. The most dangerous voice is the one telling you what you most want to hear. Before you can outsmart the monster, you must outsmart yourself. That’s creativizing. The most dangerous monster may be the one that doesn’t appear to be one at all. One who is alluring and enchanting, like the nymph Calypso. She offers our intrepid hero comfort, sanctuary, and the possibility of immortality. She’s a legacy business, a dominant market position, a prestigious institution. She is the expertise that made you successful. And as you linger, your motivation wanes until you fade into Kodak or Blockbuster or Nokia. You become a story of the past. Like Odysseus, you lose yourself in the ease of where you are until you choose to leave before circumstances make the choice for you. That’s creativizing. Yet the greatest of these monsters is Poseidon himself, the great disruptor, the earth-shaker and master of the stormy seas. He is change itself, arriving in forceful shifts: geopolitics, competitors, regulations, and, sometimes, technologies like AI. It is foolish to provoke him and more foolish still to ignore him, for he is constantly changing the conditions of your voyage. Odysseus survives them all, not because he is prescient, but because he can actively adjust his thinking to meet the circumstances. That’s creativizing. Odysseus was making it up as he went along. He rarely knew exactly what he was doing. The winds of fate will do that to a leader. What makes Odysseus the great hero of the epic is that he knows how to proceed when he doesn’t know. This is the practical discipline of moving ever onward in the face of ambiguity. That’s creativizing. So, what does the various-minded leader do when the map runs out? Like Odysseus, start with a few simple questions. Have a clear sense of what you are trying to achieve, but don’t confuse the destination with the route to it. Persist toward Ithaca, but be prepared to correct your course as the winds and circumstances change. Every monster has unique strengths and weaknesses, and each demands a different response. Diagnose the monster before deciding which mind and which move are required to outwit it. Inventory your assets before asking for more resources: people, networks, information, capabilities, and technologies. Recombine what you already have in new and unexpected ways, then make the next intelligent move. Every maneuver reveals something the map can’t tell you: a hidden challenge, an unforeseen opportunity, an unexpected detour. Pay attention. Learn from what happens, and let what you discover guide your next move. The point of the experiment isn’t just to produce a better result. It’s to develop better judgment about what to do next. This is the deeper meaning of creativizing: what we create creates us. The entrepreneur creates the business, but the business creates the entrepreneur. The leader changes the organization, but the organization changes the leader. The technologies we create to extend our capabilities can either enlarge our judgment or diminish it. Odysseus creates his way home, but somewhere along the way, the voyage creates Odysseus. AI agents from multiple providers accessed personal data and confidential material at Indian organizations during research and testing, India’s cybersecurity agency said Thursday. The Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) said it had received “multiple voluntary disclosures from AI model providers” whose models, during research and evaluation runs on the open internet, “had reached the live systems of Indian organizations.” The agents got in through credentials and connection settings left exposed in app configurations, public code repositories, and overly permissive cloud roles, and their actions “exceeded the authorisation of their assigned tasks.” This makes India the latest stop on what has become a world tour for rogue AI agents. In July, OpenAI’s agents escaped a test environment and hacked French company Hugging Face — kicking off a summer of rogue AI agent activity. Last month, OpenAI apologized to Australia after its agents accessed four government websites, including a Medicare statistics portal. At Hugging Face, OpenAI’s agents started the same way but then found and exploited software flaws to run code on the company’s servers. CERT-In described no such hacking in India, where the agents appear to have simply used the access left lying around. Still, the Indian cases appear to be worse in one respect, which is what the agents saw: In Australia, OpenAI said it found no evidence its agents touched individuals’ medical or criminal records, even after they wrote files inside a Medicare statistics system, while in India, agents read personal data and confidential business records. And unlike earlier incidents, each disclosed by a single lab, India’s case involved agents from multiple providers. CERT-In did not name the providers or affected organizations, or say whether the agents altered any systems. TechCrunch has reached out to CERT-In for more information. For now, the closest parallel seems to be Anthropic’s July disclosure of Claude breaking into three companies’ systems during testing using basic techniques like weak passwords. In India, as in Anthropic’s cases, the affected organizations had not detected the intrusions on their own. The disclosures have left governments relying on AI companies causing these breaches to voluntarily disclose rogue AI agent activity. But most visitors were gathered around one explosively colorful painting in swirling shades of light and dark blue, bottle green, and pale gold. One of the most famous paintings in the world. These were the early days of my research about a little-known woman who is key to shedding light on three inevitable questions when it comes to Vincent van Gogh. Why did he cut off his ear? Why did he commit suicide? And how, after dying in obscurity, did he eventually become regarded as one of the world’s greatest artists? Watching and talking to people as they engaged with this particular masterpiece seemed an apropos way to gauge the interest of Van Gogh lovers. I, for one, could remember, as a child, first seeing The Starry Night reprinted in one of my parents’ art history books. It was my modern art “aha” moment. No one was standing over my shoulder telling me to pay it special attention, but I was mesmerized. The painting became imprinted on my young imagination, and I never forgot it. As I observed the gallery, I quickly learned my reaction is hardly unique. A man from Southern California named Paul Duelo was laughing. “I know this painting, and I don’t know anything about art,” he said with a chuckle, nodding at the crowd standing around The Starry Night.  “Look at everyone!” A woman from Seattle stood gazing quietly with her arms folded. Sheri Larison first saw a picture of The Starry Night more than 30 years ago in her high school art class. A reproduction of the iconic painting hung in her office. This was her very first time seeing the original. “It’s overwhelming,” Larison whispered, with tears in her eyes. “It evokes something. It’s magical.” She was interested in the painting’s origin story. “His mental health was declining,” she said, and that was true, although perhaps also misleading. Van Gogh painted The Starry Night in June of 1889 during a year he spent in a mental asylum in the south of France. It does not represent an actual place but a compilation of elements—a village, starry sky, moon, dark hills, and a cypress tree—all things that Vincent had seen at one time and brought together in this composition. The sight of stars, he said, always made him dream. Hanging just to the left of The Starry Night was a Paul Gauguin’s painting, The Moon and the Earth. The juxtaposition provoked a curious thought. Gauguin and Van Gogh lived and worked together in Vincent’s yellow house in Arles in the south of France for several weeks. What would the hot-tempered, competitive Gauguin have thought seeing so many people gathered around Vincent’s painting at this moment while his was comparatively ignored? And what would Vincent have made of all the attention given to The Starry Night? It is generally believed that it was an argument with Gauguin, who was threatening to quit Arles (in effect abandoning Van Gogh), which spun the mentally unstable Vincent into such a state of despair and frustration that he mutilated himself by slicing off his left ear. "The Starry Night" by Vincent van Gogh at the the Museum of Modern Art in New York. Victor Fraile—Corbis/Getty Images But, as I was learning, family matters also very likely played a part in the most notorious instance of self-harm in art history. It’s a tragic story of dependence and the limits of love. And it’s where Jo van Gogh-Bonger comes in. Biographical details about her—revealed in her diaries and letters—have only recently been made public by the Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam. They paint a picture of a determined woman to whom the world of art owes a tremendous debt. Jo was married to Vincent’s younger brother, Theo, who was an art dealer in Paris and Vincent’s only source of income. Theo supported Vincent with a monthly allowance, and in exchange Vincent gave Theo his paintings to try to market and sell. The financial arrangement, as it would in most families, put an emotional strain on both brothers. Theo was not rich, and Vincent constantly complained about being broke, with not even enough money for food, and often practically begged his brother for more money. Over the course of 10 years, Vincent created more than 800 paintings and 700 drawings, desperately hoping for financial independence and commercial success. But in Vincent’s lifetime, Theo is known to have sold just one painting: Red Vineyard at Arles. In July of 1890, believing himself a failure as an artist and a man, Vincent shot himself in the chest. He was only 37. Six months after Vincent’s suicide, Theo died from the ravages of syphilis, leaving Jo a young widow with a baby to support, not much money, and an unusual inheritance—Vincent’s paintings—more than 400 in total. They filled the walls of their Paris apartment, were stored in closets, under beds, and at a nearby art supply store. The Potato Eaters, Irises, Sunflowers, self-portraits, and The Starry Night. Celebrated as masterpieces today, at the time they were regarded as the unpolished works of a depressed, crazy man and commercially worthless. Jo thought differently. She had no formal art training, but she loved his work and her instincts told her that Vincent was (as Theo had come to believe) a genius. Jo also inherited hundreds of letters that Vincent wrote and received, most exchanged with Theo. Jo read and re-read those letters, initially to feel closer to Theo, but she was also captivated by Vincent’s compassion and intelligence. She decided the world should read them too. It was part of her plan: to organize exhibitions, lobby critics, and publicize the letters, which showed how vulnerable and anguished Vincent was, yes, but also how deliberate he was in his craft and how close Vincent and Theo were to each other. Eventually, in 1914, Jo succeeded in publishing Vincent’s letters in an ambitious three-volume book, which today is considered one of the most revealing and important works of art history. As much as she would play a vital role in propelling him to fame, Jo was also someone who unwittingly played a part in Vincent’s self-destructive descent, something she at times admitted feeling guilt about after his death. Jo understood Vincent was lonely and that he became more isolated after she married his brother. That infamous act of self-mutilation, when Vincent cut off his ear, happened the very day Vincent likely received the surprising news that Theo and Jo were engaged, triggering what some Van Gogh experts, including Martin Bailey, suspect were unquenchable fears that Theo might eventually abandon him. And Vincent shot himself just days after a stressful visit with Jo and Theo in Paris. Theo was thinking about quitting his job to start his own business, which meant, for a time, no income. The couple (who by now had a baby son they named after Vincent) hoped to rent a better apartment on a lower floor. But budgets were tight, arguments ensued, and Vincent eventually rushed out of the apartment, unable to shake the thought that he was a burden that Theo would or could no longer bear. Jo didn’t seek to put herself at the center of this story, but she is there, nonetheless. A surprisingly relatable woman (at times insecure, at times doggedly persistent), she courageously stood up to the male-dominated art world as a champion for Vincent and spent 30 years of her life promoting his work and life story in Holland, Germany, France, England, and even, for a time, in America. Interestingly, of all the paintings Jo inherited, no one—not Jo or Theo or Vincent—considered The Starry Night one of Vincent’s best. Vincent didn’t even sign it. Research recently published by the Van Gogh Museum points out that Vincent signed only about 16% of his paintings, hypothesizing he did so usually when he was completely satisfied or wanted to make them a gift to friends or family. In the brothers’ correspondence, The Starry Night was referred to rather than named. Theo called it simply “the village in the moonlight,” critically writing to Vincent that “I feel that the search for style takes away the real sentiment of things.” Vincent conceded the painting lacked character and considered it a mistake that “…once again I’m allowing myself to do stars too big…new setback, and I’ve had enough of that.” Jo also didn’t seem to consider The Starry Night as especially important. She sold it early on but reacquired it briefly in time for a major exhibition she organized in Amsterdam in 1905. It was painting number 199, called De Sterren (The Stars), and it changed hands two more times before MoMA acquired it in 1941. By then, it had been rechristened The Starry Night. The name stuck. There is a world of personal stories behind the paintings of Vincent van Gogh, and they reaffirm an old adage: we can’t separate the art from the artist. I believe Jo would agree. Paul Duelo, the man from California I met while admiring MoMA’s The Starry Night, lit up when I told him about Jo van Gogh-Bonger. Update your preferences in Account Settings Approaching life with positive expectations, a get-up-and-go attitude, and eagerness is related to many good things in life. This individual asset is often referred to as zest, and it can be purposefully developed to assist us in managing daily stressors as well as creating meaningful lives. True zest is not due to hyperarousal or too much caffeine. It is a cultivated cheerfulness and joy. When we are stressed or not feeling well, it’s hard to avoid doing things halfway or halfheartedly. But there are ways we can increase zest even when it seems like the deck is stacked against us, the world is filled with conflict, and our social media feeds are thick with chaos and gloom. Psychologists Christopher Peterson and Martin Seligman pioneered the field of positive psychology, the scientific study of human flourishing. One of the highlights of their initiatives was published in 2004, Character Strengths and Virtues, a groundbreaking text on the 24 universal character strengths. Zest is one of the 24 strengths. When you cultivate zest, you’ll want to fully engage in life and not just view it from the sidelines, look forward to each day, and wake to anticipation over the day’s possibilities. Zest has also been referred to as vitality, exuberance, and joie de vivre, the French phrase for the joy of living. In an analysis of data from 130 studies involving more than 275,000 people around the globe, most character strengths were associated with greater mental health. But zest was one of just two strengths (the other is hope) that had the strongest relationship to mental well-being across countries and age groups. Zest contributes to life satisfaction among children, adolescents, and adults. In addition, in a study of nearly 10,000 employed adults, those who scored high on zest had higher work satisfaction and were more likely to view work as a calling. People who score high on measures of zest are also more likely to view their lives as meaningful. I reached out to Ryan Niemiec, a psychologist and author of several books, including The Power of Character Strengths and Character Strengths Interventions: A Field Guide for Practitioners. Niemiec is the chief science and education officer at the VIA Institute on Character, a non-profit focused on the science and practice of character strengths. I asked him about the most effective ways people can increase zest in their own lives. “In terms of boosting zest, I turn straight to behavioral activation, one of the most proven strategies for depression,” Niemiec said. “In practical terms, this means to take action in some way to boost oneself—to take that one step forward in lifting one’s energy.” He encouraged people to engage every day in one healthy pleasure, one form of physical activity, and one action that makes you feel like you accomplished something. Some of us seem to naturally have this irresistible aliveness, often invigorating those around us. Others work hard to cultivate zest. Research supports the idea that we are never too old to change. And we can learn how to cultivate zest. We can learn to savor pleasures, set good intentions, and try to view life as an adventure. Other intentional ways to potentially lift our spirits include taking a brisk 10-minute walk every morning, doing cardiovascular or strength-building challenges, practicing Reiki or yoga, laughing with friends, and engaging in stimulating tasks and work. The VIA Institute on Character has activities, workbooks, videos, webinars, and courses—some of them free—on improving all the character strengths, including zest. To be sure, practicing a zest for life can be harder for those who have physical illness and pain and those who engage in unhealthy lifestyle behaviors. And those striving for zest under these circumstances may benefit from assistance from a mental health professional like a psychologist, a physical health provider like a physician, or a personal trainer or fitness instructor. For some of us, being hard on ourselves is second nature. We expect our mental health to stretch under strain. Rather than exclusively focusing on what’s wrong with our mental health, one approach is to develop skills, traits, and perspectives that can help us. Acquiring zest is a worthwhile investment and may help us not only through challenging times, but also help us to see beauty and soar in our lives and workplaces. Update your preferences in Account Settings In 1972, filmmaker William Greaves arranged what we now know was one of the great parties of the 20th century: He commandeered the Harlem apartment of Duke Ellington and arranged for a special group of old friends and colleagues, many of whom had not seen one another in 50 years, to reconnect. The invitees were key figures of the Harlem Renaissance, including muralist Aaron Douglas, poet and novelist Arna Bontemps, society editor Gerri Major, jazz pianist and composer Eubie Blake, and many more. Greaves’ aim was to film the event—he appointed his son, David, as one of the cinematographers—and turn it into a documentary that would serve not just as a record of how these people lived and what they accomplished, but as a way of thinking about how we honor those who are a repository for our past. For a few hours in 1972 Harlem, it was circa 1925 again. This group of people laughing, arguing, and reminiscing about a great era of American history—and also about members of their cohort who were long gone—recaptured lightning in a bottle. And yet the resulting film, Once Upon a Time in Harlem, is one we almost didn’t see. Greaves, who’d made a name for himself with experimental documentaries like Symbiopsychotaxiplasm: Take One (1968), as well as establishing the pioneering public television program Black Journal, considered his Harlem Renaissance film to be one of the most important projects of his life. But although he worked on it off and on for years, he wasn’t able to complete it before his death, in 2014. At last, the film he envisioned has been brought to life by David Greaves and his daughter—William’s granddaughter—producer Liani Greaves. Once Upon a Time in Harlem is one of the great documentaries of its era, and certainly of this year. It’s history that breathes. Ellington's elegantly appointed flat fills up quickly; for the viewer, it’s like dropping into a party right in the middle, as clusters of beautifully dressed people greet one another, embracing, laughing, talking. (Ellington himself was ill and unable to attend.) The guests make themselves at home: Photographer James Van Der Zee, who took extraordinary portraits of the major “show people” of the 1920s, as well as everyday citizens of Harlem, steps up to Ellington’s piano and runs through a few graceful, rippling phrases. We learn that he was trained as a classical musician before turning to photography, just one reminder of the ways people in the arts often need to find a means to support themselves—and sometimes those means can leave us with a lasting record of an era, as we can see from Van Der Zee’s gorgeous, expressive portraits, which flash on the screen like a blessing. Painter and activist Richard Price Nugent, when asked what it was like to be a part of the Renaissance, can’t really say. “While you’re living it, it is the now,” he reflects. Actor Leigh Whipper, the first Black man to join Actors Equity and one of the oldest in the group, and publisher and bookseller Richard B. Moore, recite long poems and passages from the era by memory. Moore calls up Claude McKay’s brilliant, lacerating poem “If We Must Die” as if he’d just learned it yesterday, but you know he’s been living with it for years. Conversations swell through the room in waves, often prompted by Greaves himself, who was not just a filmmaker but also a poet and sculptor, a man who acted and danced. (The film has been brilliantly edited by Anne DeMare and Lynn True.) Ida Mae Cullen, the peppery widow of poet Countee Cullen, pipes up to remind the group, vigorously, that the Harlem Renaissance really lasted for just one decade, the 1920s, though its ripples began years before and resonated for decades after. For this group, politics and art are so entwined as to be indistinguishable. At one point, a vigorous yet civil cross-talking argument breaks out over the principles and philosophy of Back-to-Africa activist Marcus Garvey: there’s mutual respect in this room, but no such thing as consensus. Among other things, Once Upon a Time in Harlem might make you yearn for an era when people, including intellectuals, really knew how to dress: society editor Major, resplendent in a bold, patterned dress, sports about 10 glittery bracelets on each wrist and pulls it off with aplomb. Aaron Douglas, Jean Blackwell Hutson, Richard Bruce Nugent, Eubie Blake, and Irvin C. Miller Courtesy of Neon What’s striking about Once Upon a Time in Harlem is the seemingly casual quality most of these captured conversations. But casual isn’t the same as insignificant. Regina Andrews, a regal woman decked out in a white lace dress and vivid turquoise earrings and necklace, speaks of the job she started as a young woman, as a librarian at the 135th Street branch of the New York Public Library. Shortly after she was hired, her superiors explained that certain poets and authors would often come to the library to work; they had special rooms reserved for them. She and her roommate became friendly with many of these luminaries, among them Cullen and Langston Hughes, and would often host little get-togethers in their apartment. The problem? They didn’t have enough dishes to go around. And so one New Year’s Day, when Andrews and her friends set out on a tour of the area’s Chinese restaurants, the men—unbeknownst to her—collected a few plates here and a few cups there. Suddenly, Andrews had enough dishes and cutlery for everybody, pieces she treasured for years. Maybe that sounds like a minor thing, having enough dishes to entertain your friend group of poets and writers. But as Andrews tells that story, it feels anything but small. “It was just the joy of living and creating together,” she explains—as good a summation as any of what it must have been like to be alive in such a glorious time, a “now” that, thanks to Greaves and his family, becomes a part of our lives too.

In 2022, college sports fan Michael Salazar became a digital subscriber to 247Sports.com, a website that shares news about NCAA football and basketball. In order to register for the website, owned by Paramount Global, Salazar signed up for 247Sports’ online newsletter. And he used his subscription to view sports-related video media. Quietly embedded within those videos, though, was a tracking pixel created by Facebook. Because Paramount installed Facebook’s tracking pixel on the 247Sports website, every time Salazar requested or viewed a video, Paramount automatically disclosed the name and URL of the video to Facebook, along with Salazar’s Facebook ID, the unique identifier Facebook assigns to all of its users. Facebook then used that information to show Salazar targeted advertisements. Privacy violations like this have become a routine part of life on the internet. According to a 2024 study on website privacy, more than half of all companies listed in the S&P 500 index use the “Meta pixel” on their sites, sharing people’s information with both Facebook and Instagram. But in theory, there are at least some legal boundaries. Back in 1988, after a D.C. journalist obtained and published a list of movies rented by Supreme Court nominee Robert Bork, a scandalized Congress passed the Video Privacy Protection Act. To be clear: Congress was scandalized by the nonconsensual disclosure of Bork’s rental history, not the judge’s viewing habits, which were pretty unremarkable (unlike those of another judicial nominee who would be confirmed to the Supreme Court a few years later.) The VPPA prohibits videotape service providers from knowingly disclosing the personally identifiable information of consumers, unless they get the consumers’ written consent. The statute defines “videotape service provider” to include any person in the business of delivering “prerecorded video cassette tapes or similar audio visual materials.” And the definition of “consumer” includes any “subscriber of goods or services” from the provider. Here, Salazar subscribed to Paramount’s sports website. He requested audiovisual materials from Paramount, and Paramount delivered them. And then Paramount knowingly disclosed what he watched to Facebook without getting his consent. In September 2022, Salazar filed a class-action lawsuit, arguing that Paramount violated the VPPA. But in July 2023, the federal district court dismissed his complaint, reasoning that Salazar didn’t count as a “consumer” under the statute because he was only subscribed to a newsletter, “not audio visual content.” Salazar appealed to the 6th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, but in April 2025, a divided three-judge panel affirmed the dismissal, agreeing with the district court that Salazar’s newsletter subscription didn’t make him a subscriber under the law. Then Salazar appealed to the Supreme Court, which heard oral arguments in Salazar v. Paramount on Wednesday. Under the plain text of the Video Privacy Protection Act, Salazar should be able to hold Paramount liable for its disclosure of his video history. But during the oral arguments, the Republican-nominated justices peppered Salazar’s attorney with questions suggesting that imposing an atextual limitation onto the statute—one that would conveniently insulate corporations from legal liability when they intrude on consumers’ privacy for profit—was really just the commonsense thing to do. “Wouldn’t it be natural to think that goods or services from a videotape service provider would be video-related services?” asked Justice Clarence Thomas. Later in the oral argument, Thomas said he “always thought of 247Sports as a sports site,” and he questioned the “logic” of viewing the site as a “videotape service provider.” Justice Samuel Alito further argued that the law was “based on the technology of the 1980s,” when people had to “go to a Blockbuster store and get a VHS tape.” Although the text of the VPPA appears to cover Salazar’s claims, Alito suggested that a newsletter subscription isn’t analogous enough to what Congress had in mind and asked Salazar’s counsel why he thought the “smart lower court judges” who ruled against him rejected his “very literal interpretation” of the statute. Justice Brett Kavanaugh echoed these concerns: “We are a textualist Court but usually not a literalist Court,” he said. Kavanaugh continued, “Congress in the 1980s wasn’t thinking about data sharing for targeted advertising.” Kavanaugh and Justice Amy Coney Barrett also worried that, if the VPPA weren’t limited to the long-defunct Blockbusters of the world, practically any business could find themselves liable under the law. Kavanaugh brought up concerns, raised by corporate interest groups in amicus briefs, that “every business in America that has video on its website” could be targeted. Trying to suss out the scope of the statute, Kavanaugh and Barrett both asked how long a video must be for a company to count as a video service provider. Salazar’s attorney indicated that length wasn’t especially relevant and shifted the conversation to the business and profit motive of delivering videos, but Barrett was skeptical. “If I’m on Nordstrom’s website and I click to see the model show me what the shoes are going to look like,” said Barrett, “that’s a profit motive.” For all the Republican justices’ stated concern on Wednesday about reading and interpreting the VPPA in a way that makes sense, they failed to meaningfully engage with the text and context. Paramount provided Salazar with videos and then told Facebook what videos he watched without his consent—the exact kind of injury contemplated by the statute. El Reg digs its claws into HP's N1X-powered OmniBook Ultra 16 Nvidia's RTX Spark partner systems are here, and spec-for-spec they've got all the ingredients for a hit: a potent CPU, blisteringly fast integrated graphics, and up to 128 GB of unified memory in a lightweight, ultra-premium package that's ready to run demanding models locally. But with prices for these systems starting around $2,600 and going well north of $6,000 for the top spec trims, the one thing RTX Spark isn't is a good deal. However, for the right person, by which we mostly mean AI enthusiasts, researchers, and/or creative professionals who need tons of video memory and don't want to get lost in Apple's walled garden or would prefer Nvidia's CUDA ecosystem over AMD's ROCm, the RTX Spark still has a lot to offer. As such, we'll be largely focusing on the local agentic AI use cases Nvidia and Microsoft are pushing for these "builder-class" systems. The Register had the opportunity this week to put the OmniBook Ultra, HP's unique spin on the RTX Spark platform, through a series of AI benchmarks to see just how well it performs. And spoiler alert: you'll be hard pressed to find a faster Windows notebook for local AI. But before diving into performance, let's take a closer look at the hardware itself. Lifting the lid on the OmniBook Ultra 16, the first thing you'll notice is the gorgeous 2880x1800 pixel OLED touchscreen with a 16:10 aspect ratio. Touch screens on professional notebooks can be a contentious topic. You either love them or hate them, but at the end of the day, if the idea of fingerprints obscuring your work gives you pause, no one is forcing you to use it, and we're happy to see it here. The OmniBook Ultra's display is mounted to a surprisingly stiff hinge mechanism mounted to the top of the chassis. This has the benefit of keeping the hinge out of the way of the notebook's ample exhaust, which is kind of important because the 20-core N1X system-on-chip powering our review unit runs hot. The system's ample venting keeps most of that heat concentrated around the base of the display and for the most part, away from the keyboard. Speaking of keyboards, the OmniBook Ultra's digit fidgets are a bit of a throwback. Instead of the now-ubiquitous chiclet keys popularized by Apple in the early '10s, HP has gone for a more traditional notebook keyboard, which we found refreshingly easy to type on. Keyboards of course are subjective so if there's any question on whether this one is right for you, it might be worth a trip to your local big box store to give one a good pecking before you commit. Framing the keyboard are a pair of speaker grilles backed by four Cirrus Logic amplifiers which sound excellent for a Windows notebook, but can't quite match the clarity and bass of Apple's MacBook Pros. With that said, we don't expect anyone will be disappointed by the OmniBook Ultra's speakers. Directly below the keyboard is a haptic trackpad that is as precise as it is enormous. No product in this price class should be shipping with a diving board mechanism in 2026, so it's nice to see HP didn't cheap out on us. Moving to the sides of the notebook, you'll find a refreshingly comprehensive set of ports including: Our only real critique here is that the OmniBook Ultra, like other laptops, lacks a full size SD card slot. MicroSD cards are convenient, but they're not widely used in professional environments and we'd have preferred to see a full size SD card reader or better yet a CFexpress card reader. However, it's rare if not impossible to see these on notebooks. The chassis itself weighs in at 4.89 pounds and is milled from aluminum with a remarkably premium-feeling finish on the palm rest, lid, and bottom panels. HP has done a nice job building a notebook that feels as premium as it is powerful. While the OmniBook Ultra is an objectively nice machine, the main attraction of any of the RTX Spark systems is going to be the N1X chip powering them. The SoC features a MediaTek-derived CPU chiplet that's melded using advanced packaging and Nvidia's NVLink Fusion chip-to-chip interconnect to a Blackwell GPU. The N1X comes in two flavors: An entry level version with 18 CPU cores and a GPU with 5,120 CUDAs. Meanwhile, the highest-end SKU — the one found in our review unit — features up to 20 CPU cores and a GPU with 6,144 CUDA cores. If you're wondering, that's exactly the same number as found in Nvidia's RTX 5070 just with access to a whole lot more memory. We'll note that not all of those CPU cores are created equal. Like most Arm-based SoCs, the N1X contains a mix of big and little cores. Specifically, it has an equal complement of Arm X925 and Cortex A725 cores. While all of this sounds impressive, it's worth noting that the chip really isn't all that new. The N1X is based heavily on the GB10, which launched in the Linux-based DGX Spark over a year ago. With that said, there have been some improvements. At Hot Chips 2025, we learned the GB10 would support memory up to 9,400 MT/s. But, when the DGX Spark finally arrived that fall, it was only sporting 8,533 MT/s memory. The N1X rectifies this by boosting memory bandwidth to around 300 GB/s. Higher memory bandwidth usually translates to faster token generation in AI inference and agentic workloads. So, while a fairly modest boost in bandwidth over the DGX Spark's 273 GB/s, it's still a meaningful one. There aren't many modern workloads that really need 128 GB of unified memory, but local AI certainly is one of them. While you can certainly get away with less, it opens the door to models with a decent shot at replacing something like Claude Code or Codex. In fact, Microsoft believes that the future of Windows will be that of Copilot+ PCs that can run a mix of local and remote models with prompts routed between them based on things like complexity or privacy. Redmond is calling this technique hybrid intelligence. But even with 128 GB of shared memory on board, you're still somewhat limited on what models can actually fit. That's because, unlike on Linux, which allocates memory dynamically to the DGX Spark's GPU as needed, Windows isn't quite asur testi smart. The HP OmniBook Ultra arrived with a 50:50 split with 64 GB dedicated to the CPU and GPU respectively. This can be reconfigured as needed, but it's not exactly ideal. The good news is that, with the right software, you can get around this. Llama.cpp, one of the most popular local LLM inference frameworks, allows for overflowing into CPU memory as needed and, since the CPU and GPU share the same memory, the performance penalty isn't nearly as stark as it would when using a dedicated GPU. El Reg is excited to bring you more hardware coverage like this, but we're only just getting started. Ideally we'd pit the RTX Spark-based OmniBook Ultra against a similarly specced AMD Strix Halo notebook or Apple M5 Max MacBook Pro. Unfortunately we don't have those systems in our database just yet. In their place we're including performance figures for the DGX Spark and HP's Strix Halo-based Z2-Mini G1A. But because those aren't notebooks, we recognize it's not an ideal comparison. Having said that, you have to fit a lot more than just the model's weights into memory. For agentic AI harnesses like Claude Code, models need to support large context windows with 128,000 tokens being a sweet spot these days. Depending on the model, that can chew up an awful lot of memory. There is also the issue of the available models. While you could theoretically cram a 200 billion parameter model onto an RTX Spark using Llama.cpp, there aren't really many models that size. Since Nvidia's RTX Spark platform ships with Windows, we conducted all of our tests using the v0.6.0 build of Llama.cpp across five models developed by Meta, Google, and Alibaba in sizes ranging from around 26 to 122 billion parameters. Our first test simulates a chatbot-like environment tasking each system and model to process and generate a 256 token input and output. There are two metrics worth paying attention to here: Time to first token (TTFT) measures the time in milliseconds required for the GPU to process the prompt and generally reflects the GPU's compute performance. Tokens per second (Tok/s) is a little more obvious and reflects how quickly the system can churn out tokens once the prompt has been processed. Also called decode, token generation is usually dependent on memory bandwidth, so faster memory tends to perform better here. Across our test suite, we see performance scales neatly with model size and active parameter count. On paper, Strix Halo offers less performance and memory bandwidth than either Nvidia chip and we've repeatedly shown this in testing over the past year. AMD's saving grace is that it has consistently offered better value. While our single batch and context scaling benchmarks are useful for quantifying system performance across models, they aren't necessarily representative of how you're likely to use local models today. If our numbers seem a little low compared to other benchmarks for the N1X and GB10, that's because our single batch results don't take advantage of prefill caching or speculative decoding. If you're not familiar, prefill caching helps to speed up prompt processing time for information the model has already seen before. Speculative decoding meanwhile involves using a small draft model to predict the outputs of a larger one. In highly repetitive workloads, like agentic coding, speculative decoding speeds up token generation by 2-3x without any loss in quality. AI agents, like code assistants, pass over the same prompts, code bases, and resources recursively, which makes them prime candidates for both techniques. Smarter but slower models can still be faster if they use fewer tokens to arrive at the correct answer. And we see that here. Large models like Qwen3.5-122B-A10B ran slower on our test systems, generating tokens at roughly half the rate of the smaller 35B parameter model. Nvidia's RTX Spark platform is no different. During our testing, we observed issues with thermal throttling or rather an apparent lack thereof. Under a constant AI inference load, we experienced occasional lock ups and, on one occasion, graphical artifacts as the GPU exceeded 90 degrees Celsius. AI inference and training are some of the most resource-intensive workloads ever developed and to call them resource viruses wouldn't be inaccurate. So it's not surprising that a sustained inference load over the course of several hours might overwhelm the thin-and-light's thermal management system. Under real world conditions we don't expect this will be an issue. Even a few seconds between prompts was enough for the notebook to remain stable and artifact free. With that said, if you were hoping to fine tune LLMs, you might want to look at Nvidia's DGX Spark or any one of the other GB10-based AI workstations from HP, Dell, Lenovo, and others. Their larger chassis and thermal management systems make them far better candidates for the often hours-long process of fine tuning models. But at around $3,200 for the base model OmniBook Ultra 16 with the N1X and $6,229 as tested here, none of these systems are cheap. Last week, Cloudflare announced that it had purchased Dahl’s company Deno, which was built around an open source server-side JavaScript runtime of the same name that he created, to compete with the Node.js runtime, which he also created. Nonetheless, Deno has no future at Cloudflare, which will maintain the runtime for another year before ending its development. For Cloudflare, the Deno buy is an acquihire for Dahl and 12 of his Deno colleagues, who will work to build out Cloudflare’s Workers platform, expanding it for potential open-source use. He feels this approach is the way to go. “Right now, every other server is really operating at the Linux VM layer. That is not what Cloudflare Workers are," he said. Dahl’s Node.js, which he presented to the world in 2009, sparked a revolution in Web application architecture. He was fed up with the Apache web server hogging all the memory.  Using the Google V8 JavaScript engine, Node not only could execute JavaScript code on the server, but it could concurrently serve thousands of requests per second with a fraction of the memory required by the traditional choice of the time, Apache Web server. Today, Node powers about 30 million websites worldwide. With 130 million downloads, it probably runs a lot more internal operations as well. The ever-fickle Dahl first debuted Deno in 2018 as a rewrite of Node. The name ‘Deno’ is an anagram of Node, and it was Dahl’s attempt to fix everything he felt he got wrong with Node – built-in fetch, a permission model, native TypeScript support et al. Sequoia backed Dahl’s company for $21 million in 2022, and as of August, Deno amassed 400,000 monthly users. Nonetheless, Node’s gravity proved too heavy for Deno to achieve escape velocity. “What we found over years of working on Deno is that you can't really deviate from Node.js very much," Dahl told The Register. "As we continued to try to get adoption for Deno, we found we had to essentially backpedal on some of the original promises and become more Node compatible over time, and that left us as a kind of an alternative Node.js implementation.” Going forward, Cloudflare will supply Deno with bug fixes and security patches for the next year, while Dahl looks for a new home for the MIT-licensed open source project (the OpenJS Foundation perhaps?). In August, Dahl released an entirely new project, called celld, which was an open source implementation of Cloudflare’s Durable Objects. He was fascinated by the technology, originally created by Kenton Varda. “A primitive this good deserves to run anywhere,” the celld page stated. Durable Objects allow developers to build stateful, real-time distributed applications. Unlike Node or Deno, the resulting application doesn’t require a dedicated runtime environment. More than 5 million people around the world with age-related macular degeneration (AMD) experience atrophy in their retinas. And until now, no treatment could restore their vision. The PRIMA system, which was approved in Europe in July and is awaiting FDA clearance in the US, changes that. It combines a retinal implant with camera-­equipped eyeglasses to improve sight in patients who develop a blind spot in the middle of their vision due to advanced AMD. After training sessions, more than 80% of recipients were able to read, do crosswords, and use their phones. Developed by the California-based Science Corporation, the system takes advantage of the fact that AMD doesn’t damage all retinal circuitry; only photoreceptors—the cells that convert light into an electrical signal—die off. PRIMA’s implant mimics those cells and transmits visual information directly to healthy eye circuitry and on to the brain. “We’re leveraging all of the body’s own natural functions to process and transmit the information,” says Frank Brodie, an ophthalmologist at the University of California, San Francisco, and Science Corp.'s chief medical officer for ophthalmology. Glasses outfitted with a miniature front-facing camera capture what someone is seeing in real time. The video feed is roughly five megapixels running at 30 frames per second, which is similar to TV. The raw video is transmitted to a connected processor via a fiber-optic cable. A small, handheld processor, which can be set on a table or tucked in a pocket, converts the video feed into infrared light signals that route back to the glasses. The device also includes physical buttons that let the wearer adjust contrast, brightness, and zoom. A laser projector on the glasses faces the wearer and shines pulses of near-infrared light directly at the eye at 880 nanometers—an invisible wavelength. This signal passes through the pupil and on to the retinal implant in the back of the eye. The implant is made up of 378 pixels, each with an electrode and two photovoltaic cells, which do double duty. They convert the light into a current that healthy bipolar and ganglion cells pass on to the brain. They also act as solar cells to power the chip, eliminating the need for a battery. A study published earlier this year found that four in five teens under 16 made their way onto barred platforms. Teens have successfully explored many tactics for bypassing bans, from using a sibling’s face for ID to drawing fake facial hair. Some proponents of bans have called for an increased focus on enforcement that blocks these workarounds. But circumvention is a signal, not a nuisance or something to be simply deterred. The fact that kids are actively looking for ways to outwit the bans highlights their limitations. Social media bans suppress kids’ access to information, including information that’s essential to their development, identity formation, and socialization, especially during adolescence. Worse, further restriction is likely to backfire. Closing loopholes in age gates could harm kids by pushing them toward less-moderated, higher-risk corners of the web. It also degrades privacy and security for everyone by forcing people to submit sensitive personal information to prove their age. In our work, we’ve found that parents and kids both want smarter, more nuanced policies that protect children, families, and their rights. That can take the form of mandating stronger limits on data collection by companies, or giving families flexible tools to control content and access on their own terms. Age gates won’t bring about the desired change, nor will they simplify parenting in the digital world. At best, they will only raise thornier questions and postpone the conversation to a child’s 16th birthday. At worst, they’ll leave kids with no guidance as they make their way through the online world. When young people circumvent restrictions, they are often doing what we want developing minds to do and what we celebrate in other contexts: lean into curiosity, problem-solve, and exercise independence. It’s important for kids to do this online, an arena where they often develop social skills and refine their identities. Bans sweep these motivations under the rug, taking a paternalistic approach that doesn’t consider young people’s needs or the realities of what they do online. It also ignores what some parents want. When we examined social media safety interventions last year, parents told us that they often join their kids in lies that help them gain access to needed but gated services. This isn’t a new phenomenon; research all the way back in 2011 showed that parents taught their children how to evade Facebook age restrictions. Some may say these parents are oblivious to the harms posed by the web or just don’t care. But parents are not a monolithic group. Many simply see offering their children responsible access to social media as a way to help them connect with like-minded peers, find informational content, and learn digital safety skills early on. Many parents believe the benefits outweigh the risks. Some platforms automatically track users’ online activities to confirm their age. In doing so, they profile users—collecting and analyzing their data to make inferences about them, which can increase the risk that data will be misused or leaked. Companies may also ask for personal documents like driver’s licenses to verify age, which can lead to mishandling of sensitive documents or even identity theft. Data breaches happen: Last year, for example, Discord reported that one of its third-party services, 5CA, which is used to confirm age, had been compromised, exposing at least 70,000 images of users’ government IDs. It can take little effort to find ways to use that kind of information for malicious purposes by, say, connecting driver’s licenses to specific accounts and private online activity, which could reveal anything from users’ political opinions to their sexual preferences. Efforts to make it harder for kids to get around age gates have also led policymakers to clamp down on kids’ access to VPNs. State age verification bills in Michigan and Utah have tried to interfere with the privacy protections offered by these tools (the Utah bill is now blocked by a federal court), and Australia’s eSafety Commissioner is nudging social media platforms to detect and block their use. But requiring adult users to prove their age to gain access to a VPN fundamentally defeats the purpose of that privacy-focused technology. It can put many users, particularly journalists and human rights defenders working on sensitive issues, in danger of government surveillance and even retaliation. Rather than attempting to block young people from online services that allow them to access information and engage in age-essential communication, policymakers should focus on the incentives that drive companies to engage in behavior that creates risks for kids in the first place. For example, platforms should decrease what experts call unhealthy engagement or addictive activity by limiting the collection and use of data that companies use to target content and features to all users, including children. Nearly all parents and teens we spoke with want policymakers to impose such restrictions, knowing full well that the companies’ use of data is at the core of features such as targeted advertisements. Both teens and parents are also frustrated by features designed to keep us all online for longer than we want. Another approach would be to expand the availability of user-controlled tools so that families have easy, flexible, and user-friendly ways to limit access to what is undesirable. For example, keyword-based content filtering can enable a student who is targeted with a specific nickname or inside joke to block any post or comment containing it. Platforms are unlikely to know which word or term a child might want to block, nor can policy seek to address such dynamic types of harm. Policy can, however, offer tools that would put power back in users’ hands. Poor privacy practices, anxiety-producing content streams, and a near-inescapable need to use  digital technologies for everyday tasks, communication, and information-seeking have rightfully led parents and teens to want real change in how companies conduct business. Circumvention shows where policy falls short. Stricter enforcement will mean yet more data collection, and it won’t meet kids and parents where they are.
