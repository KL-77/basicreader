A strange thing is happening in the used EV market. Supply is up thanks to an ever-increasing stream of ex-lease cars reaching the market, and yet prices have been going up as well. As a result of America’s latest war in the Middle East, used EVs have appreciated by just over 5 percent since February. Despite the stronger pricing, used EV sales have been strong this year—up 25 percent year over year by May, although a weaker June has brought overall sales up just over 20 percent year over year, according to the analysts at Cox Automotive. Used EVs also hang around on dealer lots a lot less than new ones—the same analysts at Cox found just 38 days’ supply of used EVs in June, but 81 days for new EVs. And much of the demand is at the cheaper end of the spectrum, say the analysts at Recurrent. In previous installments of our used EV buyers’ guide, we’ve already looked at the most affordable used EVs, ending last time at $25,000—EVs that may now qualify for a $1,750 rebate if you’re in California. Today we’re looking at used EVs that cost between $25,000 and $30,000, and according to Autotrader’s search, there are more than 9,000 used EVs for sale in that price range. Broadly, you can split the results into three buckets: Cars where you’ll find plenty to choose from; ones where there might be a couple of hundred for sale nationwide; and then the oddities where there might just be a handful to pick from. We are now several years down the road from the debut of several mass-market EVs from mainstream brands, and it’s not surprising that there’s the greatest choice among models that have been strong sellers. Chevrolet is well-represented, with about 750 Blazer EVs and another 550 Equinox EVs available in our price band. The smaller Equinox EV starts new at not that much more, so used ones should be low mileage and well-specced. Neither will have Apple CarPlay nor Android Auto, which is a sticking point for a lot of you out there. There’s a decent selection of Ford Mustang Mach-Es in this price range, too, with almost 700 to choose from around the country. At this price, you’ll find Mach-Es as recent as model year 2025, but I would be quite tempted by one of the 50 Mach-E GTs I found for sale. These will only be MY23 cars, and they are less efficient than other Mach-E variants, but they also ride much better thanks to magnetorheological dampers. Although Hyundai’s ICCU reliability has commenters running from Korean EVs, the automaker recently extended the ICCU warranty to 15 years/180,000 miles, so we still feel comfortable recommending its E-GMP-based EVs, like the Ioniq 5 and Ioniq 6. There are more than 1,000 Ioniq 5s to pick from, the newest being MY25 cars. Kia’s EV6 shares the same platform, but you’ll only find half as many on used lots. Some prefer the styling of the Kia to the angular Ioniq 5. Given Tesla’s enormous market share for EVs, it’s no surprise that you’ll find thousands of them for sale, used, in our price range. There are about the same number of used Nissan Ariyas as Volkswagen ID.4s—roughly 400 of each for sale used. The Nissan is an underappreciated option in my opinion, with a better interior than the VW. There are also a couple hundred Honda Prologues, which are essentially the Chevy Blazer EV but with Apple CarPlay. The closely related Toyota bZ4x and Subaru Solterra also show up in roughly the same volumes; these will be the older models with CCS1 charge ports and the less-efficient first-generation powertrains. And if you want something a little smaller, there are low-mileage Kia Niro EVs on offer too. Sedan lovers should consider the Hyundai Ioniq 6 and Polestar 2. The former is wonderfully efficient and looks a lot better in person than two-dimensionally on a screen. The latter is now from an OEM that has exited the US market, which may well put some people off, but the cars can be serviced at Volvo dealers, so don’t be scared. At this price, we found a mix of older single-motor FWD cars, single-motor RWD cars (Polestar switched for MY24), plus twin-motor AWD versions. Or stick with Volvo itself. There are about three times as many C40 coupes available as the XC40 Recharge, but quite a lot of people prefer the looks of the fastback, so this is no bad thing. Audi’s Q4 e-tron crossover is basically a more premium VW ID.4 and does little to stand out from the alternatives here. The same can’t be said for the BMW i4, which now starts appearing in my searches as we passed the $25,000 floor. For this money, we’re looking at the i4 eDrive35, which is the less-powerful RWD version. But it’s also the most efficient, and in my opinion, the single-motor i4s are much sweeter to drive than the AWD ones (although I’ve only driven the eDrive40, not the eDrive35). An Ioniq 5, Mach-E GT, or i4 would be my picks of the bunch so far, but here are some other alternatives we found. Some I mostly include as a warning—probably safer to avoid the few Vinfast VF8s and Jaguar I-Paces out there, as well as the handful of Jeep Wagoneer Ses that have depreciated to this price range. While I would say the I-Pace was a fun-to-drive EV, our managing editor Eric Bangeman had no end of trouble with his and now drives a Mach-E instead. As for the Vinfast and Jeep, both are among the worst EVs I’ve driven to date. I wasn’t much of a fan of the Lexus RZ 450e, either (although it’s by no means as bad as the Jeep or Vinfast), a few of which you’ll find for less than $30,000. Less common options that still come with a fancy badge include the Mercedes GLB crossover and the original Audi e-Tron if you want something German, or the Cadillac Lyriq and Acura ZDX if you want an EV made in America. The Acura and Caddy will be harder to find, with the latter being mostly high-mileage ex-livery cars. You’ll also find some very nearly new Nissan Leafs and some Chevy Bolt EUVs; the Leafs include the current model that starts at under $30,000 (if you ignore the destination charge), and Chevy will sell you a new Bolt with an LFP battery pack from less than $30,000 new, so if that’s the model you’re looking for, you might be able to find either one brand new. For a small city car, the Mini Cooper SE is a hoot to drive, but limited in range. And then there are the real rarities. I was surprised to find only three Genesis GV60s in this price range; it’s smaller than the related EV6 or Ioniq 5, but with a higher spec and better interior, and its smaller size means it works great in the city. A Ford F-150 Lightning Pro might be a possibility at under $30,000 if you’re patient and if you need a pickup and don’t mind a work spec. And similar patience may turn up a Kia EV9 if you need something voluminous for the school run. I have to say, I’d be very tempted by the BrightDrop delivery vans, several of which are now within our price range—start here if you want to build yourself an electric camper wagon. For the really brave, you might even slip into one of the really fancy EV sedans. We found eight Mercedes-Benz EQSs, making it almost common compared to the single Lucid Air Grand Touring that showed up. Either of those is a lot of car for $30,000, but that goes double for the lone Audi e-Tron GT and the only Porsche Taycan (a 4S) that also appeared. They drive quite differently from each other despite sharing a platform; the Audi has a more usable interior with fewer touchscreens, and the Porsche has the better badge and keener handling. I mean, yes, plenty could go wrong with a $30,000 electric Porsche that has servicing bills belonging to a car that cost four times as much new. But I imagine it could be rather fun finding out.

Less than 2.5% of Taylor Farms' recalled lettuce went to Taco Bells

Although shredded iceberg lettuce at Taco Bell restaurants has been a prime suspect in an explosive outbreak of the diarrheal parasite Cyclospora, a new report from the Food and Drug Administration finally reveals where all the contaminated lettuce was sold—and only a sliver of it went to Taco Bell locations. The feces-tainted lettuce was sourced from Mexico and sold by Taylor Farms, a mammoth produce provider based in California. Taylor Farms has drawn scrutiny for its ties to the Trump administration and reluctance to provide information amid the outbreak, which has now sickened over 23,000 people across 47 states, killing two and sending over 500 others to the hospital. Taylor Farms reportedly attempted to delay issuing a recall amid the illnesses and, since then, has refused to clearly report how much tainted lettuce it recalled and where all of it had been shipped. The FDA’s Enforcement Report finally provides some answers: Taylor Farms recalled 236,192 cases of lettuce due to potential Cyclospora contamination. Of those, only 5,900—just shy of 2.5 percent—were sold to Yum, the parent company of Taco Bell, Pizza Hut, and KFC. The other 97.5 percent of the lettuce that potentially sickened Americans was distributed elsewhere. Walmart—under its brand Marketside—received the lion’s share, 109,476 cases, a little over 46 percent. The second-largest lot went to Taylor Farms’ own brand, which received 68,897 cases, a little over 29 percent. The remaining 25 percent went to various food service entities and two restaurants. Cross Valley Farms, a brand from US Foods with the tagline “Fresh Produce Sourced and Selected With Strict Standards,” received 19,016 cases (8 percent). Sysco received 11,519 cases (4.9 percent), Markon got 7,459 cases (3.15 percent), and Peak Fresh Produce (Performance Foodservice) got 720 cases (0.3 percent). The restaurant chains besides Taco Bell were Subway, which received 8,075 cases (3.4 percent), and Jack in the Box, which received 5,130 cases (about 2.2 percent). The information in the FDA report is significantly more detailed than what Taylor Farms provided in its recall information, which only lists abbreviations for the companies that received the recalled lettuce. For instance, one brand is identified as just “CV,” while the FDA report identifies it as Cross Valley Farms. Walmart’s Marketside brand was listed as MKTSD, while food service company Markon was listed as MARK. The notice provides no quantities. It’s unclear why Taylor Farms did not publicly reveal that the majority of the recalled lettuce had been shipped to Walmart stores while Americans were focusing on Taco Bell locations. Ars Technica reached Taylor Farms for comment about the lack of information, but the company did not respond to questions and instead sent links to its recall notice and the FDA’s outbreak investigation webpage. It also remains unclear where the food service companies that received the lettuces ultimately sent them and if they were sent to more states than the 27 Taylor Farms reported in its recall. For instance, New York and California have reported cyclosporiasis cases, but those states are not included in the recall notice. The FDA and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention based warnings about Taco Bell lettuce on epidemiological investigations. With widespread distribution of the lettuce, investigators were able to detect a clear signal from Taco Bell. On July 17, the CDC told Americans in no uncertain terms, “Do not eat shredded iceberg lettuce served at Taco Bell locations in Indiana, Kentucky, Michigan, Ohio, and West Virginia.” The FDA, meanwhile, noted that Walmart’s Marketside brand was part of the recall, but it did not indicate that it constituted the bulk of the recall. The FDA report was first identified by independent food-safety reporter Phyllis Entis. This post was updated to report that Taylor Farms declined to answer questions.

Tracking extreme heat by the hour makes climate change seem even worse

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-08-11

Visit Original Website
Global warming is leading to more extremely hot weather. There is perhaps no more obvious consequence of climate change. But changing how we analyze this problem can still help scientists think about it in a different way that may yield surprises. Generally, studies on heat extremes work with some combination of daily minimum, maximum, and mean temperatures. Sometimes they focus on multi-day heatwaves using those metrics, since the discomfort and health impacts increase for longer events. There are different thresholds you can use to define extreme heat in these analyses, either based on past statistics or on the human body’s physiological limits. A new study led by Weilin Liao and Xuanzong Zhang at Sun Yat-sen University tried breaking down heat extremes to the hourly level instead, which reveals some interesting nuances. The researchers calculated each hour of the day separately, comparing 8 am temperatures only to other 8 am temperatures. They defined extreme heat as anything above the 90th percentile of recorded temperatures at that time of day between 1981 and 2010. To put this in context, the global average over the last four decades has been that people experience about 270 hours of “hourly heat extremes” each summer. But that average obscures a very obvious trend:  that number has been increasing by 62 hours each decade. To project this into the future, the researchers used climate model results from two scenarios of future greenhouse gas emissions—one with medium emissions leading to 3°C (about 5.5°F) warming by 2100, and one with high emissions and 5°C (9°F) warming. Comparing the past couple of decades to 2070–2099, the total number of hourly heat extremes triples in the medium scenario and increases by 4.5 times in the high scenario. For every half a degree C (almost 1°F) of global average temperature increase, the study shows an increase of about 200 hot hours per summer. The trend is worst in the tropics, even though overall warming is stronger at higher latitudes. The researchers say this is because temperature variability increases in the tropics, making extremes more frequent. The number of hot hours also increases more at night than it does during the day. This is where the hourly analysis can start to seem more relatable than metrics of temperature change or counts of days. Days that fail to qualify as “hot days” still see an increase of several hot hours—mostly at night.  This wouldn’t show up if you just counted the number of hot days. And these shorter hot periods often precede or follow hot days, extending the stress a heatwave puts on your body. Obviously, those hot days also see an increase in hot hours. Right now, the average hot day contains about 16 hot hours, but that increases to 19 hours in the medium scenario and 21 hours in the high scenario. So the miserable part of the day gets longer, reducing the hours that grant some relief. The researchers also looked at differences in these trends for countries at different income levels and compared the lifetime total exposure to hot hours for people born in 1981 and 2023. Low-income countries see the strongest increase in hot hours. But even at the global average, those born in 2023 would experience about 1.4 to 1.8 times as many hot hours, depending on the greenhouse gas emissions scenario. While the overall trend in hot weather is well known, the hourly details matter. They highlight that the number of hot hours is increasing even faster than the number of hot days, for example. But the details also help us better represent what this means for human health. The researchers write, “As heat risks continue to intensify, hourly-scale assessments will become essential for anticipating health impacts, informing equitable adaptation strategies and designing responsive early warning systems that reflect the new reality of round-the-clock heat exposure.” Nature Climate Chane, 2026. In 2018, 20,000 Google employees worldwide made headlines when they staged a walkout to protest how the company had handled sexual misconduct allegations against key corporate executives, among other grievances. One of the organizers was Claire Stapleton, a communications specialist whose distinctive voice and ability to channel the corporate ethos had once earned her the internal moniker “Bard of Google.” Six months later, a disillusioned Stapleton resigned, claiming she had been demoted during the most recent restructuring as retaliation for helping to spearhead the walkout. The story of how she went from enthusiastic cheerleader to proactive dissident, and the many lessons she learned along the way, are featured in her new book, Don’t Be Evil: Bad Bosses, Fake Promises, and My Escape from Big Tech—A Biting Memoir of Twelve Years Inside Google. Stapleton didn’t set out to be a rabble-rouser. When she joined Google out of college in 2007, it was like “getting the golden ticket in Willy Wonka,” she told Ars. Awareness of her good fortune offset any initial uneasiness or skepticism, although Stapleton was sufficiently independent-minded not to move into what was then Google’s new corporate housing development in Mountain View, California. Stapleton was very good at her job, and she quickly moved up the ranks, playing a key role in shaping the company’s public image. Her first truly toxic experience came when she transferred to Google Creative Lab in New York, an environment she describes as “half utopian, half dystopian and unable to tell the difference.” She received little direction or guidance from management and found that much of the Lab’s work was simply advancing a kind of “cloying techno-optimism” she found uninspiring. Stapleton lasted about a year and ended up joining the marketing team at YouTube. But the cultural changes and her own shifting priorities and growing disquiet culminated in the 2018 walkout and Stapleton leaving the company she once championed. Stapleton wrote an essay for Elle magazine shortly after that she describes as “a psychic attempt at wrapping up my time” at Google, to help process her complicated feelings about her former employer. “It was such an abrupt and kind of traumatic ending after such a long time at the company,” she said. “And a lot of my experience was really positive.” The essay garnered enough attention to draw the interest of book agents, but at the time, Stapleton wasn’t interested in writing a book, feeling that she didn’t really have much more to say. Instead, she started a workplace advice column and did freelance marketing communications for a venture capital firm founded by another former Googler, Bill Maris. “There were a lot of ex-Googlers there, and we talked a lot about our experiences,” Stapleton said. At some point, she started wondering if they had unwittingly been in a cult, she said. “The way we were processing the separation was a bit like that,” she said. “It’s bigger than a job. It’s like an identity. It’s very emotional and psychological. It got me thinking. How did we, as an industry, end up with such whiplash from our experience of working at these companies? Big Tech in general has gone from being incredibly admired, like Google, to being parables of the perils and pitfalls of large corporations, corporate overreach, and having way too much power and personal data.” What happened to change a company that once famously embraced the altruistic motto “Don’t be evil”? Stapleton identifies one major turning point: When Larry Page replaced Eric Schmidt as CEO in 2011. Her job in communications gave her a unique inside perspective on the impact of that critical transition on Google’s corporate culture. “Everyone would have said that Eric was the corporate guy and Larry Page was the pure tech guy,” said Stapleton. “Larry had incredibly lofty rhetoric about the company’s ambitions and his hopes that it would solve all the world’s problems.” What actually happened was a significant shift in Google’s organizational structure, with every executive being given their own communications team. The marketing department ballooned. “I think it created a culture that was more corporate—the opposite of what Larry said he wanted to happen,” said Stapleton. “The company became much more complex and inefficient. The irony was Larry was calling this out: ‘We’re going to become sclerotic, and we’re going to stop innovating if the company gets too bloated.’ But that’s exactly what happened: Google became a bloated Borg. And all of a sudden, Google wanted to do cool branding, like Apple.” That led to a number of failed innovations as leadership sought to lock onto the next big thing. Longtime consumers of technology are no doubt familiar with the many missteps. Google Glass? The few who embraced it were ridiculed as “Glass-holes.” Google+? Stapleton sees that as a prime example of chasing the competition rather than innovating. (I was an early adopter and found it significantly inferior to the other social media options available at the time.) The one good product that was genuinely useful from that era was Google Reader—and the company killed it to focus on Google+ and to launch Google X. Meanwhile, the storied worker-friendly corporate culture was proving to be anything but. “Radical accountability” in practice actually meant that individual workers absorbed blame for any issues, and questioning authority was dismissed as “whining.” The place where empathy was touted as “king” now viewed it as a weakness, Stapleton writes. Formally appealing to Human Resources about workplace issues resulted in gaslighting, Stapleton says, and what she calls “PR jujitsu,” professing support while never doing anything concrete to resolve the conflict. “You point out a problem, you become the problem,” Stapleton said. Stapleton cites a book by the former head of Google’s HR, Laszlo Bock, entitled Work Rules, as being particularly insightful about these dynamics. “He talks about how organizations can get out of control,” she said. “If you let managers have too much power over people, it’s corrupting, and so you have to constrain that power however you can, and that takes a strong HR function. But as my story shows, the managers had gotten control and HR had no ability to rein them in.” Stapleton also witnessed the opportunistic shift in the tech industry’s political leanings in recent years. “I think we now all have a greater understanding that everyone’s just currying favor and power, and it’s all a big club at the top and we’re not in it,” said Stapleton. “People have broadly become disillusioned. It’s quite bipartisan to be skeptical of Big Tech. The net effect on the average tech worker is that people no longer are so hornswoggled into the image that we’re the good guys, part of something that’s changing society on the whole for good. Workers being so bought in gave these companies a kind of purchase. We haven’t figured out how to push back yet. We haven’t figured out how to solve for the excesses in tech culture. But it seems like there are some interesting sprigs of green. “ Perhaps the most moving sections of the book are those that address Stapleton’s close relationship with her older brother, Scott, a vital sounding board whose skepticism about corporate double-speak gave Stapleton a much-needed outlet for her occasional doubts. His tragic, sudden death in a motorcycle accident in Turkey in 2008 shattered her world. A grief-stricken Stapleton threw herself into her work—“performing functionality to deal with grief”—and Google’s culture encouraged that single-minded avoidance. She admits that she never fully processed her grief at the time. That made it challenging to write about his death and the aftermath in Don’t Be Evil. Initial feedback from test readers on her first draft was largely positive, but several noted that the sections about her brother came off as being very arms-length and less personal in tone, unlike the rest of the book. “I was just describing what happened because at the time, I really couldn’t go there,” Stapleton said. She ended up revising those sections while staying with her parents in Santa Barbara and going through Scott’s old emails and G-chats. “I just sobbed the entire time,” she said. “Because even though it’s been 18 years, it still feels fresh. There’s still this huge hole. But in a way, it brought him back to life a little bit. The waves of grief still come back to me now and then, but I feel like now I can welcome them a bit more because I’m also grateful to have had this wonderful sibling relationship.”

Trump to give Republicans a 3-1 FCC majority, isn't filling empty Democratic seat

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-08-11

Visit Original Website
The Federal Communications Commission has been shorthanded throughout President Trump’s second term, but it’s about to get a new commissioner. Unsurprisingly, Trump is breaking a historical norm with a nomination that would give Republicans a 3-1 advantage on the FCC. Historically, the president and Senate worked together to ensure that the president’s party maintained only a one-vote majority. Normally, the president’s party has three FCC members while the minority party has two. Instead of the typical 3-2 split, the FCC has operated with a 2-1 Republican majority during Trump’s second term. On Friday, Trump nominated Republican Danielle Thumann Severs to take one of the empty spots. Thumann Severs, a lawyer, has been working as FCC Chairman Brendan Carr’s senior counsel. She must be confirmed by the Republican-controlled Senate before being sworn in as a commissioner. The FCC has played a leading role in Trump’s attacks on news broadcasters, with Carr using his position to investigate and threaten the licenses of broadcasters that don’t provide coverage favorable to Trump. A third Republican to cast votes at commission meetings will give Carr more support for his policies. Every FCC commissioner must be confirmed by the Senate after receiving a nomination from the president. Even commissioners in the minority party need a presidential nomination. If Trump followed the historical norm, he would have nominated one Republican and one Democrat to the FCC so they could be confirmed by the Senate simultaneously. Presidents typically nominate commissioners from the opposing party based on recommendations from the other party. That’s what Trump did in his first term when he nominated Democrat Geoffrey Starks based on a recommendation from Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer. Since the FCC needs only three members for a quorum, adding a third Republican raises the possibility that Trump will fire the FCC’s only Democrat and give his party a 3-0 advantage. But stocking the commission with only Republicans would violate a law that prohibits any party from having an FCC supermajority, which could put the FCC’s decisions at greater risk of being blocked in court. While the Senate’s Republican majority can confirm Thumann Severs without any Democratic support, doing so without a paired Democratic nomination will result in a contentious confirmation process. Senate Democrats want Thumann Severs to be paired with a Democratic nominee so the commission can have a full slate of five members. But it doesn’t appear that Trump will nominate a Democrat for the fifth spot. “That will be a problem. We want pairing,” Sen. Maria Cantwell (D-Wash.) told Politico on Friday. Cantwell is the top Democrat on the Senate Commerce Committee, which will consider the nomination before it goes to a full Senate vote. The committee is chaired by Sen. Ted Cruz (R-Texas.), who told Politico that “we will assess the nomination on its merits.” Sen. Ben Ray Luján (D-N.M.), the top Democrat on the telecommunications and media subcommittee, was quoted as saying that “if President Trump wants to fill these positions, he should do them in a bipartisan way. I would certainly hope my Republican colleagues on the committee want to see some semblance of balance as well. Because who’s going to be the next president?” Shortly after his second term began, Trump declared sweeping power over historically independent agencies and fired both Democrats from the Federal Trade Commission. The Supreme Court upheld the FTC firing in a decision that solidified Trump’s power over an agency that historically was not under the direct control of the president. The FTC currently has just two commissioners, both Republicans, and three vacant seats. The Supreme Court ruling on the FTC raised speculation that Trump would fire the FCC’s one Democrat, Anna Gomez, and have the FCC be run by Republicans only. But Gomez has still not been fired despite being a consistent critic of the Trump FCC’s attacks on news broadcasters and gutting of broadband consumer rules. Gomez issued a statement congratulating Thumann Severs on Friday and said, “I look forward to continuing our work together, hopefully soon with a full commission of five, the way this agency was built to operate.” Carr issued a statement congratulating Thumann Severs but did not express any wish for a five-member commission. New Street Research analyst Blair Levin, who was the FCC’s chief of staff from 1993 to 1997, did not expect Trump to fire Gomez and leave the agency with only two commissioners because that would have prevented the FCC from having a three-member quorum. Now that Trump has proposed filling one of the FCC’s empty seats, Levin told Ars on Monday that he does not think Trump will appoint a Democrat to fill the other empty seat. Levin said Trump could nominate another commissioner who is not a Democrat, yet “qualifies as a non-Republican,” though Levin does not think this is a likely scenario. Nominating a fifth commissioner who is neither a Republican nor a Democrat, but who supports the Carr and Trump agenda, could effectively give Trump a 4-1 majority without technically violating the law prohibiting an FCC supermajority. Another wrinkle is that Gomez’s term technically expired on June 30. US law allows her to serve until the end of 2027 or until the Senate confirms a replacement, whichever comes first. Gomez would need a renomination from Trump and confirmation from the Senate to continue past 2027, or she could be replaced earlier. Hypothetically, Trump could aim for a 3-0 advantage or replace Gomez with someone “who qualifies as a non-Republican” and give Republicans a 3-1 FCC majority in which the minority member is from a third party or is an independent. Levin said he thinks Senate Republicans would let Trump make that kind of appointment even though they would “object to a Democratic president doing the same” in a future administration. But he doesn’t think Trump will do this. “My guess is that the White House believes three Republicans constitute a quorum and therefore there is no need to use the political capital that would be necessary to appoint a non-Republican,” Levin said. The Communications Act requires the FCC to have three members for a quorum. The US telecom law also forbids supermajorities on the FCC by specifying that “the maximum number of commissioners who may be members of the same political party shall be a number equal to the least number of commissioners which constitutes a majority of the full membership of the Commission.” If Trump decides to follow this law, Republicans would not be able to hold all three seats on a three-member FCC. On a four-member commission, three Republicans would be the “least number of commissioners which constitutes a majority.” By adding a Republican and resisting the urge to fire Gomez, Trump can seat three Republican commissioners, while also complying with the law that prevents Republicans from taking a 4-1 or 5-0 advantage. The fact that Trump hasn’t fired Gomez yet might indicate he is willing to let the FCC operate with a 3-1 Republican majority for the rest of his presidency. This would ensure the FCC has the maximum number of Republican commissioners and the minimum of one Democrat. Levin wrote in June that the Supreme Court’s FTC ruling doesn’t necessarily give Trump the same power to make the FCC a Republican-only agency. “The law itself indicates that Congress intended the agency to always have members from both parties. That was not an accident,” Levin wrote. “When Congress created the FCC, it did not simply establish an independent agency. It explicitly designed a multi-member, multi-party commission, writing into the Communications Act that no single political party can hold more than a bare majority of seats. It explicitly limits the number of commissioners of the president’s party, relative to the number of minority commissioners, thereby implicitly requiring members of the other party.” The US law that created the Federal Trade Commission says that no more than three FTC commissioners can be members of the same political party. But the FTC doesn’t have the same legal restriction that effectively requires the FCC to have at least one Democrat and at least one Republican. From a practical perspective, leaving Gomez on the commission won’t prevent the Carr FCC from implementing Trump’s agenda. Carr has a Republican majority for decisions that require commission votes and can accomplish much of the agenda by issuing orders through the FCC’s staff-run bureaus. Firing Gomez, on the other hand, could put the Trump FCC’s agenda at risk by giving opponents of specific decisions grounds to argue in court that the commission acted illegally. A “question for a future court will be whether a one-party FCC can ever issue rulings with the force of law,” Levin wrote. “If the President were to act to create a commission without any Democratic representation, that question would likely be litigated.” Levin wrote that having a minority party “improves the quality of the policy deliberations” at the FCC. While it hasn’t prevented Carr from testing the limits of the FCC’s legal authority, “the majority understands that a minority dissenting opinion can often serve as a template for a court to overturn the majority decision,” Levin wrote. “Thus, the majority is forced to address problems in their own preferred policies or increase the risk of a court overturning their policy.” Although Trump has not fired Gomez, his refusal to nominate a second Democrat would be a significant departure from the bipartisan practice of filling all empty seats. “Traditionally, the Senate leadership of the non-presidential party has had the right to approve or veto any presidential nominee that is not from the president’s party,” Levin wrote. “While not embedded in the law, changing that tradition could cause ripples among Republican senators looking down the road to when a Democrat occupies the White House.”

New surveillance tech links your phone to your license plate

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-08-11

Visit Original Website
Imagine that you share a ride to work with the same colleague most mornings. As it passes by a license plate reader, the camera records the car, which can be linked through vehicle records to its registered owner. Beside it, another sensor detects signals broadcast by devices traveling nearby, such as your phone and your colleague’s smartwatch. After enough trips, software may treat some of those devices as a recurring electronic signature associated with the vehicle. Weeks later, one of the same device signals appears alongside a different car connected to an investigation. The signal itself may not contain its owner’s name, but its previous association with a known vehicle gives investigators another clue they can use to work out who was carrying the device. SignalTrace, a system marketed by the security company Leonardo, is designed to work alongside automatic license plate readers. The company says it can recognize groups of consumer devices that regularly move together, then associate them with license plate records and time-stamped locations. The pattern can then be searched even when a police investigator does not know the plate number. I am a researcher who studies the intersection of data governance, digital technologies, and governments, including surveillance technologies. I see that SignalTrace could further shift how police conduct investigations, putting emphasis on people’s movements and associations before their identities are known. SignalTrace is a tested and marketed capability, but not yet an established police practice. One report indicates that several of the devices are installed in Oxon Hill, Maryland, and the company’s predecessor technology appears on an official New York state contract price list. Leonardo’s new explanatory sheet states that SignalTrace “does not identify people.” It says the system “only collects electronic signatures” from signals already being broadcast, such as Bluetooth or radio frequency identification tags. Those signatures, by themselves, do not disclose a person’s identity. The company says the output must be corroborated through ordinary investigative methods. That description relies on a narrow meaning of identity, however. A sensor may not pull a legal name from a phone, but police could still work out who likely owns the device by linking its signal to other records. For example, the same signal might repeatedly appear with a car registered to one person, outside that person’s home, or alongside another device already connected to a known subject. That means a person could become part of an investigation because of where their device repeatedly appeared and who it appeared near, even if the police had no reason to suspect that person at the outset. Leonardo’s SignalTrace product page says the system stores electronic fingerprints for later queries and can recognize a vehicle without seeing its license plate. A separate product sheet says the technology helps identify suspects through the mix of devices they carry. The patent behind the system describes targets that may be people or vehicles. It also describes searchable signatures that can be correlated with visual identifiers and used to track a target across locations. SignalTrace may begin with a nameless pattern, but its value comes from recognizing that pattern again and connecting it to information that police already possess. Once an officer links a recurring signature to a license plate record or case file, the absence of a name in the original signal offers little protection. Federal privacy guidance does not limit identifying information to names. The National Institute of Standards and Technology defines personally identifiable information as data that can distinguish or trace a person’s identity, either alone or when combined with linkable information. The key question is whether data can single someone out and follow them over time. Research on mobility data—records collected from people’s mobile devices indicating where they went—helps explain the risk. A study in the journal Scientific Reports examined 15 months of records covering 1.5 million people. Four time-and-place points were enough to uniquely identify 95% of the people in the dataset. The study did not test SignalTrace, but it shows why repeated movement can make a supposedly anonymous record distinctive. The Supreme Court has recognized that phone location records can reveal far more than movement. In Carpenter v. United States, the court concluded that people have a reasonable expectation of privacy in the record of their physical movements. The court extended that reasoning in its June 2026 decision in Chatrie v. United States. Police investigating a bank robbery had used a type of warrant to obtain anonymized location records for phones near the bank, narrowed the list based on their movements, and eventually obtained the names of several users. The justices held that obtaining location data constituted a Fourth Amendment search. It also noted that even short-term monitoring can reveal political, family, and other associations. The ruling does not determine whether police collection of wireless signals detected by SignalTrace would also count as a search. The Chatrie case involved location records, while SignalTrace is designed to detect signals broadcast from nearby devices. But the two technologies raise a related question: What Fourth Amendment protections apply when police begin with unidentified devices and use their movements to determine who might be connected to an event or another person? The deeper issue is association. A recurring cluster may reflect a family routine or shared commute. It may also capture fellow protesters or passengers who happen to travel together. The system simply observes when people are near one another—it cannot know why people were close to each other. A device can be borrowed or left in a car. A roadside sensor could capture someone standing nearby. Even a correct match between a device and a vehicle does not establish who carried it on a particular day. Still, a pattern can direct police attention. Leonardo says SignalTrace is designed to develop leads. Leads influence which records officers request and whose movements receive further scrutiny. By the time an investigator attaches a name, the inferred association has already shaped the inquiry. A study in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences illustrates the power of relational inference. Researchers followed 94 participants using phones that recorded Bluetooth proximity and calling patterns. They found that patterns of behavior could accurately classify 95 percent of reciprocally reported friendships, with proximity outside work and during off-hours playing an important role in distinguishing friends from other people who regularly encountered one another. SignalTrace uses a different method, and no independent study has shown comparable performance. The research nonetheless demonstrates that repeated proximity can reveal social ties. This matters at a protest, for example. A person might come to police attention because their device repeatedly appeared near a group under investigation. The inference would arise from the company they kept, rather than an act attributed to that person. What SignalTrace shows is a broader change in surveillance practice. Investigators may no longer need to begin with a known person or vehicle. Instead, they can begin with recurring patterns of movement and proximity, then use other records to identify the people connected to them. That distinction matters because an electronic signature can become identifying without containing a name. A phone detected beside the same devices over time may reveal a relationship before the police know who owns it. SignalTrace therefore raises a question that existing rules for license plate readers does not fully answer: How should the law treat systems that identify people indirectly through patterns and associations that their devices create? But what about the next generation of doctors still in training? According to two medical professionals on the ground, the situation is looking pretty alarming. With AI being used by med students, “the danger is not just deskilling but never-skilling,” write Simar Bajaj, a journalist and med student at the Stanford University School of Medicine, and Joseph Sakran, a trauma surgeon and executive vice-chair of surgery at Johns Hopkins Medicine, in an essay for The Guardian. “Although a doctor who has forgotten how to reason is recoverable,” they added, “one who never learned how may not be.” Bajaj and Sakran point to two alarming recent findings. One is internal data showing that around two thirds of doctors in the US are using OpenEvidence, an AI chatbot specifically designed for clinicians. The technology is already prevalent and deeply woven into how medical professionals carry out their jobs. The next is a new study published in the journal Nature Medicine that evaluated the reliability of these medical-specific large language models. Shockingly, the clinical AI tools performed worse  at answering medical queries than general purpose chatbots like ChatGPT and Claude did, the work found. According to the authors, it performed about on par to Google’s AI Overviews, which are notorious for being wonky and inaccurate. Even if AI tools may be handy for research — and putting aside the major questions over their reliability — they’re no substitute for relying on your brain, as fallible as it might be, because when you’re learning, “the struggle is the point.” “For example, trainees once asked to build a list of potential diagnoses might struggle and offer an incomplete set, learning what they missed, sometimes painfully,” Bajaj and Sakran wrote. “Now, trainees can simply ask OpenEvidence and get a nearly perfect answer, complete with possibilities they might have never considered and none of the embarrassment of having overlooked them.” Where AI is different from medical advances in the past is that it’s “not just expanding what doctors can see,” they wrote, “but inserting itself into the cognitive machinery that training is meant to build.” A bevy of research on the “cognitive offloading” that AI encourages, in which users naturally begin to outsource a lot of their thinking onto the tools, backs this diagnosis. Some studies have suggested AI impairs critical thinking, and leads to lower brain activity during cognitive tasks. It’s a conundrum gripping the medical world. Earlier this year, doctors from top medical schools proposed a stricter framework on how medical students should use AI tools. “The process of deliberately crafting the note forces us to use our brains to really wrestle with what’s happening,” Jaideep Talwalkar, associate dean of educational technology and innovation at Yale School of Medicine, told Stat News. “There’s an importance in doing that with great repetition.” Bajaj and Sakran have a few suggestions for how to deal with this. One is requiring trainees to periodically work through cases without any AI help, similar to how the Federal Aviation Administration advises aviators to periodically disengage autopilot and fly by hand. Of course, another powerful deterrent could be shame. A recent John Hopkins student found that doctors who used AI are viewed with scorn and skepticism by their peers. He’s as sensitive to—and as fascinated by—its lingering effects as his creator is. And unlike some of Lee’s earlier protagonists, this character wants to know himself. Are Men to Blame for the Birth-Rate Collapse?

Society is running out of babies, and no one can agree on why. Most people around the world, including Americans, live in a country where overall fertility is below what demographers call the “replacement rate,” meaning that without migration, the population will shrink from one generation to the next. In some places, the birth rate is so low that each generation is set to be half the size of the previous one. A number of theories have been put forward to explain why this is happening. Some experts point to smartphones and social media, others to the cost of child care, still others to existential dread or overparenting. Claudia Goldin, the Nobel Prize–winning labor economist, favors another explanation: It’s the men. In a widely discussed new working paper, she argues that husbands still fail to do their fair share of housework and child care, forcing their wives to choose between procreation and career success. As a result, women are making the economically rational decision to have fewer kids. “The more that men can credibly signal they will be dependable ‘dads’ and not disappointing ‘duds,’ the higher the birth rate will be in the face of greater female agency,” Goldin writes in a companion essay. Goldin’s theory is attractive because it promises to resolve an uncomfortable tension in the fertility discussion. Historically, falling birth rates have accompanied increases in women’s education and autonomy. This was widely seen as a good thing when it meant going from seven kids on average to two or three. Now that birth rates have fallen below replacement, however, calls to reverse the decline can sound like attacks on feminism itself. But if Goldin is right, there’s no tension. Under her framework, rising equality for women helped bring birth rates down, and only more equality will get them back up. Unfortunately, the theory does not appear to be true. The data simply do not support it. For decades, men have been increasing their share of housework and child care. But the number of babies just keeps falling. When a country gets richer, its fertility tends to decline. But in the 1990s, scholars noticed that some high-income countries, such as France and Norway, had much higher fertility rates than others, such as Japan and Italy. What could explain this variation? Some hypothesized that traditions change more slowly than economies. In the Japans and Italys, they theorized, women were being told to be modern outside the home while still being asked to perform most of the labor inside it, leaving them burned-out and uninterested in having the usual two or three children. In the Frances and Norways, meanwhile, people were having more children because they were confident that things would be fairer in their households. “Women are able to see what happens to other women,” Peter McDonald, an Australian demographer who, in 2000, wrote one of the most famous papers proposing this theory, told me. Goldin’s new paper builds off the work of McDonald and others. Her theory is that countries with low fertility are experiencing a “mismatch” between women’s desire to be modern and men’s desire for them not to be. The paper proposes a simplified economic model to describe reproductive decisions. In the model, men are either “dads” who do sufficient child care and housework or “duds” who do not. If an educated woman marries a “dud” and has children, he won’t help out much, forcing her to limit her career and end up earning the same wage as if she had not gone to college. If she instead marries a “dad,” she can have kids and still realize her full economic potential. At a societal level, because these women don’t know which type of man they’ve married until after they have had a child, they’ll base their decision on whether to have children in proportion to the ratio of dads to duds in their country. The fewer the duds, the more comfortable they will be giving birth to the next generation without fear of a wage penalty. Marc Novicoff: The birth-rate crisis isn’t as bad as you’ve heard—it’s worse All economic models are simplifications, but some make more reasonable assumptions than others. Goldin’s model assumes that only college-educated women weigh whether to have children, but in some countries, including the United States, women who haven’t graduated from college are shifting toward childlessness faster than women who have. Another key assumption is that dad-or-dud status is randomly and blindly assigned. In fact, prospective couples are probably sorting more finely than ever. People are more likely to marry other people of similar education levels, the share of marriages that end in divorce is down, and the time spent living together before having children is up. We have never had more time to examine our partners before having children, and the chance of randomly drawing someone with sharply different beliefs about household gender roles is small and shrinking. An even bigger problem with the theory is the data ostensibly backing it up. As a proxy for the share of duds in a given country, Goldin uses survey data on how men and women spend their time in a set of OECD countries. In places where women report doing a lot more unpaid work (such as child care and household chores) than men, Goldin shows, the total fertility rate is lower. In places where the work is divvied up more evenly, fertility is higher. Her paper includes a graph that shows a strong correlation between the two factors. The trouble is that the relationship that the graph describes holds up only when you stick to Goldin’s somewhat idiosyncratic selection of data points. The fertility rates she uses for her y-axis, for example, are all from 2019, regardless of when the time-use survey was completed in a given country, and despite the fact that birth rates in many countries have fallen sharply since then. This creates distortions in various directions: Finland, for example, completed the survey she uses in 2010, when fertility stood at about 1.9 children per woman, but Goldin plots it at its 2019 fertility rate of 1.35. That happens to be close to Finland’s current rate, but the same isn’t true of Estonia, where fertility fell from about 1.7 in 2019 to 1.2 in 2025. Goldin restricts her sample to OECD countries with time-use surveys completed from 2009 to 2019, but leaves out two of them—Mexico and Turkey—in which women did a large share of the unpaid work but fertility was nonetheless high in 2019. She then adds Portugal and Denmark, whose time-use surveys were conducted in 1999 and 2001, and leaves out six other countries—Australia, Austria, Lithuania, Slovenia, Latvia, and Ireland—that conducted their surveys sometime from 1999 to 2009 and whose fertility rates mostly don’t fit the pattern of the ones included. In some cases, newer time-use data exist. Factoring in the latest surveys from Poland and the U.S., for example, would show a roughly 25-percent-smaller gap in unpaid work. If you include the countries that Goldin omits, and use the most recent time-use and fertility data, what you are left with is virtually no relationship at all. The slope flattens, and the amount of variation in fertility rates that is explained by the time-use differences (known in statistics as “the R-squared”) approaches 2 percent. When I spoke with Goldin about the paper, she was open to critiques about it. “All the comments that you’ve raised, I would raise about my own work,” she told me. She granted that “you can change the model in a hundred different ways.” As for the data points, she said she was trying to stay consistent with a previous version of the paper. “Sometimes, when you’re working on something, you begin with using one set of data and you build off that. And then you don’t ask, Well, maybe I should use something else.” Later, over email, she said that the graph was “not a major part of my paper or my research” and that “cross-country analysis is to be avoided.” Derek Thompson: When a promotion leads to divorce Versions of Goldin’s argument made by other academics have also been hampered by methodological issues. In a Slate article published last year, Darby Saxbe, a psychologist at the University of Southern California, argued that “dads can fix the birth rate” by doing more chores and child care. Her piece featured its own chart comparing men’s share of unpaid work to fertility rates in OECD countries. When I tried to re-create the chart, the results looked totally different. Over email, we figured out the likely culprit: The data for Saxbe’s chart, she explained, were sourced by AI, which seems to have hallucinated some of the numbers. Goldin told me that the original idea for her paper came from an influential 2008 study titled “Will the Stork Return to Europe and Japan? Understanding Fertility Within Developed Nations.” The authors of that paper showed a strong relationship between what young mothers said their male partners contributed to care and housework in a given country and that country’s fertility rate. According to the authors’ math, a 10-percentage-point increase in men’s unpaid-labor share was associated with 0.4 more births per woman—an enormous difference. So the correlation between fertility and men’s contributions at home was robust at one point. But, if you update the data, it disappears. The authors of “Will the Stork Return” used surveys and fertility rates from 2002. Using the 2022 version of the survey (the most recent available) and 2024 fertility rates (also the most recent available for most countries) produces an almost perfectly flat relationship, one in which variation in men’s share of housework explains essentially none of the variation in a country’s fertility. Birth rates still differ among rich countries, but the male share of housework apparently has nothing to do with it. “Will the Stork Return” included optimistic predictions for the future. Observing that “men in all high-income countries appear to be taking on a larger share of household duties,” the researchers forecast that “high-income countries with the lowest fertility rates are likely to see an increase in fertility in the coming decades.” That has not come true. Men’s share of housework has continued to rise, but fertility has only declined further. This set of facts applies to basically every country for which data exist. In Finland, the gap in unpaid work roughly halved from 2010 to 2021; the fertility rate went from from 1.9 to 1.5, and now sits at 1.3. In South Korea, the gap in household work shrank 42 percent from 1999 to 2024; over that time, South Korean fertility rates went from 1.4 births per woman to 0.75. “I would guess that the theory is right and there’s other things going on that have completely overwhelmed the theory,” Bruce Sacerdote, one of the economists who wrote the 2008 paper, told me. But if men’s increased participation in unpaid labor is pushing fertility up, the effect is undetectable. If men helping out more at home could reverse the birth-rate decline, that would be great news. It would ease the burden on women and simultaneously help save the world from aging populations, stagnant economies, and crumbling pension systems. Alas, the experiment has been run, and the results are in: Men are chipping in more, and the number of babies is still falling. That doesn’t mean they couldn’t do more, but it does mean that doing more is unlikely to fix the situation. And telling women that they’re surrounded by “duds” seems unlikelier still.

Anniversaries have become rote rituals in which politicians describe scenes they did not witness and national unity that no longer exists. To get beyond the benumbed abstraction, it’s necessary to remember with specificity. September 11 was a lived event. One way to recall it meaningfully, then, is through a life. Kevin Dowdell was handsome in a regular way, with dark-brown hair in an old-fashioned side-comb and a lean triangle of a face that tapered to a good chin. But he had some gravel in him, and his forearms were as thick as the fat end of a baseball bat. He was the grandson of an Irish longshoreman who’d left a dying fishing port in Fingal and arrived at Ellis Island in 1904 with $3 in his pocket. Kevin’s father, a butcher, raised seven children on leftover baloney ends in an 1,100-square-foot rowhouse in Brooklyn. At the age of 18, Kevin went to work as a “sandhog,” the nickname for miners who dug the city’s maze of tunnels that carry cars, steam, sewers, water. He spent his early manhood descending hundreds of feet into compression chambers, which sometimes caused the “staggers” or “chokes,” as sandhogs called the bends. He dynamited through rock and dug out muck up to his knees—slow, grimy work so hazardous that it was said to kill a man for every mile. But a four-hour shift paid as if you’d worked eight. When he wasn’t sandhogging he was at Rockaway Beach, where he surfed and hung out in Jacob Riis Park with a crowd from his old Brooklyn neighborhood. Among them was RoseEllen Fedele, a tall, slim 18-year-old college student. At first he was too shy to make eye contact, but he eventually got her number. They dated for five years, which was how long it took him to get out of sandhogging—and she never doubted that he would. Between shifts he studied for and took every civil-service exam in the city. In 1980, Kevin qualified to be a police officer, and he married RoseEllen, who had become a schoolteacher. He spent just a year wearing a badge before he transferred to the fire department, intrigued by a brother-in-law’s stories about how satisfying the job was. RoseEllen had Patrick on Easter Sunday 1983. When they brought the baby home and relatives crowded to hold him, Kevin refused to let go. “Nobody’s touching him,” he joked. The Dowdells moved to the Rockaways, known as the “Irish Riviera” for its enclaves of firefighters and cops, where the neighbors might swap shifts so you could spend time with your kids. Kevin found a small bungalow that wasn’t winterized and renovated it with his own two hands. They were so poor that they couldn’t afford a stove at first; instead, they cooked on a hot plate. When James came 17 months after Patrick, they put a second crib in the tiny nursery. “You had to, like, slide between them” turned sideways, RoseEllen said. Kevin often worked seven days a week. When he was off duty, he laid hardwood floors and tile to make extra money. If he got home from work and found the boys in bed, sometimes he’d wake them up just to spend a little time with them. “Come on. Let’s take a walk.” They’d stroll down Rockaway Beach to look for old glass bottles, then get an ice cream. He taught them to surf and how to handle the large waves. “Dive to the bottom and grab a fistful of sand. By that time, the wave will have passed over. Trust me.” They did. On Sunday mornings, RoseEllen would simmer red sauce on the stove while she took the boys to Mass, then jar it for the rest of the week’s dinners. One of their few indulgences was Friday dinner in Greenwich Village, usually at a favorite Italian spot at the corner of Carmine and Bleecker Streets called Trattoria Spaghetto. “Put on something nice,” Kevin would tell the boys. “Dress like your father has a job.” He would order the veal parmigiana and a glass of red wine, look around the table at his family, and say, “This is what I work all week for.” The boys spent so much time at the firehouse that they learned the meaning of the crackling radio talk. A “10‑75” was a structure fire in progress, and “K” meant the end of a transmission. The boys would yell, “Rescue 2 to Queens 10‑75 K!” They did drills with Kevin and his team. The big men would strap 10-year-old James into a harness and hoist him over the second-floor firehouse roof. When a call came in, the boys would ride on the front bench seat of the truck, between the men in their bulky turnout coats. They’d get to press the foot pedal that sounded the horn. Kevin showed them how to release it in short blasts. “You have to tap it.” He’d pat James on the knee—the signal for another horn blare. At the site of the fire, the boys would watch through the windshield as the men dismounted in their cumbrous gear and walked through smoking doorways. “The bigger the fire,” James told me, “the bigger the smiles when they came out.” The city had five rescue units, one per borough, and their members were drawn from among the most capable firefighters in the FDNY. Kevin trained for the worst emergencies: tunnel fires, structural collapses, aviation crashes, train derailments. He carried specialized tools and was a skilled black-water diver, for when cars went into rivers. As Rescue 4’s lieutenant, he organized logistics and made swift, expert judgments about risk factors. A manhole cover weighed 300 pounds but could blow six stories into the air. Hidden fuel could be anywhere. An apartment lobby might have an adjacent dry cleaner; grocery stores had stock closets with flammable ingredients. Smoke and heat moved one way in hallways and another in vertical shafts. In a single day, Kevin and his men might pluck a stranded window washer off a building, deal with a transformer fire down a manhole, and fish passengers from the Hudson after their helicopter went down. In 2000, the unit responded to 2,524 calls. But primarily, Rescue 4 went to fires. Sometimes there were half a dozen in a shift. The bigger the fire, the calmer Kevin acted. “He could pull up to the building and tell you right away where the people are gonna be trapped and which way the fire’s gonna spread,” Liam Flaherty, a colleague in Rescue 4, told me. “I think his heart rate actually went down the worse the fire got.” Often Kevin’s men could feel the fire but not see it. “When you’re in this dark environment that’s hot, it’s like being locked in a room with a tiger,” Billy Murphy, another colleague, told me. “You’re like, ‘Where is it?’ ” Flaherty would peer through his breathing mask at an acrid black veil, trying to find his way around a burning apartment. Then he would hear Kevin’s cool voice. “Hey, Li, how we making out over there?” Over the years, Kevin worked so many “good” fires that he became something of a legend in the FDNY, according to Flaherty. In 1991, Kevin helped keep flames at bay on the 12th floor of a Seventh Avenue building to aid a stunning rope rescue of two men from window ledges. In 1993, he was among the first responders to the terrorist bombing of the World Trade Center, which left a crater several stories deep and required him to comb through hazardous rubble, still glowing orange, while gas tanks exploded in the underground parking garages. In 1994, a gas leak blew up a diner in Long Island City, dropping the ground floor into the basement and pinning a waitress under slabs of concrete. Kevin and his partners crawled into the wreckage and worked for three hours to free her as piles of debris quaked over them. He took turns holding her hand and shielding her body with his own, in case the slabs caved. They got her out alive, and won medals for that job. In 1995, he was part of an elite FEMA squad deployed in the aftermath of the Oklahoma City bombing to search for survivors and recover bodies. With his record, he could have risen higher in the department. But a captaincy would have required leaving Rescue 4 for another unit in the city, and he wanted to stay. “Better to be a happy lieutenant,” he told RoseEllen. The unit had a strong sense of brotherhood; many of its members played together in the Emerald Society Pipes and Drums, an Irish group within the department that marched in civic events. Flaherty, a tousle-haired raconteur, was their drum major. Murphy, a tall Coast Guard veteran, played the bagpipes. Kevin loved the Pipes and Drums so much that he coaxed his sons to take Irish-music lessons—Patrick, the pipes; James, the snare drum—so they could march in the St. Patrick’s Day parade, alongside Kevin himself on the bass drum. “I work with the best guys in the world,” he would tell his sons. As with every FDNY unit, the heart of Rescue 4 was the firehouse kitchen, where the men ate together, and drank coffee, and debriefed after every incident. It was also the scene of geyser-like laughter, juvenile and often at someone’s expense. If Kevin corrected the pronunciation of his name (it was Dowdell ), they’d call him “Diddle.” A shift was up to 24 hours, and between alarms they had time for pranks. Once, an officer made the mistake of complaining that he didn’t like hot dogs. For the next month, all the unit served for dinner was hot dogs. Hot dogs in barbecue sauce. Hot dogs in pasta. Hot dogs, grilled and boiled. The officer grimly waited it out. At the end of the month, a large meat loaf landed in the center of the table. Then they cut him a slice. The meat loaf was stuffed with hot dogs. Rescue 4 gave Kevin a sense of home, and a feeling that he was personally protecting his beloved Rockaways. He didn’t just respond to trouble in the area—he sought it out. After a boy in their neighborhood fell into a septic tank, Kevin installed a scanner on his dashboard; he believed he could have helped if he’d heard the call. He’d listen to emergency dispatches while driving home from Little League and say, “We’re close by. Let’s just go check it out.” Kevin would pull over if he saw a car accident and extricate someone from the wreck before EMS arrived. He’d tell his sons not to be bystanders. “Don’t sit on the sidelines. Make things happen. Do things.” Once, when Kevin saw smoke coming from a building in their neighborhood, he told the boys to call the fire department and then ran off, grabbed a garden hose, and climbed through a back window to start putting the blaze out himself. He was strict and exacting with his sons. For every minute they were late to something, Kevin docked them half an hour of free time. When they were old enough, he enlisted them in construction work, and didn’t pay them until they could lift heavy loads. “You work like a man, you get paid like a man.” He once called James home from a friend’s house because he hadn’t cleaned his room. He marched James into the doorway and said, “Look at that.” There was a pair of socks on the floor. Patrick was growing into a burly achiever who aspired to West Point. James was slighter, milder, but with a distance runner’s stamina. During long car rides in New York gridlock, Kevin would ask the boys what they intended to do with their lives. Their grandfather was a butcher. Their father was a fireman. “I want you to be the next step up from that,” Kevin told them. “I want you to do something better than me.” By late summer 2001, Kevin had earned 20 citations for outstanding performance. He was beginning to think about retirement, and he dreamed of traveling—to Iceland in particular, with its volcanic landscape forged in fire. And the boys were nearly men. Patrick was 18 and had just started at Iona University, in New Rochelle, having been wait-listed at West Point. James was turning 17 and starting his senior year at Xavier High School in Manhattan. He wanted to be a firefighter. “You’re going to college,” Kevin insisted, but he took James on a scuba-training run under the Throgs Neck Bridge. James donned an FDNY dry suit and plunged into the dark, roiling East River while his father supervised from the boat. After the dive, someone took a picture of the two: Kevin’s hand is clapped on his son’s shoulder, and James, shrugging off the suit, is grinning widely. On Sunday, September 9, the family celebrated James’s birthday. Kevin had the day off and Patrick came down from Iona, and they barbecued and ate cake. Kevin and Patrick cloistered themselves in the den to work on his reapplication to West Point. Then Kevin drove Patrick back to school and kissed him on the cheek. “Love you. See you soon. I’ll talk to you next week.” Kevin reported to Rescue 4 at 6 p.m. on the evening of September 10 for a 24-hour shift. Liam Flaherty went off duty and headed to band practice at the Elks Lodge. Billy Murphy was on vacation. The kitchen was quiet. At 8:46 the next morning, American Airlines Flight 11 made a terrifying yeering sound as it accelerated into the North Tower of the World Trade Center. Rescue 4’s summons came at 8:55. Seven men hopped on the rig with Kevin: Terrence Farrell, William Mahoney, Peter Nelson, Durrell Pearsall, Peter Brennan, Allan Tarasiewicz, and a potential recruit named Michael Cawley. The ride probably took about 20 minutes. As they barreled toward Lower Manhattan, an unnerving alert came over the dispatch radio: “Be advised we have jumpers.” From a distance, Rescue 4 would have seen the smoke surging from floors 93 to 99 of the North Tower, the tallest building in New York. About seven minutes into their drive, they might have seen the blooming explosion of the second jet, United Airlines Flight 175, as it tilted into the South Tower between floors 77 and 85. Rescue 4 arrived at a scene that was too much for the senses. A torrent of debris fell from the buildings—shredded metal and insulation, chunks of concrete, shivering glass shards—amid an earsplitting clamor of sirens and screaming. Torsos, luggage, and aluminum scraps of 767s were strewn on the sidewalks. What looked like splashes of red paint on the plazas were the remains of jumpers who had pinwheeled from the upper floors of the North Tower. Kevin ran into a stunned fellow lieutenant named Robert LaRocco, from Ladder 9, who had seen a severed forearm with a perfectly manicured hand. LaRocco managed to get a call through to his wife. He told her, “It’s raining people.” Kevin reported to a command post across the street from the North Tower, where the FDNY’s chief of special operations command, Ray Downey, was doling out orders. He assigned Rescue 4 to help with evacuations in the South Tower and, if possible, to get upstairs and beat back the fire. Kevin was seen lining up his men and talking to them intently. He then led them single file toward the South Tower, hugging the sides of buildings. A rescue squad would have had its hands full in the lobby alone. Elevators were broken, stairwells smoked, and radios were unreliable. People were streaming down, badly burned or bloody, and some had to be carried. One elevator had stopped just short of the ground floor with people trapped inside. Firefighters pried open the outer doors and could see their legs, but couldn’t find a way to lower the car enough to free them. Flaming jet fuel cascaded down the shaft as the crews worked. At 9:52 a.m., about half an hour after Kevin had arrived, a battalion chief named Orio Palmer reached the South Tower’s 78th floor, where the jet’s wing had inflicted mass casualties. “We’re going to need two engines up here,” Palmer radioed down. Wherever Kevin was—trying to force open elevators, or starting the slow ascent to 78—the elements of the fire were taking over. The second plane had struck the South Tower at more than 500 miles an hour, stripping the fireproofing from steel trusses and columns. The inferno fed on carpets, curtains, chairs, desks, computers. Plumes of soot trapped the heat. The temperature hit 1,800 degrees Fahrenheit, softening the bones of the building. At 9:59 a.m., the top of the South Tower sagged. Then it began to collapse. Beams and struts slammed into one another. As each floor failed, the falling mass increased, picking up velocity. About 500,000 tons of concrete and steel sped to the earth, reaching an estimated 124 miles an hour. Lieutenant LaRocco of Ladder 9 had just put his foot on a stalled escalator on the north end of the South Tower’s mezzanine when he felt something like a wave in the floor. He heard a mounting roar, like a space shuttle taking off. The light in the windows vanished, as if someone had yanked a giant shade down. Kevin would have had little time to think about the sound above him. It took just 10 seconds for the tower to fall. As the mass neared the ground, it generated a huge blast of compressed air and seismic waves equal to those produced by a 2.1-magnitude earthquake. Paperwork from the towers drifted miles away, littering the beaches of the Rockaways. For most of that day, RoseEllen had been at P.S. 312, preoccupied with getting her third graders back to their parents. Now, at home, she waited for Kevin to call. He always did, leaving a message when she didn’t answer. “Hey, it’s me. If you see anything on the news, don’t worry about it. I’m okay.” But 6 p.m. came and went without any word. At 12:30 a.m., Special Operations Command called and told RoseEllen that Kevin was listed among the missing; all of the guys on Rescue 4’s rig were unaccounted for, as was their captain, Brian Hickey, who had been working with Rescue 3 that day. RoseEllen stayed up all night, her eyes darting between the television news and the front door. Around sunrise, RoseEllen went onto the porch and stared down the street. “At that point, I knew,” she would say, years later. “In my head, I knew.” After a week, one of the crews digging through the pulverized wreckage at Ground Zero came across a Halligan bar, which might have been used to claw at an elevator door. It was marked with KD and R4. Kevin Dowdell, Rescue 4. The tool had been blown out of the lobby. But there was no other sign of Kevin. The remaining men of Rescue 4 set up a war room in the firehouse, manned round-the-clock shifts to dig through the wreckage, and assigned a firefighter to liaise with each family who was missing someone. “Did we find him today?” RoseEllen would ask. No, Billy Murphy would say, day after day. Whenever the door to the Dowdell house opened, three faces would swivel toward it with hope. “We were like, ‘If anyone got out of there or could get out of there, it’s him,’ ” Patrick recalled, but it was always a relative or neighbor. Two weeks after the attacks, RoseEllen decided that sitting around the house in their grief was not doing them any good. “Listen,” she said to the boys. “You’re going back to school, and I’m going back to work.” On the drive to Iona, she persuaded Patrick to finish his reapplication to West Point. “You will not—we will not—use anything as an excuse in your life,” she said. Xavier High School was steeped in mourning; 60 alumni and close relatives of students and staff were lost. Counselors and priests watched James for signs of depression, but when RoseEllen pushed him to see a therapist, he protested. “All I do is talk to people,” he told her. He would channel his emotions into running, sometimes 12 miles at a time. Back at Iona, Patrick couldn’t concentrate. He called James and suggested that they go to Ground Zero to search for their father’s remains. “If we find him, I want to be there,” Patrick told James. “I want to see him. I want to help carry him out.” James heard his father’s voice in his head. Don’t sit on the sidelines. Make things happen. Do things. “I’ll meet you there,” James told his brother. Rescue 4 held a meeting about whether to allow them to join the search. They took a vote, and the boys won. Murphy and Flaherty geared them up with fire boots, gloves, Carhartt suits, helmets, and tools. The guys worried about giving the boys nightmares. Murphy warned them: “If we come across somebody, I’ll ask you to step away until we find out who it is.” The pile, as it was called, was eight stories of contorted metal, sticking up like giant fork tines, spread over 16 smoldering acres. The burning electrical smell was sickening. As Patrick and James clambered through it, they could feel heat in their boot soles. Grapplers lifted steel from the smoking heaps, and the diggers showed the boys what to look for underneath: a boot, a piece of helmet, the reflective tape from a turnout coat. But the main sign of human remains was the odor of decay. As soon as the diggers smelled it, they would wave at the machines to stop. The first time the boys were present for a recovery, the crew cast sidelong looks at James and Patrick. The men and boys removed their helmets. A chaplain was called. They stood at attention as the remains were carried away on a flag-draped stretcher. For seven months, James and Patrick worked at Ground Zero as much as they could. “Being away from it became harder than being there,” Patrick recalled. When his classes at Iona were done for the day, he’d find a ride to the city and dig half the night, then return to campus at 3 or 4 a.m. slit-eyed from ash and lack of sleep, stowing his dusty gear in a garbage bag in his dorm. James kept his Carhartts and tools in his Xavier locker. He would shed his school uniform—blue oxford shirt, necktie, insignia-patched sweater—and tromp out the schoolhouse door, transformed, in coveralls and boots. The crew found parts of helmets, and parts of people. They found impaled bodies that had to be cut away from the steel with torches. They found intact bodies in strange voids; how long had some people survived under the wreckage? Sometimes the boys just put bone fragments in buckets. At the firehouse, Patrick and James sat around the kitchen table debriefing like members of the company. They listened to the men of Rescue 4 wrestle with survivor’s guilt. If they had been on duty, they’d have been in the South Tower at 9:59 a.m. Survival was a matter of lefts and rights, as Flaherty said. They posed terrible “What if?” questions. Should the fire commanders have foreseen the towers’ collapse? If so, should they have kept men from going up? “Well, what was the alternative?” Flaherty argued at the table. “There’s people trapped upstairs. We have to go get them.” Could you imagine, he said, if the FDNY had just given up on the towers? “That would have been the worst day in the history of the fire department the other way.” The kitchen conversations were a kind of counseling for the boys. “We had our people, and we just talked amongst ourselves, and that was enough,” Patrick remembered. So were the funerals for firefighters they played at with the Emerald Society Pipes and Drums. They stood by graves in kilts and red tunics that Flaherty had given them, and performed “Amazing Grace” and “Going Home.” Many children of the dead were preschoolers or grade-schoolers. RoseEllen saw that the firefighters were helping to steady her sons. “Is school good?” Flaherty and others would ask the boys while they dug through the pile. “If your grades aren’t good, you can’t be here.” As a picture of Kevin’s movements with Rescue 4 emerged from survivor accounts, the recovery concentrated on a spot near the South Tower elevator shafts. In late October, on a day the boys were not working, a crew found the remains of four men from Rescue 4. But not Kevin’s. A few feet away was a large, burned-out space where fires had raged below the tonnage for weeks. “You recover two guys that were kind of close by to each other in the rubble, and then you don’t get anybody else,” Patrick said. “It’s really hard. You don’t know how that happens, but that’s how it happens.” On the morning of November 12, 2001, when an American Airlines plane accidentally crashed into the Rockaways shortly after takeoff, RoseEllen could see the smoke from the porch. “You are not going over there,” she told James. When Rescue 4 pulled up to the crash site, James was already there in his gear, hosing down a burning house. “You’re one of us now,” a firefighter told him. James, though just 17, took the FDNY entrance exam and passed. By spring, the pile had become a pit, with almost 1.8 million tons of debris removed. The site was practically broom-clean. The recovery process was down to DNA testing of the smallest fragments. Even then, no Kevin. “There’s nothing there,” Murphy told RoseEllen. “It’s over.” They held Kevin’s memorial service on April 20, 2002. His casket was filled with dust from the spot where they believed he had died, and mementos of him: his extra turnout gear, a pair of fire boots, a copy of the Irish Voice newspaper, travel brochures for Iceland. Letters from his sons and siblings. Hundreds of people attended, including the mayor. Patrick played a bagpipe solo of “Amazing Grace” as the casket was carried into the church. “He was a teacher, mentor, tradesman, listener, and my best friend,” Patrick said during his eulogy, concluding with an Irish blessing that Kevin had recited to him: “May the best days of your past be the worst of your future.” The memorial service marked an abrupt end to months of mission. “It was so consuming from September to April that after it was all said and done, there was a sense of ‘Now what do we do?’ ” James recalled. Kevin was irrecoverable. But they had his voice. Don’t sit on the sidelines. Patrick had just been accepted to West Point, and in June he entered “Beast Barracks,” the academy’s rigorous six-week cadet-induction program. It made the news: 9/11 firefighter’s kid joins the War on Terror. Overnight, he went from friends consoling him to officers barking orders. He marched to breakfast, marched to lunch, and spent nights shining his shoes. What if he couldn’t cut it? He feared letting his father down. He refused to buy his mother a West Point sweatshirt. “I didn’t want it to be a constant reminder of my failure if I didn’t make it through,” Patrick said. The combination of mourning, loneliness, and self-doubt made him mutinous. He racked up punishment tours for lateness and absences, and was made to march in circles carrying a rifle for five hours at a time. He would sneak out to bars to meet old friends from the Rockaways. He felt like he was trying to break free from prison. He considered quitting, to escape the pressure. RoseEllen felt powerless and wished for her husband. Patrick had relied on Kevin. “I didn’t know anything about the Army,” she said. “I couldn’t help him.” Flaherty stepped in. “Bro, failure is not an option,” he told Patrick. “You cannot—you cannot—drop out.” He warned him that quitting would alter the trajectory of his life. Patrick rallied, though not before he missed a 9/11 anniversary ceremony because he was walking another punishment tour. This time, it was about a girl: He’d met Katie Neudeck at a party in Rockaway Beach at the end of summer break. He was supposed to report back to West Point that day, and his lateness cost him 20 hours of walking. She was motivation to graduate; so were the pictures of his father taped to his dorm-room wall, and a steel bracelet on his wrist etched with LT. KEVIN DOWDELL 9-11-01. Instead he enrolled at Iona. RoseEllen was alone in the house, but had two sons she still needed to get to the finish line of manhood. Patrick and James were strapping—bigger than their father, all bone and muscle and hard-charging. They had chosen dangerous futures, and there was little she could do about it. “That’s what they knew,” she said. In 2006, when James turned 21, he dropped out of his final semester at Iona to enter the FDNY academy; he then joined Ladder 174, in Flatbush. (He finished college the following year.) That same spring, Patrick graduated from West Point and prepared for deployment to Iraq. “I want to kill terrorists,” he declared to the Associated Press. By 2008, he was a platoon leader on combat patrols in Baghdad. With Patrick skirting roadside bombs and James running into fires, RoseEllen could only pray. Her faith told her that Kevin was still on watch. “I just said, in my head, He’s protecting them,” she recalled. This is what Kevin Dowdell worked all week for: Patrick would make captain in the Army, and James would take his old man’s badge number and carry his Halligan tool. And RoseEllen— who never allowed pain, hers or theirs, to be an excuse—would see them through. While Patrick was serving abroad, James was earning a medal. At 4:30 one morning in March 2010, Ladder 174 responded to a heavy fire in a two-story building. James and another firefighter battled their way to the second floor with a fire extinguisher and, working in high heat and zero visibility, forced open the door of a rear apartment. In the smoke, James found an unconscious nine-month-old on a bed. Their team got four people out of the building alive, including the infant. James joined Rescue 2. He would be working with some of Kevin’s former colleagues, and would be expected not to drag the anchor chain, as they said in the department about slackers. In 2017, while working under Flaherty, James set the unofficial FDNY record for a rope rescue when he descended 200 feet down an unlit Con Edison shaft to help workers trapped in an underground elevator. “I was just the dope on the rope,” he said, crediting his partners who’d made the rig. Early last year, he was promoted to lieutenant—his father’s rank. He’s now studying for the captain’s exam. After Patrick left the Army as an assistant operations officer for an artillery battalion, he thought about joining the FDNY. He scored first on the entrance test, out of about 45,000 applicants, but again heard his father’s voice: I want you to do something better than me. Patrick didn’t think there was any vocation greater than firefighter, but he opted for a career in business. He works in cybersecurity in Manhattan, and his home is on the bayside of Rockaway Beach, where he can look directly at One World Trade Center and the twin light towers that shine every September 11. Each year on the anniversary, RoseEllen and her sons return to Rescue 4’s firehouse for a memorial ceremony. Firefighters gather in their dress uniforms. They bow their heads for a moment of silence in front of a plaque bearing the names of the dead. Invariably a pedestrian will pause in the firehouse driveway and say, casually, “What’s going on? Is there a parade or something?” James or Patrick will raise his head and say, “It’s September 11.” After the memorials, the Dowdells return to the Rockaways for a family dinner. They leave the seat at the head of the table open, with a glass of red wine in front of it. At the end of their meal, they pass around the glass, and each person tells a story about Kevin. Among those seated at the family table is a retired firefighter named Tom O’Day, whom RoseEllen began seeing in 2010 (“He understood everything,” she said). When Hurricane Sandy hit the Rockaways, RoseEllen evacuated but Tom stayed to save her possessions; he carried every memento of Kevin he could find above the flood line. In 2019, Tom and RoseEllen went to Iceland, where she buried a Mass card from Kevin’s memorial service on a remote beach, placed a rock over it, and noted the coordinates. Five grandchildren, ages 5 through 13, are also at the family table, as are Patrick’s and James’s wives, Katie and Chrissy. They all live within walking distance of RoseEllen and the old home, where an oil portrait of Kevin hangs in the front hall. The grandchildren have been going to firehouses since they were born. As they run around, climbing and sliding down the fire pole, they talk about Grandpa Kevin as if they knew him. Recently the youngest grandchild, Carly, sat with RoseEllen, looking across Jamaica Bay. “I see Grandpa Kevin’s building,” she said. “What happened to him?” RoseEllen explained again about the bad guys who flew planes into the buildings and made them fall. In the aftermath, firehouses cared for the families of the fallen with something beyond duty. Beyond duty: two grieving boys hunting for their lost father and instead recovering other people’s fathers, and playing hymns for them. The FDNY lost 343 men on September 11; 65 of their sons and daughters now serve in the department. Twenty-five years later, Kevin is still missing. James’s promotion to lieutenant fell on what would have been his 70th birthday. “Never Forget” is no slogan to the Dowdells. “We live it, and we live with it,” Patrick said. Living without Kevin is a form of bravery, of course—a fire all its own. Living his example is how they find their way out of the smoke. Conservatives may not like that, but “Make marriage compulsory, unhappy, and about economic survival instead of love again” is not exactly a popular position. Many couples may wish they had met earlier in life, and some people marry their high school sweethearts, but I don’t know all that many single adults who wish they had married the person they dated at 17. I do, however, know a great many people who spent their 20s building meaningful careers, forging important friendships, dating enough to learn what did and did not work for them romantically, and generally enjoying themselves—and becoming the kinds of people who might feel ready for a lifelong financial and romantic commitment, which is no small thing. A great many women make use of today’s incredible reproductive technologies not because they’re stereotypical Career Women casting men aside in pursuit of the corner office, but because they want pretty simple things that haven’t materialized yet: a loving relationship with someone who would also make a good husband and father; a life that feels stable enough to bring children into it. No woman can snap her fingers and make the right man and a stable life materialize (and many women, at many times in our lives, don’t want the right man to materialize). But if she has the resources, she can freeze her eggs and perhaps buy herself a little time. AOC is one of the most prominent politicians in the United States. She has been in Congress for the past seven years, and has made a name for herself as one of its most diligent, intelligent, and effective members. She has helped draw an entire generation of progressives into politics, and totally changed the game for young people, young women, and democratic socialists. If she wants a baby, I hope she has one. But her life has also been pretty meaningful as is—and she’s been able to accomplish what she has precisely because she’s been able to make her own choices about whether to have children. Her egg-freezing decision is a part of that continuum. The critics are right that egg freezing doesn’t guarantee you a baby. But I suspect that women know as much. It’s also true that the egg-freezing industry has an incentive to overpromise and oversell. But there is also just simply much we don’t know about a technology that can have a 10- or 20-year horizon—that is, if a woman freezes her eggs at 20, researchers need to wait a few decades before they can answer the question of whether she’ll ever thaw them, and what will happen next. The data on egg freezing remains mixed. One meta-analysis found that of more than 13,500 patients who went through egg-freezing cycles, only about 10 percent returned to use those frozen eggs. The rest ostensibly had children some other way, or chose not to have children at all. Of the 10 percent who retrieved their frozen eggs, 34 percent became pregnant, and 29 percent had a live birth. That means that most women who froze their eggs did not wind up with a baby from those eggs (which, to be clear, is not the same as them not winding up with a baby at all). This information is useful for women to have, and the fertility industry should be required to be transparent with patients. But it’s also not the same as saying that egg freezing is pointless or doesn’t work, which seems to be the narrative adopted by many on the political right. The analysis also found that “the most widely reported reason for fertility preservation is single relationship status or the lack of a partner, particularly one who is willing or committed to having children”—which suggests that many women who froze their eggs eventually did find a willing and committed partner and went on to have children naturally; a small minority went on to have children using their thawed eggs either with a partner or on their own; and others likely did not find a willing and committed partner and decided not to have children at all. Any of these outcomes are possible for AOC and any other woman who freezes her eggs. I would imagine she knows that no single outcome is guaranteed and there is no way to live a life totally insulated from regret or misstep. But in being the person in charge of this decision, she is at least making the best decision for her given the information she has and the desires that she feels, even if she can’t guarantee where it will lead. This is, in essence, what feminists have long wanted for women: not perfect or frictionless lives; not the false promises of fairy tales; just the very real adult work of having to consider what you want and make choices given what you have. Last week, President Donald Trump continued his defiant stance against the Supreme Court’s birthright citizenship decision from earlier this summer, firing off two new executive orders about which people born in the U.S. should be declared citizens. Despite losing at the Supreme Court on the legal issue in late June, the Trump administration remains committed to twisting a long-settled constitutional question into a tool to keep Black and brown people out of American life. The conservative legal movement, at first skeptical of Trump’s original executive order attacking birthright citizenship, has now fully embraced the cause. And the cause’s survival can all be traced back to our Supreme Court’s unwillingness to give the Trump administration the overwhelming legal defeats it deserves. In Trump v. Barbara, the court struck down Trump’s first executive order on birthright citizenship. Signed on his first day in office, “Protecting the Meaning and Value of American Citizenship” purported to strip the right to be a U.S. citizen from children born to parents who are in the country unlawfully, as well as parents lawfully but only temporarily in the country. This was in spite of the fact that the 14th Amendment grants birthright citizenship to “all persons” who are born in the United States and subject to its laws. All persons. The only exceptions to this rule that have ever been consistently recognized are for people technically born within the country’s borders but under the legal authority of a separate sovereign, such as children of foreign diplomats. It’s this exception that Trump’s first executive order tries to capitalize on. Trump barely took the effort to even change the name of the order the court had struck down, calling the new one “Continuing to Protect the Meaning and Value of American Citizenship.” This new order seeks to expand the number of people who fall under the foreign diplomat exception. People who would no longer get citizenship would include children whose parents have been named as part of a designated terrorist organization, are foreign government officials, work for foreign-based nonprofits, or are involved with certain international business transactions. The order stretches the birthright citizenship exception as it’s been traditionally recognized beyond recognition. As the American Civil Liberties Union explained in its aftermath last week, “Birthright citizenship is guaranteed by the Constitution. No additional executive order can change the meaning of the Constitution.” Trump’s second executive order focuses on a related citizenship issue: “Ending Birth Tourism.” A longtime pet project of the president, birth tourism is the practice in which companies help foreigners enter the United States, usually under visa applications, for the express purpose of having children in the U.S. who would then be citizens. There are no statistics establishing birth tourism as a wide-ranging problem. At oral argument, Solicitor General John Sauer could only provide anecdotes about newspaper clippings from China, but admitted that “no one knows” the true number. Unbothered by his solicitor general’s concession, Trump continues to repeat with no basis that hundreds of thousands of birth tourists have given birth in this country. Never mind that tourists account for less than 2 percent of all United States births—and that 2 percent figure is all tourism, even before you make potentially ill-founded judgments about whether someone committed fraud to visit this country. The birth-tourism executive order, if it holds up in court, seems likely to lead to frequent ill-founded judgments. First of all, committing fraud in visa applications is already illegal. Border agents already have the authority to question a foreigner’s reason for applying for a visa or entering the country. And in case there’s any question about whether this order might be applied evenhandedly across foreign countries, remember that this is the same administration that is currently taking almost exclusively white South Africans as refugees. And Section 5 of the order allows the secretary of state and Homeland Security to grant any exemptions they want to parents who might otherwise fall within the order’s orbit. All in all, then, the order takes a building of preexisting visa laws and simply paints it over with a coat of whiteness. To understand how these new executive orders push the boundaries of both U.S. and international law, it’s worth taking a moment to appreciate just how deeply ingrained birthright citizenship is in our country’s history. Chief Justice John Roberts’ opinion for the court explained how the long-held understanding of birthright citizenship dates back to the English crown. The idea that nearly everyone born on the land became a citizen was so pervasive as to have a name: jus soli, or “the right of the soil.” Legal scholars in the early 1800s treated this right as both intact and broad. In the words of Supreme Court Justice Joseph Story: When it comes to birthright citizenship, “nothing is better settled.” But in the infamous case of Dred Scott v. Sandford, the Supreme Court did an about-face and declared that formerly enslaved Black Americans were not citizens, even though they were born here. That case wouldn’t just go down as one of the most shameful moments in the court’s history; it was also the legal system’s most significant contribution in devolving the country into the Civil War. Dred Scott weighed heavily on the mind of the reunited country that passed both the 14th Amendment and the Civil Rights Act of 1866, both of which recognized citizenship as a birthright for virtually all persons born in this country. And a later case about a child of Chinese parents, United States v. Wong Kim Ark, confirmed that the 14th Amendment’s citizenship clause applied to people of all races. It’s not unusual for President Trump to try to ignore or twist a legal ruling on an issue he lost on. The better question is why he thinks the effort will succeed. After all, there are other areas where Trump has quietly retreated after a court loss or public outcry. The reason the president persists is that despite all this history, text, and precedent, only five of the nine justices read the 14th Amendment as protecting birthright citizenship in Barbara. The narrowness of that margin was and continues to be a scandal. Justice Brett Kavanaugh sided with Justices Neil Gorsuch, Clarence Thomas, and Samuel Alito in saying they would rule that the constitution does not protect birthright citizenship for children of immigrants who don’t have permanent legal status or are temporary visitors. (Kavanaugh, in a separate opinion joined by nobody, thought the executive order only contradicted a federal statute that Congress could change.) Let’s be clear: This court was one vote away from doing Dred Scott for yet a new group of people who lack political power. All in defense of an order that has been rightly condemned as “blatantly unconstitutional.” Barbara, and now these new orders, is also a symptom of a court that operates in a lopsided manner that favors the administration. This is especially true in the immigration context. In the temporary protected status case, for example, the 6–3 court pulled the rug out from under the Immigration Act of 1990 and the constitutional guarantee of equal protection, letting the Trump administration kick out hundreds of thousands of people from Haiti and other countries. When this court upholds a Trump executive order, it is with the brightest green-means-go signal a majority can muster. But when they rule against the administration, like with Barbara, there isn’t equal force on the other side. Roberts’ opinion for the court, despite being well-written and legally correct, resists critiquing the original order with the forcefulness this issue deserves. And the dissents are littered with both reasons why they believed the Trump administration was right in this case and pathways to future actions that would also be valid in their view. Most obviously, there was Kavanaugh’s road map for a congressional statute that he believed could somehow expand the number of constitutional exceptions to birthright citizenship. Collectively, the lesson that the court gives the administration is clear—just try again. This lopsided approach allows the court to reap the benefits of positive headlines when they rule against the administration, all the while paving a stepping stone for further abuses. This pattern could be seen clearly this term when the court’s conservatives overturned the last remaining plank of the Voting Rights Act that two of them had joined the liberals to uphold just three years ago. Even beyond last week’s orders, conservatives are already meeting to develop a plan to overturn the Barbara ruling altogether. By seeking to overturn Barbara, the anti-birthright legal movement hopes to create a different lopsided relationship between Black and brown children and their government. As the court in Barbara explained, birthright citizenship launches a reciprocal relationship between a person and the sovereign. At birth, the person implicitly agrees to be subject to the laws of the government. In exchange, the government owes that person treatment equal to that of others born on its soil. The Trump administration wants to rupture one half of that relationship. They would like children of immigrants to suffer under fear of violence, imprisonment, and deportation. But they want to relieve themselves of their own obligation to recognize the right of babies who have quite literally only known America from the second they were born. As a businessman who’s spent his life making deals that would screw over the other side, perhaps it’s not surprising that Trump would want to renege on America’s side of the bargain with children of immigrants. What is disheartening, though, is to have a high court that seems on the brink of letting him break that bargain. There have been two eras in our nation’s history in which a coalition of Americans tried to deny birthright citizenship to minorities. In the first era, the coalition temporarily succeeded against Black Americans, leading to a Dred Scott decision that everyone now agrees was an immoral mistake. The second era was against Asian Americans, and that effort failed with a Wong Kim Ark decision that went from settled law to contested precedent in the span of a couple of years. We are now in the third era. Let’s hope that the Barbara decision was the final destination, and not a temporary stop on the way to stripping the right that allows all other rights to flourish. Every few decades, someone announces that science has reached its end. In 1903, the revered physicist Albert Michelson wrote that the “facts of physical science have all been discovered.” In the 1980s, Stephen Hawking predicted that theoretical physics might be finished by the end of the century. With the explosive arrival of artificial intelligence, the feeling is in the air again—this time accompanied by a Nobel Prize. In 2024, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper of Google DeepMind were awarded part of the Nobel in chemistry for their neural network AlphaFold, which predicts the three-dimensional structures of proteins by learning from thousands of experimentally measured shapes. This devilish problem had resisted systematic attacks for half a century; AlphaFold seemed to have solved it once and for all, and the world became fixated on the promise of its approach. Hassabis and his team called AlphaFold “the template for how AI can accelerate all of science to digital speed.” A wave of startups building foundation models for biology, chemistry, and materials discovery raised billions of dollars, buoyed by DeepMind’s success. AlphaFold had shown that the combination of AI and sufficient data could make groundbreaking discoveries (even if we did not understand the underlying mechanisms involved), and it seemed, once again, that a path through the rest of science was laid out before us. To be sure, AI will bring extraordinary changes to science, but it has become increasingly clear that AlphaFold, and things like it, may not be the best template for that metamorphosis. Though it is a profound achievement, the conditions that produced the likes of AlphaFold are rare, and the time it will take to meet those conditions in other fields will be measured in decades, not years. Instead, the acceleration of science will come about thanks to another approach: AI agents. The primary condition for AlphaFold’s success was the existence of the Protein Data Bank, a data set of roughly 170,000 experimentally validated protein structures on which DeepMind’s team could train its model. The creation of the Protein Data Bank was not simple: It took 53 years of international scientific cooperation and, by a recent estimate, roughly $21 billion worth of experimental work to assemble. Efforts of that scale are infamously difficult to fund, next to impossible to coordinate, and hugely time-consuming to execute; they have often been unsuccessful as a result. But even in fields with the requisite cohesion and resources, and where the relevant data are not rendered inaccessible by commercial ownership, another barrier is too little discussed: the scientific impossibility of generating comparable data. In the case of protein structures, the key experimental technique—protein crystallography—is an unusually replicable and dependable tool, so much so that over 25 Nobel Prizes have relied on it. But in most of experimental science, results vary more often than not. Cell lines drift. Chemicals have trace contaminants. Lab humidity changes. The creation of measured datasets that will be consistent enough, accurate enough, precise enough, and scalable enough to train a modern neural network in biology or most of chemistry would require new kinds of measurement and new standardized approaches—none of which will be ready anytime soon. Of course, there are a handful of fields where these requirements are met: weather forecasting, much of genomics, very limited areas of chemistry. These may see AlphaFold-style breakthroughs soon, if they haven’t already. Government support for the production and coordination of those datasets will be critical, as the US National Security Commission on Emerging Biotechnology has argued. But for most open questions in science, we will need a different plan, at least in the short term. Luckily, something quieter and more modest has begun to show promise. Scientists have always reasoned under uncertainty. Biologists working to identify new drug targets have never had perfect datasets. Instead, they combine docking calculations and known structures, factor in molecular dynamics, run a handful of binding assays, and use their judgment to weigh each method according to its particular strengths and points of failure. The skill of science is not in any single tool; it is synthesizing what many tools produce, and revising the results as the evidence comes in. This is how most working research actually proceeds. But until very recently, no software could do it. Agents now can. Simply put, an agent is an AI reasoning engine that has been given access to tools—digital or physical—and the capabilities to use them. Over the last few years, a fundamental architectural shift in AI has enabled the rapid proliferation of these programs, which are powered by large language models, dramatically reducing the need for scientifically specialized datasets. For science, this technological advancement represents a foundational change: it has allowed us to create digital tools that can mimic the iterative, highly contingent process of actual research. While tools like AlphaFold apply a powerful approach to a limited question, agents are inherently generalists. They do not represent a new way to do science—instead, they digitally model the human process of discovery. Consider Google’s AI Co-Scientist, announced in May. Researchers gave it a one-page brief and a goal: Figure out how antibiotic resistance spreads between bacterial species, a key driver of drug-resistant infections. The system spun up sub-agents. One drafted hypotheses from the literature. Another picked them apart like a peer reviewer. A third ran tournaments to rank the strongest candidates. A fourth refined the winning hypothesis. The agent concluded that resistance genes were hitching rides on bacterial viruses, borrowing whichever virus could ferry them into a new host. The hypothesis was correct. Researchers at Imperial College London had spent a decade reaching the same conclusion through painstaking wet-lab work; their paper, previously unseen by Co-Scientist, was still in peer review. Agents like Co-Scientist are still novel tools, and there are real challenges to overcome before they become a ubiquitous part of the scientific process: They are still liable to hallucinate, their judgment is not consistent, and they have memory and input constraints that limit the time they can run autonomously. But these technical barriers will fall away, and as they do we will begin to notice the compounding effects of scientific agents on the reliability, consistency, and velocity with which science is done. Perhaps most notably, agents offer a structural fix for science’s “reproducibility crisis,” the widespread problem of researchers’ inability to replicate each other’s results. For decades, the scientific community has begged researchers to share their raw data and exact code in an effort to standardize experimental processes. But researchers have long resisted this tedious administrative work, which happens after the interesting science is already done. Agents, in contrast, automatically log every move they make, creating an exact record of the method that led to their results and allowing for precise replication. A second consequence will be an amplification of scientific memory. The transfer of knowledge between researchers is a famously murky process; if it isn’t done over years of training and observation, graduate students are left to pore through the messy lab notebooks kept by decades of predecessors, looking for the details that will make or break their protocol. As agents become an increasingly large part of the scientific process, though, a lab’s entire scientific history will be recorded in a central, standardized repository of institutional knowledge. But the most important impact of agents will be speed. In any field, when testing an idea takes less time than arguing about it in a meeting, people stop debating and just run the test. An agent that can read a thousand papers in an hour, design 500 molecules, and learn from its failed tests by morning will bring down the cost of experimentation and fundamentally change the pace at which science gets done. It will also give researchers the freedom to chase bold, strange questions they never would have risked their time on before, opening scientific doors we have yet to imagine. While the AlphaFold template will certainly be key to incredible discoveries, it alone will not bring us to the end of science. Instead, the shift toward agentic AI represents a much rarer tier of breakthrough: a tool that envelops every field of science at once. Historically, tools of such scope have arrived just a handful of times: calculus, statistical inference, spectroscopy, the computer. Each revealed a world of problems no one had thought to formulate, and those problems, in turn, defined their fields anew. With agents, another such transformation is upon us. Nine years on, transformers are the engines inside every major large language model on the market. “The entire AI industry is built on transformers,” says Justin Dangel, cofounder and CEO of the AI startup Subquadratic. “They are one of the most important innovations in the history of computer science, and they’ve changed the world.” But transformers are starting to show their age. Many of the recent advances in LLMs, such as the development of so-called reasoning models and their ability to handle large amounts of input at once, are not neat extensions of that core technology but workarounds that patch over some of its fundamental flaws. A growing number of scientists and engineers are now asking what’s coming next. LLMs are not going anywhere, but the way they get built is up for grabs. (MIT Technology Review dubbed this future generation of models LLMs+ in this year’s list of the 10 things that matter in AI.) Enter a wave of startups hoping to push the boundaries of this boomtown technology. Some will no doubt fail—but they have everything to play for and far less to lose than the companies at the front of the pack today. But first, the problem. The key strength of transformers lies in a mechanism called dense attention, which encodes the meaning of a block of text in a series of numbers. The process involves comparing every word (or part of a word, known as a token) in that text with every other word via a form of multiplication. Dense attention can capture the meaning of text with remarkable accuracy. But as the length of that text grows, the number of computations needed to process it adds up fast. A document 10,000 words long might require a transformer to perform 50 million multiplications. That’s the main reason LLMs suck up so much power. The costs are huge. OpenAI is set to spend $50 billion on computing this year, according to the company’s president, Greg Brockman. And the International Energy Agency predicts that the total amount of electricity consumed by data centers will double by 2030. What’s more, transformers struggle with what many of the latest models are designed to do. Because of the way they process text word by word, transformers are not great at keeping track of a lot of information at once (in other words, what's known as their context window cannot get too large). And yet if LLMs are to carry out harder tasks, they will need to take in larger amounts of data: a whole library of documents, an entire code base, or in the case of agents, output from other LLMs. As for reasoning models, they work by writing notes to themselves (in a kind of scratch pad known as a chain of thought) and then reading them back, which again adds to the amount of data to stay on top of. As LLMs get bigger and better, transformers have become a bottleneck. The technology’s key strength is now a limitation. Here are four new ideas for how to solve the transformer problem—innovations that could change LLMs for good, making them faster, far more efficient, and (maybe) even smarter. An obvious way to make LLMs faster and cheaper is to tackle the problem head on and change the way attention works. Swapping out dense attention for a mechanism called sparse attention, which runs calculations on only some pairings of words in a block of text instead of all of them, can radically reduce the amount of computation LLMs need to do. Researchers have come up with plenty of sparse attention mechanisms over the years. The problem is that none of them were as good as dense attention at capturing meaning. That might have changed. Subquadratic, a startup based in Miami, claims it has invented the first sparse attention mechanism that rivals top mainstream LLMs on a handful of tasks, including search and coding. It’s a huge claim (and some people in the industry remain skeptical). Subquadratic says its model, SubQ, works by figuring out on the fly—for each piece of text it is given—which words matter and which don’t. The company also claims that thousands have signed up to its waitlist and plans to make the model widely available soon. Meanwhile, Manifest AI, a startup based in San Francisco, is coming at the problem from a different angle. Instead of changing how attention works, it is replacing it with something else. It has developed a mechanism it calls power retention, which stores only the most relevant information for a given task and ensures that the amount of data an LLM has to keep track of doesn’t blow up. Attention mechanisms force LLMs to keep track of everything in their context window. A sparse attention model (such as SubQ) throws out a lot of the individual words, but it still retains a rough picture of everything it has seen. In contrast, power retention works by providing the model with a rolling summary of its context window. As new information is added, less relevant information is dropped. The basic principle of retention has been around for a decade. Manifest AI claims it has updated those techniques to build models that can stand up to transformer-based LLMs for the first time. The company says it is possible to adapt a transformer model into a power retention model with minimal retraining. To demonstrate this, it has turned an existing open-source coding LLM called StarCoder into a version that uses power retention, called PowerCoder. It has also released a model called Brumby, which it claims rivals some versions of Alibaba’s popular open-source model Qwen. Manifest AI wants its power retention tech to become the go-to solution when LLMs need to carry out tasks that involve processing huge amounts of data. There are many useful applications, Manifest AI’s cofounder and CTO, Carles Gelada, claimed in a video announcing his company’s technology last year—from analyzing videos that are hours long to building agents that can stay on task for weeks at a time. Liquid AI, an MIT spinout based in Cambridge, Massachusetts, hasn’t changed or ditched transformers fully but pairs them with its own tech, liquid neural networks, to build what cofounder and CEO Ramin Hasani calls LFMs (liquid foundation models). Liquid AI’s models are far smaller and use less energy than most LLMs. The firm builds models for car makers, including Mercedes, which run on the small chips inside vehicles. Its latest models can run on a Raspberry Pi, a low-powered hobbyist computer that costs $50. Its models are available for free to any organization with an annual revenue less than $10 million. And they have proved popular: The company has racked up almost 34 million downloads, says Hasani. Liquid neural networks were inspired by worm brains. They are an extension of another type of neural network that predates transformers, called convolutional networks. The key innovation is a mechanism that lets a model adapt its behavior to new information, so it can learn as it goes. That’s not possible with transformers: Once a model is trained, its behavior is fixed. Liquid AI’s first models were pretty basic but could fly drones or drive vehicles. With LFMs, the company is trying to scale up its technology to compete with mainstream LLMs. Its new models match the performance of rivals four times bigger, including versions of Alibaba’s Qwen and Google’s open-source LLM Gemma. A typical LLM is built from a stack of transformers wired together. Liquid AI’s recent LFMs are hybrid models made up of 20% transformers and 80% liquid neural networks. That ratio was hit upon by another AI system that Liquid AI has built, which it uses to help design all its models. “It’s the core technology of our company right now,” says Hasani. This designer AI sifts through many different combinations of neural networks—liquid, convolutional, and more, as well as transformers—and comes up with designs that bolt different ones together to hit a sweet spot of performance and efficiency. Hasani thinks transformers were just the beginning: “Your brain is an AGI system, you know, and it operates with 20 watts of power. How is it possible? We can get a lot more innovative.” Almost all LLMs produce their output one word at a time. It makes sense, because that is how people speak and write. But for computers, it’s very inefficient. It is faster and cheaper for LLMs to generate text all at once—spitting out whole sentences or paragraphs in one shot. That’s the approach taken by Inception, a startup based in Palo Alto, California, which is building LLMs using a technique called diffusion. Diffusion is better known as the technology that drives most image and video generation models. Diffusion models are trained to take a random grid of pixels—like the static on an old TV set—and turn it into an image. They do this by working on all the pixels at the same time, figuring out which need changing to make the static look more like a high-definition photo. It turns out this process works on text too. Inception has trained its LLMs to take a random string of words and turn it into sentences that make sense. Diffusion LLMs still use transformers to encode meaning, but by producing whole blocks of text at once, they make transformers do more for less. “You’re still using a big transformer model, but you can predict many tokens at the same time,” says Inception’s cofounder and CEO, Stefano Ermon. “That’s why these models are so much faster and cost-efficient compared to what most other people are building today.” The challenge was to take a technology designed for image generation and apply it to text. With images, if you need to change a blue pixel to a red one you can step through intermediate colors, says Ermon. That doesn’t work with text: “When you have ‘cat’ and ‘dog,’ there is not really something in between.” Ermon is also a researcher at Stanford University. In 2024, he and a pair of his Stanford colleagues figured out the math to make diffusion models work with text. They trained a diffusion model that matched the performance of GPT-2—an LLM that OpenAI built in 2019—but was 10 times faster. It was enough for Ermon to spin out a company. Today he has his sights on the big league. Inception claims its latest model, Mercury 2, performs as well as some of OpenAI’s GPT-4 models, released in 2023, but again 10 times faster. “We’re bullish about this approach because it’s the one that is going to scale up,” says Ermon. The only things that matter are speed and cost, he adds: “Ultimately, the currency is going to be intelligence per dollar.” Inception is not the only company betting on diffusion. Google is also experimenting with this approach and has built a prototype LLM called Diffusion Gemma. But Ermon is not worried about the competition. “I think it's validating,” he says. “This is the future.” Pathway, another startup based in Palo Alto, is perhaps the most extreme of this new bunch. It wants to free LLMs from the constraints of language. The firm has built a type of LLM called Dragon Hatchling (named after the dragons in Terry Pratchett’s novel Color of Magic, which materialize if you think about them hard enough). Its standout result so far is a high score on a benchmark that pits LLMs against more than 250,000 very hard sudoku puzzles. Dragon Hatchling beat more than 97% of the puzzles; several leading LLMs from the top labs failed to solve any. The point Pathway wants to make is that despite their remarkable success at many different tasks, there are still crucial classes of problems where LLMs fail. Sudoku is just one example. If we want LLMs to come up with genuine, novel solutions to real problems, we need to move beyond transformers, says Pathway’s cofounder and CEO, Zuzanna Stamirowska. That’s because transformers force LLMs to do everything with text. But language is not the best tool for certain kinds of reasoning. “It’s very difficult to represent a sudoku board word by word,” says Stamirowska. Pathway’s solution is to change the math behind the transformer, replacing the attention mechanism with a mathematical structure called a state space. Instead of encoding information word by word, state spaces compress it into a more abstract representation. Using this technique, Dragon Hatchling can still process and produce text, but it can also mimic forms of reasoning that do not involve sequences of words. This not only makes Pathway’s model more efficient, but (in theory) it lets it take on tasks that other LLMs cannot do. Think of chess or mathematics—those kinds of puzzles are not held in your head as a long sentence, says Stamirowska: “The eureka moment that pops up in your brain isn’t necessarily in language. We would argue that if you have to reason in language, you’re somehow constrained.” Stamirowska admits that a mainstream LLM could read a book about how to solve sudoku and then write code to do it. But we want to build models with more than book smarts, she says: “The hope for AI is not to solve sudoku; it’s to cure cancer. There’s not a book for that.” “Transformers are an engineering convenience that we fell on,” she adds. “It started a religion, but it’s silly to think that a breakthrough won’t happen again.” Subquadratic has now shared more details about its new model. But some are still skeptical. It makes it easy to trick them into doing things they shouldn’t, such as telling you how to sabotage an aircraft’s navigation system. A new technique has let the company probe deeper than ever into the weird workings of an LLM. The company is doubling down on AI for science.
