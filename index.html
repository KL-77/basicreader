The Bantu expansion changed Africa. Scientists are still tracing how.

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-07

Visit Original Website
It’s not easy to extract iron from its ore. But the peoples of the ancient city of Great Zimbabwe—the capital of a thriving kingdom in southern Africa between 1000 to 1700 AD—were masters. At sites near the city’s stone ruins, clusters of iron-smelting furnaces survive today as mounds of rubble. At Mashava, a village 25 miles northwest, fragments bear fingermarks of those who long ago smoothed the structures’ clay interiors. Some of the furnaces were nearly an adult’s arm span in width, with walls thick and strong to contain the heat of more than 2,100° Fahrenheit needed to smelt iron. Unlike other furnaces that required workers to force air into them with bellows, these were designed to suck air in, then out through a chimney, no human labor required. This would have made it significantly easier to produce the iron, which was fashioned into hoes, axes, and arrowheads. “The number and size of these furnaces, together with the use of natural-draft technology, indicate intensive and technologically sophisticated iron production,” says archaeometallurgist Ezekiel Mtetwa of Chicago’s Field Museum of Natural History, who has long studied the furnaces. Scientists are extremely interested in the peoples who created this and other iron-powered societies across eastern and southern Africa. Research points to an adaptable and innovative group from western Africa as the masterminds. Known as Bantu speakers, these communities began gradually expanding across the continent’s southern half, starting from 6,000 to 4,000 years ago. In doing so, they introduced a fundamentally new, sedentary way of life—characterized by farming, raising livestock, pottery-making, and metalworking—to regions previously dominated by hunter-gatherer communities. This migration transformed not only the region’s history and technology, but also its languages: Today, around 350 million Africans speak one of roughly 500 distinct Bantu tongues, from southern African Zulu to eastern African Swahili. And at least as many Africans—as well as many African Americans and Afro-Latinos—carry Bantu ancestry in their DNA. Reconstructing this expansion has been a tough puzzle, especially given the region’s sparse written historical records and limited research funding. But a unique blend of scientists—linguists, archaeologists, and geneticists—have teamed up to unearth clues about the deep origins of the Bantu-speaking people, their path across Africa, and their relationships with other cultures. Although much debate and uncertainty remain, little by little the work is uncovering one of history’s largest and longest human migrations. “It completely reshaped the demography in sub-Saharan Africa, and we are really interested in trying to understand it,” says population geneticist Sandra Beleza of the United Kingdom’s University of Leicester. The first evidence of the Bantu expansion came from observations of similarities among Bantu languages. They’re so alike, in fact, that when the Portuguese explorer Vasco da Gama sailed around the southern tip of Africa in 1497, a Portuguese crew member who had spent time in west-central Africa could reportedly communicate with locals in southeastern Africa. Linguists working in the 19th century started referring to the language family as Bantu, the plural of muntu, meaning “human being” in many Bantu tongues. Later linguists reasoned that the languages stemmed from an ancestral people who migrated across the continent from western Africa, which harbors the greatest diversity of Bantu languages (as would be expected for where the languages originated). Soon, archaeologists found supporting evidence: across eastern and southern Africa, pieces of pottery of similar yet diverse styles, alongside first-time traces of village life, metal-making, domesticated animals, and western African crops—all developed within the last 2,000 years. Northern sites usually dated back to earlier time periods than sites farther south. “You can clearly see a north-to-south progression,” says archaeological scientist Shadreck Chirikure of the University of Oxford. By the 1990s, however, some scholars began to question the idea that Bantu languages and skills spread through a migration. Couldn’t these cultural habits have spread while people stayed in the same place, with hunter gatherer communities passing along new technologies, languages, and habits to each other? Genetic techniques that could analyze people’s ancestry helped to resolve that debate. All of us share DNA with our relatives: 50 percent on average with siblings, 12.5 percent with cousins, 3.3 percent with second cousins, and so on. Though these proportions become minute the further back in time a shared common ancestor lived, statistical techniques can tease out patterns. Sure enough, a 2009 genetic analysis of 2,432 Africans found that Bantu-speaking people from western, central, and southern Africa are much more closely related than one might expect given their geographic distance. And in 2024, a team of scientists analyzed the genomes of 1,763 Africans and found the greatest genetic diversity among Bantu speakers in western Africa. As distance from the region increased, diversity declined. “This is a typical signature of a population expanding from one place to another,” explains coauthor Carina Schlebusch, a geneticist at Uppsala University in Sweden. But while linguistic and genetic data point to western Africa—particularly the Grassfields region of Cameroon—as the Bantu homeland, conclusive archaeological proof is thin. At the best-studied archaeological site in the Grassfields, a rock shelter called Shum Laka, pottery fragments appear dating from 7,000 to 6,000 years ago and become abundant by 5,000 to 4,000 years ago. Bowls and jars at first decorated simply with incisions and stamps and later bearing the marks of carved or braided tools rolled over wet clay are a possible, yet inconclusive, trace of Bantu ancestors. South of Cameroon, archaeologists have searched for artifacts in one of the Bantu-speaking people’s first destinations: the Congo rainforest. A handful of times between 2010 and 2023, archaeologist Alexandre Livingstone Smith of Belgium’s Royal Museum for Central Africa traveled via boat along the Congo River as part of a team led by linguist Birgit Ricquier. In a number of villages, Smith found pottery shards of a style consistent with that found farther west, dating back to 2,300 to 2,000 years ago. “That would be the potential first Bantu settlers of the forest,” Smith says. Many experts interpret the trails of pottery as evidence that ceramics traditions were brought by Bantu-speaking people all the way from their original home. What’s unclear, however, is when and how Bantu-speaking peoples acquired their farming and metalworking skills—and, in particular, whether they invented iron smelting themselves or learned it from neighboring, iron-extracting cultures. Scientists have been able to deduce the path that Bantu speakers took to eastern and southern Africa. Some scholars have argued that the path split into two before entering the Congo rainforest, with one group heading east while another went south through the forest. But evidence is now mounting that these groups crossed the rainforest together, and only upon reaching its southern fringes roughly 2,500 to 3,500 years ago did they split into east-moving and southward-moving groups. Some of this evidence comes from linguistic studies. Not unlike the evolution of species, languages diverge and transform over time, creating “trees” that linguists can reconstruct from similarities in grammar and vocabulary. In 2015, a group of researchers did this computationally using data from about 400 Bantu languages. They observed a much larger diversity—and deeper splits—among the Bantu languages spoken in the Congo rainforest, suggesting that Bantu-speaking ancestors spent a long time there, allowing their languages to diversify. Languages spoken farther east and farther south appeared to stem from a less diverse subset of this larger group, explains coauthor Koen Bostoen, a linguist at Belgium’s Ghent University. Later linguistic studies, as well as genetic analyses, have reached similar conclusions. In 2023, Beleza and her colleagues compared the genomes of Bantu speakers from Angola—south of the Congo rainforest—with those from the eastern African country of Mozambique and ones from Cameroon, the Bantu homeland. Remarkably, the eastern African samples were more genetically similar to those from Angola, hundreds of miles to the south of Cameroon and south of the rainforest, than to any of the samples from Cameroon. That points to a recent common ancestor in the Angola region—suggesting that the Bantu split into eastern and southern streams only once they reached the rainforest’s southern edge, Beleza explains. By the time the Bantu people commenced their movement east and south, they had already come into their own as a farming, iron-making, livestock-herding, and pottery-making people, Chirikure says: Many ancient Bantu settlements across eastern and southern Africa contain evidence of this Bantu “package.” Key to the Bantu’s success in southern and eastern Africa, experts suspect, was their sedentary lifestyle. This—and their iron tools—would have allowed them to produce more food and grow in number, propelling them to occupy new territories. Their lifestyle affected the landscape as forests turned into farmland, and it also affected the hunter-gatherer communities who were already living there. Some groups were likely crowded out and disappeared, experts suspect, while others were probably pushed to marginal regions, including Indigenous groups such as rainforest hunter-gatherers and Khoisan peoples, who continue to live in central and southern Africa, respectively, to this day. Still, scientists are learning that Bantu peoples had deep relationships with these two groups. In the 2024 genetic analysis, Schlebusch and her colleagues found that some Bantu-speaking groups in central Africa share about an eighth of their DNA with rainforest hunter-gatherers, and some southern African Bantu speakers share a similar amount with Khoisan groups. Clearly, their ancestors once intermixed. Curiously, in the case of the Khoisan, this mixing tended to arise from pairings of Bantu-speaking men with Khoisan women. One 2021 study of southern African Bantu speakers found that the DNA inside their mitochondria—which people inherit only from their mothers—had greater-than-expected proportions of Khoisan DNA. But the Y chromosomes, which come from fathers, had much higher proportions of DNA with Bantu ancestry. Some experts speculate that this pattern could be the result of Bantu men raiding Khoisan women or preferentially marrying Khoisan women for whom the traditional bride prices would have been cheaper. “Whenever there is a power difference… between two groups, it tends to melt down to this,” says coauthor Ananyo Choudhury, a geneticist at the University of Witwatersrand in South Africa. This pattern also left marks on the language. The characteristic clicks of Khoisan languages also occur in southern African Bantu languages Zulu and Khosa. Bostoen suspects that the children of Khoisan-Bantu pairings were, at first, bilingual. Eventually, “they lost their mothers’ languages, but they left a substrate of the mothers’ languages in the Bantu language.” Archaeologists are also studying the relationships between Bantu farmers and their neighbors. While there may have been conflicts over land, the groups also cooperated with and learned from one another, says Décio Muianga, an archaeologist at Eduardo Mondlane University and the research institute Kaleidoscopio in Maputo, Mozambique. He’s been excavating a rock shelter in Mozambique containing thousands of years’ worth of stone tools, wild animal bones, and wild plant remains—telltale signs of hunter-gatherers living at the site. But interestingly, Bantu-style pottery also appears. “My assumption is that the hunter-gatherers were getting these ceramics from the farmers,” Muianga says. “They were interacting, communicating, and exchanging resources.” There is evidence, too, for cultural exchanges: European anthropologists have reported that some Bantu-speaking groups would solicit San hunter-gatherers to conduct traditional rainmaking ceremonies, for example. Bantu social networks eventually extended well beyond Africa. By 1100 to 1600 AD, many of the complex state societies that Bantu speakers built developed trade networks across the Indian Ocean. Archaeological data shows they imported Chinese porcelain and glass beads; in return, they exported gold, ivory, and iron. In fact, a 12th century Muslim cartographer once wrote that iron from parts of southern Africa—likely referring to Great Zimbabwe’s iron-producing hinterlands, Mtetwa suspects—was deemed to be of much higher quality than that from other places like India. Mtetwa has found evidence that iron smelters near Great Zimbabwe would add bone fragments to the furnaces, perhaps as part of a smelting-blessing ritual, but one with scientific merit: This material contains calcium, which can accelerate the separation of iron from the rest of the ore. That may be a testament to the Great Zimbabweans’ iron-smelting skills: “Some materials introduced within ritual or medicinal practices may simultaneously have had technically beneficial effects on the smelt,” says Mtetwa. Though they’ve made great progress in reconstructing the Bantu world, scientists are aware of, and trying to address, the limitations of their research so far. Linguistic studies, for instance, have been a popular tool to reconstruct Bantu history, but Bostoen cautions that very few Bantu languages have been documented thoroughly enough to definitively trace linguistic trees back in time. Linguists may never get a complete dataset, because some old branches of the Bantu language family may have died out. Another limitation of computational Bantu language-family reconstructions is that they’ve usually assumed that languages have evolved in isolation after different Bantu groups separated. But there may have been extensive cross-pollination among languages as different groups met and interacted. In fact, when linguists factored in the possibility of contact among Bantu languages in a 2026 study, they couldn’t reconstruct the diversification patterns described by previous linguists, says coauthor Igor Yanovich of the University of Vienna, who wrote about the challenges of Bantu linguistic studies in the 2025 Annual Review of Linguistics. In his view, linguists should first focus on thoroughly documenting Bantu languages before attempting to use them to understand Bantu history. In hopes of adding new insights, Schlebusch is working to obtain DNA from ancient human remains. By revealing the identity of people who were living in particular places at particular times, this could help to elucidate when and where different Bantu communities moved into, overlapped with, or repopulated areas. “What we see more and more in genetic data is that the movement was probably complex—that it was these ‘spread over spread’ events,” Schlebusch says. Archaeologists, for their part, hope to fill in the vast gaps in the archaeological record of sub-Saharan Africa—by both conducting new excavations and re-examining existing artifact collections for fresh insights. But all this work requires funding for research and laboratories, especially from African governments, says Chirikure; he, like many of his colleagues, has largely relied on funding from Europe, which is limited. Without more funding, “you are not generating fresh ideas, you are not formulating new hypotheses and testing them using some of the cutting-edge science,” he says. Scientists from all three disciplines are hopeful that they will eventually tell the complete story of how an innovative people spread and diversified across sub-Saharan Africa, mixing traditions with those of earlier peoples and of others who still live there, to create myriad cultures, each one a unique blend of food, philosophy, language, music, and traditions. “If you travel from West Africa today all the way to East Africa, you have a unique cultural diversity that is very hard to find in many other parts of the world,” Muianga says. An Estonian newspaper recently revealed that a British soldier disobeyed a direct order to remove the SIM card from his cellphone before being deployed to a part of Estonia, approximately 90 miles west of the Russian border. As a result of his refusal to engage in this element of operational security (opsec), the soldier, Daniel Barton-Kennedy, had his phone hacked, and nude photos of himself were stolen and used as blackmail against him. Eventually, Barton-Kennedy was court-martialed for disobeying a lawful command, pled guilty, was reduced in rank, and ordered to serve 10 months in detention. According to a 21-page British appeals court document shared with Ars Technica by Eesti Ekspress, the Estonian newspaper that first reported on this saga, the story begins in January 2026. At the time, Barton-Kennedy was a corporal, with over seven years of service, serving in the 1st Battalion of the Duke of Lancaster Regiment of the British Army. His unit was deployed to participate in “Exercise Eastern Warrior” in Tapa, Estonia, roughly halfway between Tallinn, the capital city, and the Estonia-Russia border. While in the United Kingdom, on January 7, Barton-Kennedy, along with other members of his unit, were briefed on their orders and given specific instructions to “remove SIM cards and switch off location settings and to use a trusted virtual private network on all personal electronic devices.” Once the unit arrived in Estonia, days later, they were given further briefings and reminders of this policy. Despite this, Barton-Kennedy ignored the instruction. On January 19, Barton-Kennedy informed his platoon commander that he had received a message “from an unknown party who had acquired nude photographs of him from the photo section of his mobile phone and had identified him on social media.” Barton-Kennedy initially acquiesced to paying the attacker 100 British pounds ($133) but balked when he was asked to pay 250 British pounds ($333), and only then informed his platoon commander what had happened. Eventually, Barton-Kennedy was court-martialed and pled guilty at a hearing on July 30. During that process, according to the court filing, Barton-Kennedy understood the orders that were given to him at the time, but asked the court to “consider the personal circumstances” that he faced at the time. Without his phone, he told the court, he could not reach his daughter on her birthday, nor his pregnant partner. In a written statement, Barton-Kennedy said he was stunned when he received the first blackmail message. “Despite still being in a state of shock and uncertainty, I recognised that I had a duty to report the compromise without any further delay,” he wrote. Additionally, he said that he “deeply regrets” the incident and added that serving in the British Army “has been one of the greatest privileges of my life.” Barton-Kennedy appealed the sentence, which was denied in August 2026. In response to an inquiry for comment, Lt. Col. EP Mackie, a British Army spokesperson, told Ars in a statement that the security measures imposed are meant to protect the safety of military personnel. “Any breaches of Army security measures that might compromise personal safeguarding or operational security will be dealt with swiftly, and offending personnel can expect uncompromising disciplinary action,” he wrote.

A man in his 20s showed up to a UK hospital with the hilt of a knife sticking out of his face. The 15 mm-wide blade hidden within went completely through his right eye and through his brain, with the tip of the blade hitting the top of his skull. Despite the ghastly injury, the man was alive and conscious when he arrived. He could still talk and move all four limbs. His doctors described him as being in a state of agitation. On the Glasgow Coma Scale, he scored a 12 (E3 V4 M5), indicating a “moderate” brain injury, just one point above “mild.” He could open his left eye when told, but otherwise kept it closed (E3 score); he could talk and answer questions, but was confused (V4); and he intentionally moved away when pressure was applied to his body (M5). In a report published Friday in BMJ Case Report, his doctors said they were concerned that in his confused, agitated state he may try to pull the knife out on his own. So, they swiftly sedated and intubated him. They put him on prophylactic treatments for bacterial infections, including tetanus. Then they used images to see what was going on in his head. The blade was entirely through the right eye socket and eyeball. It traversed the left frontal and temporal lobes of his brain, passing through in front of—and without apparently injuring—the corpus callosum, which connects the two sides of the brain and is full of nerve fibers. The blade also missed the left internal capsule, which holds another bundle of nerve fibers. In terms of damage to major blood vessels, doctors found that the blade was close to the left internal carotid artery, a major blood vessel, but didn’t seem to have damaged it. Doctors did find, however, that a branch of the middle cerebral artery looked to be indented by the knife. After consulting with various medical specialties, the man’s doctors moved to emergency neurosurgery. They opted to remove part of his skull to directly see into the brain, but used a conservative “pull-and-see” method to slide the blade out rather than invasively excavate it. After it was out, the doctors saw no signs of significant hemorrhaging. The brain wound was cleaned and drained before surgeons patched up his skull. His right eye, however, couldn’t be saved. Given its extensive damage, it was removed. The man then moved to intensive care, where he recovered rapidly. A day after surgery, doctors were able to remove his breathing tube. He developed some minor right-side weakness, but it resolved two days later. Scans of his brain showed steady improvement—a little bleeding and swelling, and there was some air in his brain tissue from where the knife had been. But otherwise, it appeared to be healing. He developed no infections and had no seizures. Despite the “dramatic intracranial trajectory,” his language function was preserved, and he had no loss of movement or coordination, they reported. He was discharged from the hospital and at follow-up appointments remained “neurologically well,” with ongoing eye and mental health care. As for how the injury happened, his doctors still don’t know. “The mechanism is unclear,” they wrote. However, they noted he had a history of depression. The doctors reflected on how lucky the man was to have survived and to have such a full recovery. Such injuries that go through the eye and the brain are rare. When they do occur, they’re usually from “high-velocity mechanisms” such as gunshots or other ballistic injuries. Knives, rods, and nails fit into the rarer “low-velocity mechanisms. “Such “low-velocity penetrating brain injuries carry substantial morbidity and mortality because of hemorrhage, vascular injury, abscess, meningitis, cerebrospinal fluid leak, seizures and neurological deficit,” they wrote. But such fortunate recoveries aren’t unheard of; they cited reports of other remarkably unexpected recoveries from seemingly devastating brain injuries. That includes the famous case of Phineas Gage, a railroad foreman who survived an 1848 accident that sent a large iron rod directly through his head. The rod entered below his left cheekbone and exited the top of his skull. (Although he survived and recovered, Gage’s personality was said to have changed after the accident. The doctors in the knife-wound case did not mention any such personality changes.) “Echoing classic and contemporary examples of severe frontal lobe penetration with unexpectedly preserved neurological function, including Phineas Gage and a reported nail-gun injury, this case highlights that outcome may depend less on external severity than on precise trajectory relative to critical vascular, cortical and subcortical structures,” the doctors concluded.

Ships loiter near subsea cables amid Iran's warnings to Trump

Several tanker ships linked to Iran have been loitering over subsea Internet cables in the Gulf of Oman near the Strait of Hormuz—a possible indication that the Iranian government is preparing to attack such undersea infrastructure in response to any renewed US and Israeli strikes. The movements of three sanctioned tankers and an Iranian fishing boat have been tracked near major undersea cables by the maritime intelligence firm Windward in October, according to the Financial Times. Six active cables currently cross through the contested Strait of Hormuz, linking the Middle East to Europe, Africa and Asia, with two more cables having segments under construction in the region. Four countries, including Iraq, Kuwait, Bahrain and Qatar, depend entirely upon such subsea telecommunications connections running through the Strait of Hormuz chokepoint to link with the outside world. But other countries also depend heavily on the region’s undersea cable network—one third of India’s westbound Internet traffic goes through the Persian Gulf and Strait of Hormuz. One cable under construction, the 2Africa cable, is owned by a tech consortium that includes the US company Meta. That was one of the major cable corridors visited by the drifting Iran-linked ships. Windward used both ship-tracking data and satellite images to track the vessels as they slowed down and looped around near multiple cables, with some ships then “going dark” by switching off their automatic identification system (AIS) transponders that normally broadcast their location and other information. Such movement and behavioral patterns suggest the Iran-linked ships may be “mapping the cable routes, or placing charges or mines on them while dark,” Windward told the Financial Times. Windward highlighted the examples of two sanctioned tankers linked to Iran that spent days drifting “directly over regional cable corridors… both with no commercial reason to stop” in a blog post on October 26. Both ships carried no commercial cargo. Since the US and Israel first launched a major war with Iran in February 2026, Iran’s response beyond targeting US military bases, ships and aircraft has included attacking commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz and hitting oil refineries and other energy infrastructure along with airports in various Gulf countries. Iran has also made a point of threatening or attacking US Big Tech infrastructure in the Middle East, including launching multiple drone strikes on Amazon data centers. In May 2026, a spokesperson for Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps claimed Iran would “impose fees on Internet cables” belonging to US tech companies and other owners. But more recently, multiple Iranian officials and commanders of Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) have warned that they could directly attack the undersea cables running through the Strait of Hormuz. This came after President Donald Trump repeatedly threatened to renew major US military strikes on Iran and warned he could “annihilate” the country during a speech at the United Nations in New York on September 22. “Seven major cables run through the Strait of Hormuz, supporting global Internet operations and commercial data transmission,” one IRGC spokesperson said on October 20. “We remind the enemy of this.” The latest phase of the war has involved more limited US military strikes against Iranian targets, even as Trump has alternated between threats and touting the possibility of a diplomatic breakthrough. Meanwhile, Iran has increased drone strikes and other attacks on commercial ships to exert its own pressure on the United States, with October marking the highest number of ship attacks since the war began. Much damage has already been done. The limited fleet of cable-laying ships used for installation and repair has not been able to venture safely into the region since the war began, which forced the Meta-backed 2Africa cable to pause its planned expansion. The war has also compelled US tech companies and Gulf countries to accelerate efforts to develop alternative cable routes bypassing the Strait of Hormuz. The Middle East is not the only part of the world where threats to the Internet’s undersea backbone have intensified. Subsea infrastructure is increasingly at risk from sabotage and gray-zone warfare from the Baltic Sea to the Pacific Ocean.

Many people are not big fans of game publisher EA right now for good reason, but for those who remember a better time in the 1990s, there’s a little good news. Twenty-five classic games from that EA era have been lightly remastered and updated, then placed for sale on Steam and GOG, including several Wing Commander games and nearly the entire Ultima series. Below is the full list of remastered or re-released games: In a lengthy blog post published this week, Jim Vessella, a senior producer at EA, described the efforts that went into remastering Wing Commander I-IV and Prophecy, Privateer 2, and Road Rash. It’s a long, detailed story that’s worth reading yourself, but here’s a top-level summary. After working on the mostly widely acclaimed Command & Conquer: Remastered Collection in 2019, Vessella writes that he learned about and visited a room at EA’s Redwood Shores campus that contained a large archive of poorly labeled or sorted physical media from ages past. In his free time, and with the help of others, he began cataloging this archive and discovered, among other things, that it contained virtually the entirety of the original masters of Wing Commander III and Wing Commander IV‘s full-motion-video cutscenes. The story that ensues involves building a small team of contractors, poring through the footage 17 hours a day on swing shifts to label and digitize it, and then coordinating with several key community members—particularly Matt Wells, who has made community enhancement patches for some of the Wing Commander games—to put together remasters of those games. The flagship offering of those remasters is much, much higher-quality full-motion video sequences than have ever been available before, but other enhancements include 60 frames-per-second gameplay, a plethora of newly available .INI tweaks, and full controller support (though looking at the Steam forums for the games, some folks are having trouble with said controller support). For Privateer 2—the little-remembered sequel to Wing Commander: Privateer—the crew couldn’t find the original, high-quality remasters of the FMV scenes, and ended up getting in touch with the game’s original cinematics director, Steve Hilliker, who had the tapes in his attic. Hilliker had always wanted to take a new color-grading pass, and he did just that, while also remastering and upscaling all of the cutscenes. Similar updates were given to Wing Commander Prophecy. The blog post also details GOG’s involvement in restoring and remastering Prophecy and Road Rash. For the latter, EA re-licensed the entire original soundtrack for re-release. The e collection is on sale on GOG and Steam right now; previous owners of the old versions of the games on GOG and the EA storefront get these remasters for free. None of the games cost more than $5 each. One good story doesn’t undo a lot of bad news and questionable decisions from EA, but this shows that a few passionate people can do something remarkable for game preservation, even if some of the work was done on a volunteer basis outside normal working hours. (EA committed some resources to the restorations as the project progressed, though.) While the remasters aren’t perfect, the gaming industry could use news like this. It’s worth reading the full story in the blog post for a journey down memory lane and a look at how involved a restoration process like this can be.

Blockchain analysis firm Chainalysis said that hackers have obtained sensitive personal information—including names, usernames, password hashes, and live tokens—for all of its users following a breach of its network security. The accomplishment could be a major coup for the attackers. Both Chainalysis and public sources have revealed a long list of high-profile customers, including government agencies such as the FBI, US Immigration and Customs Enforcement agency, and the US Air Force, as well as private companies such as Microsoft, Coinbase, and Barclays. It’s unclear if the data theft exposed customers to additional threats. Last Friday, Chainalysis said it had been the target of a cybersecurity incident in October that was similar to the hacks of other platforms during the same month. The external actor used AI agent-assisted offensive techniques to gain unauthorized access to Chainalysis systems and internal tools. An email the company sent to affected customers said some “non-sensitive customer data, such as user IDs, full names, email addresses, and organization role labels for users provisioned on our platform were accessed.” It’s not clear why the company considered such information “non-sensitive.” In a Wednesday update, the company said that part of the “ongoing multifront cyberattack” involved an “on-path attack” that exposed a subset of customer passwords, and that the attack vector had been identified and fixed. Also known as an adversary-in-the-middle attack, on-path attacks happen when a hacker gains access to a piece of infrastructure sending traffic back and forth between two parties. Chainalysis has yet to say where the infrastructure compromise occurred and if the data obtained from it was TLS encrypted. The company said later that day that an investigation two days earlier (on Monday) had revealed that “certain customer information including names, usernames, password hashes and live tokens for all Chainalysis users were exposed to a malicious actor.” It also said it had detected “abnormal activity” and, in response, took down its login page and know-your-transaction service, which monitors threats to cryptocurrency holdings in real time. The company has also removed access to its Reactor and Data Solutions services. In an update on Friday, Chainalysis said it had begun phasing in customer access to KYT and Address Screening and expected more customers to follow. It asked customers to submit their trusted IP addresses so they can be added to an allow list and to prepare to reset credentials once they regain access. The credential rotation is mandatory. The company says its investigation has yet to reveal any evidence that any investigation data has been accessed or Chainalysis attribution data has been manipulated. Several important questions remain unanswered. As noted, the source that made the on-path attack possible is still unknown. About two-thirds of humanity now lives in countries where fertility is too low for each generation to replace itself. The result is rapidly aging workforces in most of the world, and the population in parts of Europe is already shrinking. One way to measure aging is by looking at the old-age dependency ratio, a demographic indicator that captures the number of people in working age (15 to 64) relative to the number of elderly, retired people (64 and older). By 2035, for instance, Italy’s old-age dependency ratio will have risen from an already high 39% to 50%. Greece, Portugal, and Spain are close behind. A similar demographic shift is underway in Latin America, including in Cuba, Puerto Rico, Costa Rica, and Colombia. Just a few decades ago, these countries had much smaller elderly populations compared to Europe and thus had lower old-age dependency ratios. Now they are catching up with Europe on this measure. In some parts of Asia, demographic concerns are more heightened as populations are expected to age even faster than in Europe and Latin America, especially in China, South Korea, Taiwan, and Thailand. By 2035, the old-age dependency ratio is expected to increase from 21% to almost 34% in China. In Hong Kong, it is expected to exceed 55%. North America’s population will continue to grow and will age more moderately, mostly because of immigration. In the United States, the old-age dependency ratio is forecast to increase from around 28.8% today to about 34% by 2035. Immigration policy will shape the exact extent of population change and aging in the country. The implications of this global trend are far-reaching, and for many, simply alarming. As the International Monetary Fund (IMF) puts it starkly: “For those countries where fewer workers are available and labor force participation rates drop, economic output is bound to fall.” Other forecasts warning about pressures on living standards, fiscal problems, and broader social challenges are equally gloomy. And aging will affect politics and social dynamics in various, often unforeseen ways, opening up new fissures in society. Yet demographic change can also create  opportunities, especially in societies whose institutions can adapt and that can make the right technological investments. These are epochal challenges. We cannot afford complacency, but alarmism is not warranted either. Several of the consequences of aging are not as dire as many assume and claim. Aging shifts the balance of electoral power toward older generations, which complicates the politics of pensions. The stakes are especially high in Italy, Portugal, and Spain, where public pension obligations are ballooning and are expected to exceed 15% of GDP by 2045. An aging population could also make it harder for a society to form a consensus on forward-looking investments that may take decades to pay off, such as those necessary for a more complete green transition. Some researchers have pointed to the age divide in the Brexit vote in the United Kingdom. In the 2016 referendum on whether Britain should leave the European Union, most British voters 65 and older chose Leave, while those under 25 overwhelmingly voted Remain. With stagnant or declining populations in Europe and parts of Asia, and a growing young population in Africa, the pressure for international migration will intensify as well. Recent experience suggests that migration could become an even greater source of political tensions. These are valid concerns, but the glass may be more than half full. Aging is accompanied by longer, healthier lives. The prospect of more healthy years extends the horizon of all age groups in society and could become a basis for new coalitions supporting public investments for the present and the future. Younger and expanding populations are no recipe for consensual politics either. I worked with the economists Leopoldo Fergusson and Simon Johnson to study what happened in developing countries in the early decades after World War II as their mortality rates declined sharply. Antibiotics and other new medicines, along with public health campaigns against diseases such as malaria, helped more people survive. More children lived past infancy; populations grew rapidly. We found that these population expansions and growing numbers of younger people in the developing world led to greater domestic conflict and a much higher likelihood of civil war. Competition and conflict over resources were fierce. Welcome to this week’s edition of the Surge, a politics newsletter that totally predicted Democrats would get 260 House seats and 56 Senate seats in our heads weeks ago but, you know, didn’t feel like telling anyone. Well that was sump’m else, huh? Crazy to think that Democrats were toast two years ago and that, starting in January, every American will be required to pledge daily allegiance to a nonbinary god. But anyway, the 2026 midterms are old news—what’s going on with the 2028 Republican presidential field? And could Democrats have picked up more than a measly nine Senate seats? Will anyone not vote to impeach Pete Hegseth? Let’s first look ahead to what Democrats are planning to do with these majorities beyond passing national Sharia law. If there’s any downside to flipping (likely) nine Senate seats and winning the largest House majority this century, it’s convincing the voters who brought you there that they’ve already seen the greatest accomplishments of the next two years. The very act of winning ends Republicans’ partisan legislative agenda. It ends Donald Trump’s ability to gift 37-year-old brainmelt cases lifetime appointments to the federal bench. Trump will not be able to get another Supreme Court nominee confirmed come January. He will not be allowed to do as he pleases with the understanding that congressional committees will willingly ignore it. The most important elements of the incoming Democratic agenda, in other words, have already been completed. In terms of how Democrats will spend their next two years in Congress, though, there’s not much to overthink. They were elected principally on two issues: lowering the cost of living and reining in corruption. They should stick to those. On the cost of living, they will vote early and often to end Trump’s tariff regime, either by disapproving existing ones or passing new legislation reclaiming congressional tariff authority. They will vote early and often on war powers resolutions to end the Iran war. They will vote to reverse Medicaid cuts—new work requirements for which will kick in next year—and Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program cuts, which have already kicked in. They’ll vote to restore enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies to lower health insurance premiums. In short, they’ll vote to reverse all of the MAGA moves that voters hated and that cost Republicans the election, leaving Republicans the choice to filibuster, veto, or sustain Trump’s veto on any of these popular moves. Trickier tactical decisions will come on must-pass government-funding bills. Most notably, Democrats can use the power of the purse to cut off funding for the Iran war, and they’ll attempt something along those lines in their opening bids. But we can already see many of the new, novice Democratic members and senators from red states getting queasy when the question becomes whether to shut down the government over the issue, even though Democrats (and Republicans, for that matter) have heard pretty resoundingly from the voters that they want this war finished. On corruption, there’s more to do beyond oversight hearings—and there will be oh so many of those. Because those hearings can and should fold into legislation. Among the many things we’re eager to learn more about is the level of insider trading, favor granting, and prediction-market gambling from within the White House. We suspect the findings will be substantial. And that’s a great opportunity to extend popular proposed bans on congressional stock trading to the White House, as well as to ban prediction-market betting in the executive branch. As important as any individual policy, though, is the need to stick to them, and to stay focused squarely on what midterm voters demanded without getting sidetracked by factional demands from one segment of these big, broad majorities that put them at odds with the other. Democrats were handed a gift from God in these midterms that they didn’t actually deserve. The Democratic coalition had been in a death spiral, having comprehensively lost the white working-class vote and watching anxiously as minority working-class voters drifted in the same direction. Without some correction, the Democratic coalition would soon be down to English majors. Thanks to Trump doing everything in his power to shatter the very groups that got him elected, Democrats were able to earn a tryout in the Great Plains, in the Deep South, in Texas. If they want to stay a viable party in the Senate and the Electoral College, it’s imperative to show that they’ll stick to the program. Vice President J.D. Vance’s presidential hopes nosedived on Election Day, and he knows it. He’s the deputy to, and chief public cheerleader for, the very same president that the nation comprehensively rejected. And his legacy contribution to the midterm campaigns was to serve as chief public cheerleader for Ohio gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy after the candidate weaponized his opponent’s childhood rape against her. The 2028 presidential nomination has never looked more up for grabs. So it wasn’t surprising that his damage control came quickly and at length in a Twitter essay the following morning. And it was our first look at how he would attempt to balance his day job with his need to separate himself from his day job. “The reason we lost is the war,” Vance wrote. “The president himself said so last night.” OK, sure, Trump admitted that energy prices were the factor. It gets more interesting in the next part. “If you’d like to argue it was worth it, that losing the Senate for the next 6 years was worth it, then do so,” he added. “Argue it was a worthwhile cost to pay. Agree or disagree, that’s at least honest.” To whom is this directed? He’s braying vaguely against neocons and hawks within the party. But he’s not allowed to be the avatar of the anti-war faction, as he’d like to be, because his boss started the war and made Vance a spokesperson for it. Maybe Trump will give him permission to become an open opponent of the war—he’s generally pretty loosey-goosey about demanding loyalty from his subjects, right? Right. Best of luck to Vance as he figures all this out! He’s not the only presidential hopeful entering the essay contest. (It’s not a coincidence that these guys all have between one and two Ivy League diplomas.) Ted Cruz, now the lone statewide-elected Republican in the postrevolutionary Woke Texas, rattled off and gave credence to a whole list of voters’ concerns: “Food is too high, gas is too high, diesel is too high, insurance is too high, life is too high,” he wrote. “Tariffs have made everything too expensive, and the Iran war has dragged on too long.” He observes these things as if they just came out of the blue. Why are there tariffs? Why is there an Iran war? This man behind the curtain goes unnamed. The game, then, is correctly blaming Trump’s policies for why the GOP crashed and burned, without ascribing those policy choices to a source. For the 80th time: It’s not complicated why the GOP lost! Many of them come from Trump. Former Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, a notable supporting figure in the administration’s collapse, posted: “We were too successful the first year of the Trump administration.” Ah, yes, the winning was all too front-loaded, cannibalizing wins that could have been had closer to election time. But Trump’s approval rating has been a downward line since Day 1. Some right-wing commentators blamed the failure of Senate Republicans to pass the SAVE America Act, the GOP’s strict voter-ID and voter-verification law, for the losses. He certainly benefited from the performance of Susan Collins, who, even in losing, was the only Senate candidate to improve upon Republicans’ statewide margin relative to 2024. Did Trump Learn Anything From the Midterms? We Already Have Our Answer.

After suffering a historic rebuke in this week’s midterms, the infamously brash Donald Trump sounded almost chastened. “We’ll, you know, look forward to working with them,” he said of Democrats in a subdued election-night phone call with the New York Times. “We’ll probably work something out.” Even as Trump emphasized his power to veto legislation, his postelection comments led some to imagine that a humbled dealmaker in chief might really be willing to play ball. “Donald Trump may be a bit crazy, but he is not stupid, and he is a good politician,” Sen. Bernie Sanders said on MSNBC, suggesting the president might even be open to signing a Medicare for All bill into law. In the end, Trump’s conciliatory tone lasted less than three days. On Friday afternoon, the president announced that he was elevating Stephen Miller, an anti-immigrant hawk who has been with him since the 2016 campaign, to be his next White House chief of staff, describing Miller as “one of my most trusted advisors.” Democrats, including some who previously sounded hopeful about persuading Trump to back legislation that would lower costs for inflation-pinched Americans, reacted with fury. “Stephen Miller is a dangerous xenophobe and reckless extremist who has American blood on his hands,” wrote Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next House speaker. “His presence in government should be eradicated like a malignant tumor. Fire him today.” It isn’t unusual for presidents to reshuffle the org chart after a midterm drubbing. But those personnel changes are usually a tacit admission that something has gone wrong, a public hitting of the reset button. After Republicans flipped the House in 2010, Barack Obama replaced Rahm Emanuel, his feisty chief of staff, with Bill Daley, a pragmatic centrist whom Obama hoped could help him work across the aisle. In the wake of a brutal loss, letting certain heads roll can look like a politically savvy sop to the will of the voters. But Trump, who infamously tried to overturn his 2020 election defeat, has never cared much for the will of the voters. And promoting Miller, who is among the administration’s most despised figures, is the opposite of what most presidents would do after an overwhelming electoral beatdown. Even among Trump aides, Miller has an unusually long history of bigotry. In high school, he criticized administrators for allowing Spanish-language announcements and once reportedly jumped into a girls’ track race in an apparent attempt to prove the athletic superiority of men. In college, he wrote columns for Duke’s student newspaper that criticized feminism and argued that “Christianity is embedded in the very soul of our nation.” As an aide to an Alabama senator, Miller promoted white-supremacist talking points, including the racist “great replacement” conspiracy theory. After arriving at the White House, he zealously defended separating migrant families at the border. In the president’s second term, Miller helped architect Operation Metro Surge, the January immigration-enforcement blitz in Minneapolis that killed two American citizens whom Miller falsely branded domestic terrorists and assassins. At first glance, doubling down on Miller now looks like a strange choice even for Trump. The president appears to be well aware of how extreme his adviser’s views are. As the Wall Street Journal reported this week in a midterms postmortem, Trump has privately admitted that Miller’s zeal for deportations had “gone too far.” The evidence suggests that backlash to those efforts helped sweep Republicans out of power across the country this week, particularly among Latino voters. But promoting Miller starts to make more sense when you consider who Trump is at his core: a confrontational man who managed to get elected president twice in large part by stoking divisions over immigration. Ever in search of a scapegoat for his own failures, Trump has reportedly blamed Republicans’ hefty midterm losses on Susie Wiles, whom Miller is set to replace as chief of staff. And rather than confront the reality of defeat, Trump appears to be seeking succor among his core supporters. “Prominent MAGA figures I talked to this week cited Stephen Miller as their dream choice for chief of staff,” the Daily Mail reporter Charlie Spiering posted after the news broke. “This pick really re-energizes a gloomy base.” The president also prizes loyalty, and Miller spent the years between Trump’s terms laying the groundwork for a “spectacular migration crackdown.” We’ve also lived through a version of this before. In 2018, after Democrats retook the House, Trump sounded similar notes of conciliation. “We have a lot of things in common on infrastructure,” he said the day after the election. “We want to do something on healthcare, they want to do something on healthcare, there are a lot of great things we could do together.” None of it ended up happening. Instead, the president soon found himself in a standoff with Democrats over his demand that a government funding bill include money for his southern border wall, an impasse that yielded what was then the longest government shutdown in U.S. history. A repeat performance may already be in the offing. The federal government will run out of money next month, and elevating Miller isn’t the only sign that Trump plans to keep immigration at the forefront. “Deportations will continue,” Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin posted after Democrats won the House and Senate. Even Vice President J.D. Vance, who on Wednesday published a 600-word X post blaming the war in Iran for Republicans’ dismal midterm, showed little interest in finding common ground over immigration. “I’m open to the idea that we should change enforcement—by focusing more on businesses that hire illegal labor, for instance,” Vance wrote. “But anyone pushing amnesty is lying to you.” On Friday, Vance put out a statement congratulating Miller on his promotion. Elevating Miller isn’t the only sign that Trump plans to spend the last two years of his second term the same way he spent the first two. Democrats won key races by casting the president as an out-of-touch billionaire more interested in beautifying Washington than in making groceries cheaper, but Trump is already hitting the gas on finishing his White House ballroom and erecting a triumphal arch near the Lincoln Memorial. After an election in which many voters rejected building data centers to power artificial intelligence, Trump reiterated in a Friday post on Truth Social that “only foolish leadership will say ‘NO’ to the riches they bring!” And even as key Trump allies seem to want the president to make Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth the fall guy for Iran, some reporters think replacing Wiles with Miller could save Hegseth’s job. But the core issue of Miller’s promotion is immigration, the subject of the incoming chief of staff’s overriding obsession. For nearly two years now, this administration’s deportation policies have brought death, violence, and misery to communities across the country. With Miller at the helm, it would be a surprise if the next two years turn out much better. In the early evening on Tuesday in Los Angeles, it was becoming clear that big things were afoot a few thousand miles away, in Texas. Gina Hinojosa was about to become governor-elect, unseating Greg Abbott, and, in the Senate race, James Talarico was about to flush Ken Paxton down the toilet. Over here on the West Coast, I had been hoping to spend the night covering the campaign parties of Karen Bass (the establishment Democrat mayor) and Nithya Raman (the progressive challenger, who on Thursday would be declared the winner). But I couldn’t connect with the campaigns to get a press credential. And so instead of parachuting into the city’s Democrat-on-Democrat conflict, I found some Texans who were about to spike the football after beating a much grander enemy. The Chicks were about to take the stage for their third night at the Dolby Theatre in Hollywood. I love this band. Their song “Easy Silence” was the first-dance song at our wedding. The ticket was a mere $40 on StubHub, and I wanted to see the show. But more than that, I had a feeling that going to see The Chicks on this night, of all nights, might mean witnessing the greatest display of “libbing out” in U.S. history. “Well, hello, Los Angeles,” lead singer Natalie Maines said after the night’s first song, as an audience likely lacking a single registered Republican roared in approval. Then all three bandmates pulled out red fans emblazoned with “FUCK TRUMP” and began fanning themselves as Maines went on. “Welcome to our bluenami. So glad we could enjoy this night together. Texas is blue. The Senate is blue. The House is blue. This would be a very different night if that wasn’t the case.” Maines also said she was on a significant amount of prednisone, and that it was keeping her going by this point in the tour. The last time an American president started a war of choice in the Middle East that became a presidency-ruining quagmire, this trio of Texan musicians became the first real victims of what, today, conservatives would call “cancel culture.” Touring in London, Maines let loose on a fellow Texan, George W. Bush. “Just so you know, we’re on the good side with y’all,” Maines told the crowd. “We do not want this war, this violence, and we’re ashamed that the president of the United States is from Texas.” Maines’ remarks became an expensive, life-threatening spectacle. Country radio stations took cues from an enraged conservative audience and reduced the band from one of the most popular acts in the country to a pariah, more or less overnight. Death threats prompted the installation of metal detectors outside their concerts. Maines and her bandmates, sisters Martie Maguire and Emily Strayer, required beefed-up personal security. Fans booed the announcement of their nomination at the Academy of Country Music Awards; they lost out to Toby Keith, who showed a fake picture of Maines and Saddam Hussein at his shows. The ladies didn’t get a measure of revenge until 2006, when they released a Grammy Awards Album of the Year, Taking the Long Way. The album’s most memorable song was “Not Ready to Make Nice,” a rebuke of a mother who would make her daughter hate a perfect stranger. In line at the door, I saw a woman in a black bra top and a cowboy hat practically cackling as she looked at the New York Times app and told a friend, “Talarico is doing it.” This was a Hollywood audience champing at the bit for the Chicks to lead a victory lap. What transpired was a bit of general-interest liberal catharsis, the concert equivalent of “Don’t boo, vote,” the social media platform Bluesky, and a “No Kings” rally all rolled into one. But it was also something specific to this band: the ultimate Texan vindication, which happened to materialize while the Chicks were getting ready backstage. The Chicks had already “won,” in that the world had come back around to them. But on Tuesday, their story became a circle. The band decided this was not a night for subtext. The run of show included the Chicks telling the story of the time they became arguably the most high-profile targets of reactionary cultural conservatism. There was a video presentation and everything, running through various Fox News hits from back in the day about what evil witches they were for opposing the Iraq War. There’s a Texan flavor to that presentation. “We were a little bit insulated at the time from the controversy back in the states,” one of the bandmates told the audience, setting up “Not Ready to Make Nice.” (I believe this was Maines talking, but here you get the uncertainty that comes with being in a dark, crowded auditorium as you drink a Lagunitas.) “When we got home, we had to go to Texas. That was a long time ago, but it was a scary proposition. I think when it happened, we were a little bit going through the stages of grief. It was shock, and then we were sad, but by the time we got home, we were mad, and we knew we had to put it into songs.” Then they played the most widely acclaimed of those songs, the one about their many conservative haters. The Chicks sprinkled standard resistance liberalism throughout the program. Maines (I think) said that until a few weeks ago, they’d been light on hope, before making a trip to the Obama Presidential Center and getting “a reminder that the country can be led by someone who’s not a deranged [something].” (I’m almost certain the last word was “lunatic,” but my notetaking got shoddy.) Later, we were told that the Chicks had an awesome time with the audience, “and our country’s had an awesome time tonight, not just because the prednisone did its job but because we won all those elections.” There were Civil Rights Movement clips playing in the background for some of the show. Technical debt shoveled aside to make large-scale AI training more resilient ML admins will be happy to learn that one bad GPU may no longer mean starting an entire training job from scratch. The latest 2.15 release of PyTorch includes a rewritten backend for Nvidia's Collective Communications Library (NCCL), designed to improve fault diagnosis and recovery when GPUs work together. This latest feature shows how its creators continue to advance the software from being a tool for researchers to being a cornerstone in production AI work. The interface to the networking library in PyTorch needed a rewrite – it was drowning in technical debt, said PyTorch core developer Tristan Rice, a Meta software engineer specializing in distributed computing, during a live YouTube livestream on Thursday. NCCL is pronounced "Nickel" and stands for the Nvidia Collective Communications Library. It provides PyTorch with a set of inter-GPU communication primitives, allowing GPUs to communicate within a single-node or across multi-node clusters, which is vital for running inference on large neural networks and for other tasks in building and maintaining LLMs. The previous PyTorch backend for NCCL, called ProcessGroupNCCL, could spawn dozens of communicators per process group, leading to intermittent deadlocks and unpredictable performance. The new version of the NCCL backend comes with a strict rule: One process group gets only one communicator during initialization. By default, this rule eliminates crosstalk between multiple communicators and all the complexities that entails. If a program requires parallelism, it will have to dispatch more process groups. The updated library also better handles stragglers by allowing for easier reconfiguration. If a GPU crashes or runs slowly, a third-party orchestrator can exclude the laggard and even assign a replacement worker. A “flight recorder” has also been added to give admins a better idea of what went wrong. Previously, if a cluster of GPUs stalled—perhaps from a timeout or deadlock—the user would just get a generic message. The new flight recorder captures all operations in a series of snapshots that can be inspected after a failure occurs. Rice noted the cleaned-up NCCL backend, also called ProcessGroupNCCL, will give developers a blank slate, making it easier to add new features. The legacy ProcessGroupNCCL is part of the 2.15 package as a rollback, but if the new NCCL backend works as expected, the older version will be removed in the 2.16 release, planned for early next year. Why, you may ask, does a machine learning framework need a specialized communications library? Like NumPy, PyTorch supports numerical computing, but it also provides tools for building and training neural networks on CPUs and GPUs. In these environments, workers must constantly exchange data such as tensors, gradients, activations, and weights. The standard TCP-based networking stack can introduce bottlenecks in large GPU clusters, so NCCL uses optimized communication paths to move data between GPUs while reducing CPU overhead. Nvidia initially released NCCL at SC15 as a general-purpose accelerator, and it was adopted by the Facebook folks when they first built PyTorch in 2016. Their goal was to build a Python-based version of the Lua-based Torch ML library. “Every major ML lab (OpenAI, DeepMind, Meta, Nvidia) uses PyTorch. It powers everything from diffusion models and LLMs to recommender systems and robotics. A sweeping bit of legislation designed to cut red tape could speed up the buildout of energy infrastructure in the US. With the dust barely settled on this week’s momentous midterms, the Senate is set to vote on the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) on November 9. It’s a plan that could finally start an overhaul of the slow, complicated process for getting energy projects approved, as part of something called permitting reform. People on both sides of the political aisle have been pushing for this type of change for some years. The key question is whether this will cut greenhouse-gas emissions from the US energy sector or push them higher still. The federal permitting system is designed to protect the environment against industrial pollution from large projects like power plants, transmission lines, and natural-gas pipelines. Today, permitting is governed by a range of laws, including the National Environmental Policy Act and the Clean Water Act. And if there’s one thing you might know about the current state of permitting in the US it’s that it’s slow and incredibly complicated. So in its 417 pages, BAAJA attempts to tighten things up. The bill has provisions that narrow the sorts of projects that need to apply for federal permits and others that enact deadlines in hopes of speeding up a process that today can take years. (There are also specific provisions about transmission planning, mining, and the rest of the kitchen sink; this thing is dense.) One thing to know about this bill is that it’s largely technology neutral: It opens the doors for the buildout of projects involving both fossil fuels and renewables. So there’s a weird mishmash of legislators and outside groups supporting it. The bill’s four sponsors in the Senate are two Republicans and two Democrats. The White House has backed this permitting reform bill, with its Council of Economic Advisors finding that it would reduce review times and have economic benefits like boosting GDP. Clean energy groups like the American Clean Power Association have largely come out in favor of the bill too, with the group’s analysis finding that it could unlock 350 gigawatts of new clean energy for the grid. Some environmental groups, however, oppose the bill, or parts of it. An assessment from the Natural Resources Defense Council says the parts about transmission could have substantial benefits for the grid, but the narrowing of environmental reviews could be damaging for public and private lands. Many in the climate world have long been calling for permitting reform, arguing that renewables stand to benefit more than fossil fuels.
