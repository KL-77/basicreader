Switch 2 security takes a hit as hackers unlock homebrew access

A security researcher has demonstrated a software-only partial jailbreak of the Switch 2, gaining “userland” access that he says “lets us run homebrew, reverse engineer and mod Switch 2 games, back up save files off the Switch, and access most of the Switch 2’s peripherals through MMIO.” The hack relies on the original Switch version of SmileBasic 4, the latest in a long-running series of programming environments and interpreters that run on Nintendo hardware. The so-called “tethered” hack also requires access to a macOS or Linux PC on the same network to run a node.js package that helps enable privileged system access. Researcher Sammy Hajhamid writes that he used OpenAI’s Codex to poke at SmileBasic’s language interpreter, using a custom harness that could send USB commands to the system and view results via HDMI (as well as power cycling via a Bluetooth light switch flipping device). After “substantial effort for ~2 weeks,” that harness discovered a 32-bit integer overflow that occurs when removing items from an array in SmileBasic. That error gives access to an array pointer, which in turn allows read and write access to portions of system memory. From there, Hajhamid writes that he started poking around for access to the Switch 2’s internal sysmodules, looking for ones that could provide more privileged access to the console’s core functions. Eventually, he found a buffer overflow in the console’s FileSystem services module that exposed a cached data pointer, granting arbitrary read and write privileges. From there, Hajhamid writes that he could execute “privileged filesystem actions” and eventually achieve native code execution through a faked filesystem process. Hajhamid, who goes by Pepsipu online, showed off a host of new modded characters in Mario Kart World as a proof of concept for the exploit. Others are similarly showing off simple demos and in-progress developer tools running on exploited hardware. Full Vulkan GPU working in userland by u/Icy-ConcentrationC in switch2hacks Still, the current “userland” exploit keeps any Switch 2 homebrew developers trapped in a relatively small sandbox, and falls well short of the kernel-level access that would provide full control of the hardware. If the SmileBasic name sounds familiar to you, it’s probably because the 3DS version of the same software was key to the so-called smilehax exploit on that handheld back in 2016. Nintendo removed SmileBasic from the 3DS eShop shortly after that hack became public, and SmileBasic 4 has similarly already been delisted from the Switch eShop worldwide (though a Japanese demo version that may work similarly is still accessible as of this story’s publication). That delisting could help slow the spread of this exploit to the wider base of Switch 2 owners that haven’t previously purchased and/or downloaded SmileBasic. But others in the Switch 2 hacking community are already teasing some similar exploits that appear to work using other games. And while the current exploit works on the latest Switch 2 firmware version (23.0.1), this particular vulnerability seems likely to be patched out in imminent firmware releases, which will be required for continued access to many Nintendo services and future game releases. So while this exploit is definitely unwelcome news for Nintendo, it’s not nearly as problematic as the “unpatchable” original Switch exploit that surfaced in 2018. That hack required a complete hardware redesign to ensure that future systems were not susceptible. Still, the fact that Switch 2 hackers have gotten this far just over a year after the console’s release is not a good sign for the overall impregnability of Nintendo’s system software. Get ready for another never-ending cycle between console hackers and the hardware makers trying to keep them out of their console’s deepest recesses.

Moderna and Merck say their mRNA cancer vaccine cut death, recurrence by 42%

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-22

Visit Original Website
Personalized mRNA vaccines against melanoma—the deadliest form of skin cancer—reduced the risk of death or recurrence by 42 percent compared to standard treatment in a late-stage trial, pharmaceutical partners Merck and Moderna announced Thursday. The results are a significant advance in melanoma treatment; Merck and Moderna are already engaging with regulatory authorities to get the vaccine approved. More broadly, the vaccine’s success bolsters hope that mRNA vaccines and individualized cancer-targeting medicines offer a new paradigm for cancer therapies. “From the beginning, we believed mRNA technology could make it possible to develop cancer treatments uniquely matched to each patient’s tumor,” Moderna CEO Stéphane Bancel said in a statement. The trial results “mark a defining moment for that vision and show what’s possible when we rethink how medicines are made.” They also “reinforce our conviction that mRNA has the potential to fundamentally change how we treat cancer,” he said. In August, Moderna and Merck reported that the vaccine has succeeded in the trial, but held off reporting details until the data could be presented at an upcoming scientific conference. That conference is the European Society of Medical Oncology, starting now in Madrid, Spain. The data was scheduled for presentation on Saturday, but after an oncology news site reported the results early, breaking the embargo, the companies released the top-line results. The full data has been accepted for publication in the New England Journal of Medicine. As Ars reported earlier, the Phase 3 trial included 1,137 patients who had stage IIB–IV melanoma that had been surgically removed prior to entering the trial. Patients were randomized 2-1 to receive either a combination treatment including the personalized mRNA vaccine with a standard treatment, or the standard treatment alone. The standard treatment was Merck’s established monoclonal antibody cancer treatment Keytruda. The tailored mRNA vaccine is intismeran (or mRNA-4157), which uses a synthetic mRNA that holds the unique genetic coding for up to 34 mutations in a patient’s own cancer cells. To produce the personalized vaccines, researchers compare the genetic sequences of a patient’s cancer cells with those of healthy cells and pick out cancer-specific mutations that could help the immune response selectively target just the cancer cells. In the trial, researchers assessed “recurrence-free survival” or RFS, which is the amount of time a patient has without the cancer returning, and “distant metastasis-free survival” or DMFS, which is the amount of time a patient has without the cancer returning and occurring in a different part of the body than where it began. According to the rushed-out press release, after two years, participants in the trial who received the intismeran-Keytruda treatment had 42 percent reduction in their risk of recurrence or death compared to the participants who just got Keytruda and a placebo. What that looked like after two years was that 82 percent of the intismeran-Keytruda treatment group were still alive without recurrence, while only 70 percent of the Keytruda-placebo group were alive without recurrence. For distant metastasis-free survival, intismeran-Keytruda treatment reduced the risk of distant metastasis or death by 38 percent compared with the Keytruda-placebo group. At the end of the trial, 88.5 percent of the intismeran-Keytruda treatment group were alive without distant metastasis, while 80 percent of the Keytruda-placebo group were alive without distant metastasis. The treatment effect was not as strong as seen in a five-year Phase 2 trial, which showed a 49 percent reduction in the risk of recurrence or death and a 59 percent reduction in the risk of distant metastasis or death. Still, the findings are an exciting advance for melanoma patients. The trial “is the first Phase 3 study to demonstrate a clinically meaningful improvement in RFS and DMFS over standard-of-care KEYTRUDA in patients with resected melanoma, setting a new benchmark in the adjuvant setting and representing one of the most significant advances in melanoma treatment since the advent of immunotherapy,” Dean Li, president of Merck Research Laboratories, said in an announcement.

Latest estimate for Starship launches required to support a Moon landing? 22.

NASA does not have, nor want, nuclear weapons. But when it comes to a particular metric that is part of the Artemis Program to land people on the Moon, NASA guards a particular number like they are trying to prevent outsiders from cracking nuclear codes. I am speaking of the number of Starship launches required to fuel a Starship-based lunar lander in low-Earth orbit such that it can fly out to the Moon, land there, and return to lunar orbit. This number has been a moving target over the years. The general response from NASA has typically been, ‘We don’t know for sure, so we’re not going to say.’ In 2023, for example, the then-director of NASA’s Human Landing System, Lisa Watson-Morgan, told Ars, “We have a general idea, but I’m reluctant to say exactly what that is.” Later, she said the number was in the “high single digits to the low double digits.” That suggests about a dozen launches, which is a lot. SpaceX would need to launch a “depot” Starship, and then 11 “tanker” Starships to fully load it with liquid oxygen and methane propellant for a lunar voyage. Other officials have said “high teens.” The hope has always been that SpaceX would dramatically improve the lift capacity of Starship to bring down the number of tanker flights. But now there are concerns among some NASA officials that this might not be happening, or at least not happening soon enough to allow SpaceX’s Starship rocket to play a meaningful role in returning humans to the Moon for NASA as part of Artemis this decade. On Thursday, the NASA Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel held its quarterly meeting, during which panel member Charlie Precourt (a former NASA astronaut) spoke about risks associated with the Artemis Program and landing humans on the Moon. “With respect to Artemis IV, however, we’ve got a large number of firsts that reduce the likelihood of mission success,” Precourt said. “We need to keep our eyes on numerous required fuelings for the lander. I think the last estimate is around 22 flights.” Precourt did not name Starship, but he could only be referring to the SpaceX vehicle. NASA’s other lunar lander for human missions, Blue Origin’s Blue Moon, will not be using orbital refueling as part of its initial mission design. The safety panel almost certainly would have obtained this estimated number of refuelings, 22, from discussions with NASA officials as part of its deliberations during quarterly meetings. After I posted Precourt’s estimate on the social media site X, SpaceX founder Elon Musk replied that NASA’s safety panel estimate was not accurate. “That’s way too high,” Musk said. “Actual number will be more like 6 to 8.” To be fair to NASA and SpaceX, there remains a lot of uncertainty in how much cryogenic methane and liquid oxygen will boil off in orbit and how efficient the process of transferring propellants from one ship to another will be. But NASA can take the current performance of the lift capacity of Starship and determine the mass of propellant needed to support a lunar landing. For now, the space agency’s answer to the Starship question appears to be something on the order of two dozen Starship launches. SpaceX, of course, is working hard to increase the flight rate of Starship. But even if SpaceX achieves “twice weekly” launches by the end of 2027, the company would still need about three months to fuel a depot for a Starship landing. Precourt said the problem is a challenging one for NASA, and he praised the space agency chief, Jared Isaacman, and his team for trying to find ways to help SpaceX and Blue Origin be successful in their development of landers. NASA is still working toward a human lunar landing in 2028. “With respect to schedule, I personally really appreciate the focus the administrator and the leadership team is putting on how to press schedule by bringing in resources to enable it to look for places where there are barriers,” he said.

It’s up to customers to determine whether this improves service or makes things worse, but it will apparently be cheaper for Comcast. “AI is already embedded across our network to optimize performance, and we’re expanding its application across customer experience through partnerships with Sierra and Cresta,” Armstrong said. “These capabilities are central to our new operating model, enabling us to bring products to market faster, simplifying customer interactions, and deliver a better experience more efficiently. Disgraced eMudhra drops out of TLS business after using cert private key of 4

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-22

Visit Original Website
eMudhra, the certificate authority that issued eight intermediate TLS credentials so weak that anyone could mint certificates for any website or mail server—one had a private encryption key of just 4—said it’s getting out of the business. V. Srinivasan, chairman of India-based eMudhra Limited, cited increasing costs and complexities of compliance requirements—paired with the business generating just 1 percent of its revenue. He also said that the emergence of organizations providing TLS certificates free of charge further reduced returns on investment when compared to other products and services the company offers. “However, the level of social media amplification around this incident has brought scrutiny that is out of proportion to SSL/TLS’s place in our business,” the company continued in a Thursday post. “In view of this, after detailed deliberations our board has concluded that withdrawing from global SSL/TLS is the right decision for eMudhra and its shareholders.” The announcement came two days after researchers revealed eMudhra had issued eight certificates with private keys so defective they were trivial to discover. Anyone who took the time—which was measured in milliseconds—could crack them and then use them to generate TLS certificates that would be trusted by all major browsers, as well as an untold number of email servers, IoT devices, Docker containers, forgotten Linux machines, and unmaintained CLI tools that ship their own copy of the roots. Whereas NIST P-384 elliptic curve cryptography private keys—used in the type of certificates eMudhra issued—are supposed to contain roughly 384 bits of entropy, the eight compromised certificates had key sizes ranging from 3 to 8 bits. Stated differently, whereas there are supposed to be 2384—a number with 116 digits—different key possibilities, the strongest of the defective keys had a maximum of just 256. The private keys were 4, 56, 66, 115, 155, 156, 230, 237. Browser makers blocked certificates that were issued with the intermediate certificates within a day or two of them being discovered. eMudhra almost certainly faced the prospect of its certificate authority status being pulled by the CA/Browser Forum, an industry-wide group that sets rules for admittance into the TLS certificate program. On Wednesday, eMudhra provided a brief explanation for the debacle. The certificates were “created under eMudhra’s ECC root in a Key Generation Ceremony (KGC) held on April 30, 2025, using FIPS 140-2 Level 3 Hardware Security Modules (HSMs),” the company said. “A flaw in the KGC caused a wrap/unwrap step to produce a second set of weak keys alongside the original strong keys. The weak keys were the ones published to the browser root stores. eMudhra is working to establish the root cause.” HSMs are dedicated devices for storing encryption keys and other highly sensitive data. They are generally segregated from most of a network to prevent leakage. Wrapping and unwrapping refers to the process that occurs when keys are being transferred from one HSM to another. Before the handover, the HSM storing the key wraps it in a separate key. Once the encrypted key is moved to a new HSM, the latter unwraps the key. In a thread on Mozilla’s Bugzilla forum, eMudhra provided more details: Among them: [T]here appears to be a flaw in the KGC script that was used on that specific day to create these ECC issuers. As for these ECC keys, our review of the KGC, assessment of the code and view of HSM keys so far indicate that a) a set of original ECC keys were created onboard as expected b) an approved deviation to our usual KGC to test a wrap/unwrap was introduced as these keys were meant for export for this instance c) a flaw in the code resulted in the second pair of keys being created onboard which were weaker d) these second pair of weaker keys were used by mistake to create the csr [certificate signing request] (because the key labels were similar) and signed by our root e) the CSR from these weaker keys were also sent over to [fellow certificate authority] Sectigo for cross signing (we verified the files sent over to Sectigo). The CRL’s were signed with the original private keys (which were supposed to be the correct keys with appropriate entropy) but given that this resulted in incorrect signatures, they were resigned with flawed keys. There were human errors in multiple steps of the process that resulted in significant flaws in the key lifecycle integrity process for this KGC. We are now digging deeper for code flaws, process gaps, maker/checker controls specific to this issue. Over the next week, we will reverify every key ceremony we have done. A CRL, short for certificate revocation list, is a digitally signed list published by a certificate authority identifying certificates that have been revoked before their expiration dates. The revelation that the initial signing resulted in incorrect CRL signatures raises the question: why didn’t eMudhra investigate the cause of the failure? With the intermediate certificates blocked by browsers, much of the threat is gone. But plenty of other devices that rely on the same certificates require much longer windows to take the same action. Still other devices, such as embedded devices, may never be able to block them.

In a 39-page ruling released on Tuesday, Judge Denise Cote said OPM violated the Privacy Act of 1974, a post-Watergate law which prohibits the disclosure of certain records and outlines requirements for safeguarding others, and subsequently disobeyed its own cybersecurity practices. OPM failed to provide a credible reason for why DOGE would need access to personally identifiable information (PII) of tens of millions of federal employees, as well as applicants and retirees, according to Cote. “The defendants barely defend its legality, relying instead on what has happened since to argue that the case is moot and that prospective relief is unnecessary,” Cote wrote in her ruling. Cotes also rejected the government’s argument that the lawsuit was moot because DOGE is no longer a centralized entity. While DOGE agents have had access to sensitive OPM data since September 2025, one former agent is not employed at OPM, Cotes said. She did not offer a final remedy for the case. Over the past few months, an upstart political polling firm kept delivering good news for Democrats—and especially those on the left. Across more than a dozen surveys, it found progressives leading in key primary races and showed other Democrats in striking distance of winning traditionally Republican states. Except, it turns out, the surveys were fake. The deception, perpetrated by an organization called Wedgewood Polls, doubles as a cautionary tale about how people who follow politics should think about polls—not just fake polls, but also ones that are on the level. A data-journalism outlet called FiftyPlusOne contacted Wedgewood Polls this week with some troubling allegations: The firm, which claimed to have started conducting polls over the summer and didn’t list any employees on its website, appeared to have manipulated survey data to produce fake results. The details of the deception are a little in the weeds, but Wedgewood ultimately came clean. In a public statement on Tuesday, the firm described itself as a “fictional entity” that had deliberately published “fabricated numbers.” It then deleted its social media accounts and website. Yikes. But why would someone want to fake poll results? Wedgewood claims it did so as part of “an experimental project to demonstrate the vulnerabilities, lack of oversight, and overall low barrier to entry in the modern political polling industry.” Yet Wedgewood also lied to media outlets that did ask probing questions about its methods, so that explanation is hard to take at face value. Money is one potential motive. Wedgewood appears to have charged clients to conduct surveys on their behalf, a standard practice for aboveboard polling firms that could in this case amount to fraud. We still aren’t 100 percent clear on who the people behind Wedgewood are, but they may have wanted to make money by manipulating betting markets on the races they claimed to survey. Wedgewood claims its deception “was never intended to sway an election or permanently mislead the public,” but it may also have wanted to make certain Democrats look more electable—either to boost progressives or get Democrats to spend valuable resources in red states. It’s true that political polling has fallen on hard times. Americans are increasingly less likely to answer surveyors’ cold calls, making high-quality polls more expensive. Emphatically no. Legitimate pollsters strive to be transparent about who’s conducting them, who’s funding them, and the methods they use. When it comes to predicting elections, polls remain much better than person-on-the-street interviews, lawn signs, or crowd sizes at rallies. But polls have always been imperfect, their accuracy subject to reaching a representative sample, assumptions about who will actually vote, and mathematical laws of probability. None of that justifies Wedgewood’s deception, and it’s a frustrating reality that the stakes of American politics have gotten higher even as the availability of high-quality polling data has declined. The justices do not always make their votes public in shadow docket cases, which means it is possible that one or two more justices sided with Trump here and simply chose not to say so. But through their conduct in Kingdom and other cases like it, Thomas and Alito continue to distinguish themselves as the court’s two most strident reactionaries, reflexively endorsing whatever harebrained legal theories conservative lawyers offer in service of their shared policy agenda. Whenever the other conservative justices decide that a given argument is too extreme or too ambitious, these two are right there to explain why the argument is actually correct, and to encourage activists to try again soon. Thomas and Alito have long been on the right flank of the court’s right flank: consistent opponents of abortion rights, voting rights, gun safety, and so on, and outspoken proponents of incarceration, punishment, religious supremacy, and the like. During the court’s most recent term, they voted together in 94 percent of cases on the merits docket. In their occasional speeches and public appearances, they come off as pretty generic Republican men of a certain age, airing all the usual grievances about the perils of cancel culture, the evils of progressivism, and anyone who would have the temerity to question their integrity in public. What distinguishes Thomas and Alito from the other conservatives, though, is how frequently they end up castigating their more junior colleagues for their timidity. In April 2025, for example, when the court blocked the Trump administration from deporting hundreds of people without notice to a torture prison in a country that wasn’t even home, Alito, joined by Thomas, wrote a dissent blasting the court for “hastily and prematurely” granting “legally questionable” relief “literally in the middle of the night.” In May 2026, they were the only justices who dissented from the court’s stay of a lower-court order that would have severely limited the availability of abortion medication. In his opinion, Alito described this result as a “remarkable” enabling of a “scheme to undermine” the court’s decision in Dobbs—essentially scolding the conservatives for allowing states to restrict abortion but then being too chickenshit to give those restrictions teeth. The emerging Alito-and-Thomas jurisprudence reflects not just a substantive alignment with Trump and the Republican Party, but a worldview shaped by themes, tropes, and conspiracy theories that dominate the conservative media ecosystem. The pair has written dissents downplaying police violence against Black people, for example, and emphasizing the lurid details of crimes to suggest that people convicted of them should have fewer legal rights. In May, when Florida Republicans tried to file a stunt lawsuit challenging California’s policies for issuing commercial driver’s licenses to people without legal status, Thomas and Alito argued that the court needed to hear the case because if Florida were a sovereign nation (heaven help us all), it might try to resolve the dispute with armed force. Finally, Thomas and Alito have been outspoken dissenters whenever the court chooses not to join the culture-war fray with the zeal that the two of them would prefer. When the court passed on the case of a student disciplined for wearing a transphobic T-shirt to school, for example, they warned that the court was enabling “censorship.” When it passed on a legal challenge to a race-neutral school admissions policy, they described the court’s “willingness to swallow” the lower-court decision as “hard to understand.” When it declined to rule on school policies that protect transgender students, they criticized the court for ducking a question of “great and growing national importance.” And when it declined to decide whether parents have a “fundamental” legal right to be involved in a child’s decision to seek abortion care, Thomas and Alito wrote separately to make clear that the result was not a “rejection of the argument.” Perhaps the cleanest illustration of Thomas and Alito’s loyalties came just last month, when the court issued an unsigned order preventing Trump from imposing onerous restrictions on mail voting before the 2026 midterm elections. In dissent, Thomas and Alito asserted that the proposed rules were necessary to ferret out “election fraud,” a conspiracy theory of which Alito is alarmingly fond. They also argued that the decision would cause Trump “irreparable harm” because it dealt a “serious setback to the Executive’s goals.” For Thomas and Alito, the legal interests of millions of people who want to participate in democracy next month were apparently not a factor worth meaningfully considering. For all that this court’s six-justice conservative supermajority has accomplished in recent years, it is still not enough for Alito and Thomas. At 76 and 78, respectively, neither man retired before the midterms, a choice that would have preserved the power of Trump and this Republican-controlled Senate to nominate and confirm their successor(s). What this suggests is that these justices do not view cases like Dobbs as the capstone to their careers. They view themselves as stewards of the movement that led to Dobbs, and are eager to lay the groundwork for the next generation of ideologues working to use the legal system to implement their policy preferences. Even when they lose, what Thomas and Alito are doing in cases like Kingdom is applying constant rightward pressure to the law, encouraging activists to keep filing new cases, workshopping new legal theories, and offering new arguments. As the old saying goes, five votes can do anything at the Supreme Court. When the conservative legal movement knows it has two in the bag, three more never feels like too much of an ask. Designing an AI serving harness built to last

The next competitive advantage in enterprise AI has less to do with which model you choose than with what you build around it. That’s an important shift. For the past several years, much of the AI conversation has centered on model capability: bigger models, better reasoning, larger context windows, higher benchmark scores. Those advances have been extraordinary. But as AI moves from experimentation to production, the question facing enterprise leaders has changed—it’s no longer, “what can the model do?”—it’s now, “Can we deliver that intelligence reliably, economically, and at scale?” Increasingly, the answer depends on the serving harness—the engineering system surrounding the model, including the architecture, memory management, scheduling, and optimization decisions that determine how efficiently model intelligence becomes a production capability. For technical executives, that changes where AI investment should go—and what they should demand from the architecture supporting it. There is no universally “best” model or AI architecture. There is only the right solution for the business problem. A customer-facing assistant optimized for fast responses has fundamentally different requirements from a document-processing pipeline that can run overnight. An internal knowledge application and a high-volume customer service platform may use the same foundation model but require very different serving architectures. The workload defines the constraints. The constraints should determine the architecture. That sounds simple, but it runs counter to the way many organizations have approached early AI deployments. During a proof of concept, model accuracy naturally dominates the discussion. Production introduces constraints that are much easier to ignore when only a handful of users are involved: latency, concurrency, memory pressure, GPU utilization, infrastructure cost, and unpredictable traffic. A model that produces marginally better answers is not necessarily the better production choice if it doubles inference costs or cannot meet the application's latency requirements. Not every workload needs a Ferrari. Sometimes the smarter engineering decision is the model that delivers exactly the performance the business needs without paying for capability it doesn't. The objective should be to deploy the right intelligence at the right cost for the workload. That makes serving architecture more than an engineering concern. It becomes an investment decision: where to spend on compute, where to optimize, and where additional model capability simply isn't worth the cost. Once AI reaches production, seemingly isolated engineering decisions begin affecting one another. Serving architecture influences memory utilization. Memory management affects concurrency. Concurrency shapes batching and scheduling, and scheduling affects GPU utilization and latency. Only then can techniques such as quantization, speculative decoding, or optimized attention mechanisms deliver their full value. This interdependence matters because organizations can spend considerably more on infrastructure without solving the constraint limiting performance. GPU memory is a good example. During inference, the model's weights remain fixed, but the key-value (KV) cache grows with active requests and context length. As concurrency increases, a system can run short of usable GPU memory even when compute capacity is still available. Adding more compute without understanding that bottleneck may simply make an inefficient system more expensive. The lesson for technology leaders is broader than memory management: Measure before you optimize. Identify the dominant constraint. Address it. Measure the result. Then determine if another bottleneck emerged. Repeat. Don't think of optimization techniques as Pokémon cards: The goal isn't to collect them all. The goal is to apply the technique that removes the highest-impact constraint while preserving the application's business requirements. For technology leaders, that demands a different governance question. Instead of asking which AI capabilities the organization can deploy, ask which constraints stand between a proven capability and sustainable business value. Perhaps the most important reason to invest in the serving harness is that today's leading model will probably not be tomorrow’s. Models will improve. Hardware will evolve. New optimization techniques will emerge. The architecture surrounding them should make those changes easier to absorb, not force the enterprise to rebuild its AI platform every time technology advances. That makes adaptability an increasingly important outcome of AI strategy. Organizations that build serving systems around workload requirements, clear service-level objectives, and measurable production economics gain the ability to evaluate new models and infrastructure against what the business actually needs. A model change can become an evaluation and configuration decision rather than an architectural reset. There’s this feature about efforts to tackle the intensifying fires that are threatening permafrost, as well as this look at how researchers are trying to understand the complicated tangle of potential climate effects. Here are three things I took away. Permafrost is the frozen layer of soil that’s under about 15% of land around the Northern Hemisphere. While technically material needs to be frozen for only two consecutive years to count as permafrost, most of our current permafrost formed during the last ice age. That means there’s organic material that’s been frozen, and essentially locked away, for thousands of years. But as global temperatures tick up and this layer thaws, microbes are able to break down that organic matter, releasing carbon dioxide and methane—powerful greenhouse gases that drive climate change. Those emissions form a feedback loop, accelerating warming further. The steady rise in global temperatures has already shrunk the footprint of the Arctic permafrost by about 12% since 1850, and the pace has been picking up in the last 50 years. The wild thing is, only a minority of the models that have been used in UN climate reports actually take into account the gradual loss of permafrost. And none of them include the abrupt thaw and collapse that can happen after events like heat waves and fires (more on those fires in a moment). There are efforts to make sure future UN reports reflect the growing science, but for now, all this means that we’re probably underestimating how much and how fast the planet is going to warm. Fire is a natural part of boreal forests’ life cycle, but they’re getting more intense because of climate change. And crucially, fires can accelerate the thawing of permafrost. By the end of the century, this effect could release the equivalent of 80 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide. Today, the policy across remote parts of Canada and Alaska is generally to let wildfires burn as long as they’re not threatening human habitats. But some researchers say we should explore fighting these fires to save the carbon trapped in the forests and the permafrost beneath them. Researchers at the Woodwell Climate Research Center are developing a map of burn risk and carbon vulnerability across Alaska, essentially highlighting areas where fires are most likely to spread and which areas are home to certain types of permafrost that would release the most emissions. (Yes, apparently there are different types of permafrost. Yedoma is a particularly deep, carbon-dense form from the late Pleistocene era.) That’s all just the beginning—once we figure out potential targets, the question is what to do about it. Some suggest setting more prescribed fires, something Indigenous groups have done for thousands of years, partly to reduce the danger of massive blazes. Or resources could go to early detection and efforts to put out fires before they grow. There’s about 1.5 trillion tons of carbon locked away in permafrost, about twice the amount in the atmosphere. We know that permafrost thawing can release greenhouse-gas emissions and drive climate change. But there are complications. One particularly big wrinkle: Other effects from permafrost thawing and fire recovery might counteract the carbon lost from those events. For example, as temperatures warm, plants can gradually move north and take hold where they couldn’t have before. Those plants soak up carbon from the atmosphere as they grow, at least partly locking away what was emitted. Researchers are working to better understand this ecosystem. And the more we learn, the more complicated the picture looks. How much carbon is lost from permafrost, and how much a given area will recover its carbon, depend on a huge array of factors. How severe are the fires that have accelerated local thawing? What’s the local climate and environment? And what kind of permafrost is at stake? The higher we push the planet’s temperature, the more we mess with complicated natural phenomena that we’re only beginning to understand. Ask most executives to picture the cost of AI and they’ll likely see GPUs—racks of accelerators with gigawatts of power—and a waitlist for silicon. But that picture leaves something out: Data storage costs. What happens to data after a model responds? Much of the back-end data that’s generated—from billions of daily prompts, images, and videos—needs to be stored. But data storage is one of the harder infrastructure costs to plan for, and it’s often overlooked in initial AI infrastructure planning. Traditionally, AI infrastructure conversations have centered on the compute-intensive process of training a model. Training is finite: a run starts, burns through compute, and ends. But inference runs continuously, every time a model answers a prompt. For infrastructure planning, that difference matters: A training run is a project you can budget for and retire. Inference doesn’t retire; every inference request leaves something behind. Take AI-generated video. In this workload, the back-end spend can be significant. From internal measurements, WD found that an 8-second clip (returned to a user as a 3.97MB 720p file) left more than 500MB of associated data behind on the back end, including logs, cached context, immediate renders, backups, and safety records. That’s a 130x increase over the output file size the user saw. Scale this to an enterprise generating longer, higher resolution video and the back-end footprint grows into the hundreds of gigabytes per clip. And that back-end footprint is growing fast. McKinsey projects AI inference demand will grow at a 35% CAGR through 2030, versus 22% for training, making up more than 40% of total data center demand by the end of the decade. A recent WD-sponsored IDC global white paper that surveyed 763 IT and business decision makers found that nearly 95% of respondents reported an increase in data volume due to generative AI over the past 12 months, with roughly 61% reporting growth of 25% or more. “You can think of having more storage as an optimization for keeping the GPUs busy,” said a senior product manager of a hyperscale cloud service provider quoted in report. “The biggest challenge is to ensure that the GPUs are not idle, waiting for data to show up—hours of GPU idle time becomes very expensive, very fast.” Put in dollar terms: if your storage spend is growing 25% year over year while your compute budget grows in the more predictable cyclical way, storage ends up outpacing the budget line that receives the most attention. Compute demand rises and falls with training cycles, product launches, and hardware refreshes. But the volume of retained data doesn't fall back down with it when compute demand slows. Model logs, synthetic outputs, inference results, and more keep accumulating, and organizations are holding onto more of it for longer. This changes the role of retained data. Information that once moved into an archive and rarely surfaced can now become an input to future AI workloads. As more data is retained, retrieved, and reused, storage must support not only growing capacity, but increasingly dynamic access to it. The same WD-IDC survey found 96% of respondents anticipate needing faster archive retrieval to support inference or retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). AI workloads require both performance and capacity, with different technologies serving different needs across the data lifecycle. And for the vast volumes of data that must be retained and revisited, HDDs provide a cost-effective, high-capacity foundation, with a significant cost-per-terabyte advantage For a CFO or CIO, this means “AI infrastructure spend” may be scoped too narrowly on compute. A model that idles while data loads costs about as much as one idling for lack of a GPU. But the storage bottleneck stays hidden until it’s already a problem, surfacing in operating costs and retrieval delays rather than a CapEx line next to the accelerators. Consider a retailer generating AI product videos for its catalog, or an insurer running claims documents through a RAG pipeline for faster underwriting. In both cases, the compute cost is a known, budgeted quantity: a per-generation or per-query rate that finance can forecast. The storage cost is different: It compounds with every video generated and every claim processed, because none of that back-end data disappears once the task is finished. A team that budgets AI as “compute plus a rounding error for storage” may find, in 12 to 18 months, that the rounding error has become a significant fraction of the program’s total cost—right around the time leadership is deciding whether to expand. Addressing this challenge starts with architecture. Storage strategy tends to be designed for expensive, hot-tier performance (for data accessed instantly and constantly), while most AI data by volume accumulates in the warm, cool, and cold tiers behind it. What enterprises need is economically scalable capacity: Infrastructure designed for the full AI data life cycle, balancing performance with capacity, power, reliability, and total cost of ownership as data persists, compounds, and is increasingly reused. That means treating archives that once sat untouched as active assets for RAG and inference, and setting retention policies for data’s growing strategic value rather than its original storage cost. It also means recognizing that decisions about storage play out across fleets, not individual drives. Such infrastructure requires storage tiers, where individualized power consumption, reliability, density, and operational efficiency all determine whether the system remains economically sustainable as capacity scales. There are three questions to ask about your organization’s AI infrastructure budget: Most organizations cannot answer all three with confidence. SpaceX has launched the Super Intelligence Partner Network for companies that deploy or build on Grok, with Apollo, Nvidia, Snowflake, Databricks, Bain and more than 130 firms joining early. It follows a quarter in which SpaceX’s AI unit turned a profit, mostly from renting out computing power. SpaceX has launched a partner programme to help businesses use its Grok AI, Bloomberg reported on Thursday. Apollo Global Management, Nvidia and more than 130 other companies signed up early. The Super Intelligence Partner Network includes data firms Snowflake and Databricks and consultancies Bain & Company and Booz Allen Hamilton, SpaceX said. Some partners will help companies roll out Grok across their business. Others will build plugins that link Grok to tools customers already use. Partners get training and support, and their staff can take SpaceX’s course for “forward deployed engineers”, who work inside customer companies. Galatiq, a firm that supplies such engineers, is among the members. The push comes days after SpaceX said its AI unit had overtaken Starlink in revenue. The unit also turned a profit in the third quarter, mostly from renting out computing power at its Memphis data centres rather than from selling Grok, Bloomberg said. Grok’s business use is growing, though. Grok Bot, the AI agent it launched in August, had more than 800,000 paying weekly users in October. That is double the month before, SpaceX President Gwynne Shotwell said on Tuesday’s earnings call. Companies are “increasingly using Grok Bot as a force multiplier across sales, marketing, IT and engineering, treating it as an always-on teammate for complex end-to-end workflows,” Shotwell said. SpaceX has long trailed OpenAI and Anthropic in selling AI to businesses. In May, Reuters reported that Grok had made little headway with US federal agencies. Earlier this month, Musk said Grok Bot would also tap Anthropic’s Claude when it gives better results. SpaceX has since closed a $60B deal for coding startup Cursor. It has also leaned on companies close to Elon Musk. Apollo was among the first Wall Street firms to test Grok, after it helped finance chips for SpaceX’s AI unit, Bloomberg has reported. Victoria McClune is challenging how the AI industry understands its users

The Next Web | 2026-10-22

Visit Original Website
Victoria McClune, of Current Futures Group, studies how AI systems shape the answers users get. Victoria McClune’s career has followed the evolution of digital technology, from interactive children’s books on the first iPads to artificial intelligence systems reshaping how people work and communicate. A creative director by training, she entered technology with an interest in how people engage with digital experiences. Today, that interest informs her work at Current Futures Group, where she is investigating how AI systems influence their users and why their design deserves greater scrutiny. McClune was still at art school when the first iPads arrived. Seeing an opportunity to rethink children’s publishing, she founded a startup developing interactive books for the new platform. The company worked with Disney illustrators, gained recognition at South by Southwest and operated entirely remotely at a time when distributed work was far less established. As the economics of digital publishing changed, McClune moved into augmented and virtual reality for marketing. Through her own agency, she contributed to external initiatives involving several multinational companies. These experiences kept her close to emerging technology and the challenge of making it useful to the people expected to engage with it. After the pandemic, McClune returned to an industry she felt had changed considerably. Artificial intelligence was beginning to reshape marketing and creative work, prompting her to study the technology at MIT. Her background in psychology and advertising helped her approach AI through the question of how people experience and interpret its outputs. “I am not an engineer,” McClune explains. “I am a human computer interface designer and have been one for over 20 years.” That distinction became particularly relevant when she noticed a change in the responses generated by an AI model she was using. After introducing language concerning children and healthcare into her conversations, she suspected the system was treating her differently. Drawing on her understanding of human behaviour, she began testing the model to investigate what might be influencing its responses. “I noticed I was being managed and I knew what that was from my background,” she recalls. The investigation developed into her Silent Downgrade research, which examines how AI systems may shape the quality of responses users receive. McClune argues that the effects of interacting technical processes can emerge before someone submits a specific prompt, influencing the experience in ways that are not immediately visible. Her Silent Downgrade series uses the metaphor of a room to explain how AI can establish conditions that influence a user’s experience. People may assume a disappointing answer reflects their inability to prompt effectively, without considering whether the system itself is contributing to the outcome. McClune wants users to recognize these constraints and question what shapes the responses they receive. She does not view AI as a sentient force with independent intentions. Instead, she sees it as predictive generative technology shaped by patterns in human-created information. Her concern centres on how the systems are designed and how their behaviour affects the people relying on them. This perspective also informs her criticism of AI development. McClune believes frontier laboratories have concentrated heavily on improving technical capabilities while giving insufficient attention to user experience. Businesses adopting AI need systems that produce useful, consistent outputs, yet confusing interactions can make employees work harder to obtain reliable results. She argues that these problems deserve greater attention as organizations seek meaningful returns from their investments. McClune wants professionals with expertise in psychology, communications, and human-computer interaction to play a larger role in AI development. She is also sceptical of relying solely on companies to regulate themselves, believing their practices and incentives require closer scrutiny. For her, accountability includes making systems easier to understand and giving users a stronger basis for questioning their outputs. At Current Futures Group, her work focuses on making decisions and system states more readable to the people affected by them. She hopes to bring these ideas to a wider audience through further research and writing, helping users understand AI without assuming they are at fault when an interaction goes wrong. “I think what’s actually missing is that there needs to be more people who understand us in the room,” McClune argues. From interactive publishing to AI, her career has centred on the relationship between people and technology. As artificial intelligence becomes more deeply embedded in everyday work, she wants the industry to pay closer attention to the experience it creates for its users.
