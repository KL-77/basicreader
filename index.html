Doctors pull knife from man's eye that went through his brain—and he's fine now

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-06

Visit Original Website
A man in his 20s showed up to a UK hospital with the hilt of a knife sticking out of his face. The 15 mm-wide blade hidden within went completely through his right eye and through his brain, with the tip of the blade hitting the top of his skull. Despite the ghastly injury, the man was alive and conscious when he arrived. He could still talk and move all four limbs. His doctors described him as being in a state of agitation. On the Glasgow Coma Scale, he scored a 12 (E3 V4 M5), indicating a “moderate” brain injury, just one point above “mild.” He could open his left eye when told, but otherwise kept it closed (E3 score); he could talk and answer questions, but was confused (V4); and he intentionally moved away when pressure was applied to his body (M5). In a report published Friday in BMJ Case Report, his doctors said they were concerned that in his confused, agitated state he may try to pull the knife out on his own. So, they swiftly sedated and intubated him. They put him on prophylactic treatments for bacterial infections, including tetanus. Then they used images to see what was going on in his head. The blade was entirely through the right eye socket and eyeball. It traversed the left frontal and temporal lobes of his brain, passing through in front of—and without apparently injuring—the corpus callosum, which connects the two sides of the brain and is full of nerve fibers. The blade also missed the left internal capsule, which holds another bundle of nerve fibers. In terms of damage to major blood vessels, doctors found that the blade was close to the left internal carotid artery, a major blood vessel, but didn’t seem to have damaged it. Doctors did find, however, that a branch of the middle cerebral artery looked to be indented by the knife. After consulting with various medical specialties, the man’s doctors moved to emergency neurosurgery. They opted to remove part of his skull to directly see into the brain, but used a conservative “pull-and-see” method to slide the blade out rather than invasively excavate it. After it was out, the doctors saw no signs of significant hemorrhaging. The brain wound was cleaned and drained before surgeons patched up his skull. His right eye, however, couldn’t be saved. Given its extensive damage, it was removed. The man then moved to intensive care, where he recovered rapidly. A day after surgery, doctors were able to remove his breathing tube. He developed some minor right-side weakness, but it resolved two days later. Scans of his brain showed steady improvement—a little bleeding and swelling, and there was some air in his brain tissue from where the knife had been. But otherwise, it appeared to be healing. He developed no infections and had no seizures. Despite the “dramatic intracranial trajectory,” his language function was preserved, and he had no loss of movement or coordination, they reported. He was discharged from the hospital and at follow-up appointments remained “neurologically well,” with ongoing eye and mental health care. As for how the injury happened, his doctors still don’t know. “The mechanism is unclear,” they wrote. However, they noted he had a history of depression. The doctors reflected on how lucky the man was to have survived and to have such a full recovery. Such injuries that go through the eye and the brain are rare. When they do occur, they’re usually from “high-velocity mechanisms” such as gunshots or other ballistic injuries. Knives, rods, and nails fit into the rarer “low-velocity mechanisms. “Such “low-velocity penetrating brain injuries carry substantial morbidity and mortality because of hemorrhage, vascular injury, abscess, meningitis, cerebrospinal fluid leak, seizures and neurological deficit,” they wrote. But such fortunate recoveries aren’t unheard of; they cited reports of other remarkably unexpected recoveries from seemingly devastating brain injuries. That includes the famous case of Phineas Gage, a railroad foreman who survived an 1848 accident that sent a large iron rod directly through his head. The rod entered below his left cheekbone and exited the top of his skull. (Although he survived and recovered, Gage’s personality was said to have changed after the accident. The doctors in the knife-wound case did not mention any such personality changes.) “Echoing classic and contemporary examples of severe frontal lobe penetration with unexpectedly preserved neurological function, including Phineas Gage and a reported nail-gun injury, this case highlights that outcome may depend less on external severity than on precise trajectory relative to critical vascular, cortical and subcortical structures,” the doctors concluded.

Ships loiter near subsea cables amid Iran's warnings to Trump

Several tanker ships linked to Iran have been loitering over subsea Internet cables in the Gulf of Oman near the Strait of Hormuz—a possible indication that the Iranian government is preparing to attack such undersea infrastructure in response to any renewed US and Israeli strikes. The movements of three sanctioned tankers and an Iranian fishing boat have been tracked near major undersea cables by the maritime intelligence firm Windward in October, according to the Financial Times. Six active cables currently cross through the contested Strait of Hormuz, linking the Middle East to Europe, Africa and Asia, with two more cables having segments under construction in the region. Four countries, including Iraq, Kuwait, Bahrain and Qatar, depend entirely upon such subsea telecommunications connections running through the Strait of Hormuz chokepoint to link with the outside world. But other countries also depend heavily on the region’s undersea cable network—one third of India’s westbound Internet traffic goes through the Persian Gulf and Strait of Hormuz. One cable under construction, the 2Africa cable, is owned by a tech consortium that includes the US company Meta. That was one of the major cable corridors visited by the drifting Iran-linked ships. Windward used both ship-tracking data and satellite images to track the vessels as they slowed down and looped around near multiple cables, with some ships then “going dark” by switching off their automatic identification system (AIS) transponders that normally broadcast their location and other information. Such movement and behavioral patterns suggest the Iran-linked ships may be “mapping the cable routes, or placing charges or mines on them while dark,” Windward told the Financial Times. Windward highlighted the examples of two sanctioned tankers linked to Iran that spent days drifting “directly over regional cable corridors… both with no commercial reason to stop” in a blog post on October 26. Both ships carried no commercial cargo. Since the US and Israel first launched a major war with Iran in February 2026, Iran’s response beyond targeting US military bases, ships and aircraft has included attacking commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz and hitting oil refineries and other energy infrastructure along with airports in various Gulf countries. Iran has also made a point of threatening or attacking US Big Tech infrastructure in the Middle East, including launching multiple drone strikes on Amazon data centers. In May 2026, a spokesperson for Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps claimed Iran would “impose fees on Internet cables” belonging to US tech companies and other owners. But more recently, multiple Iranian officials and commanders of Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) have warned that they could directly attack the undersea cables running through the Strait of Hormuz. This came after President Donald Trump repeatedly threatened to renew major US military strikes on Iran and warned he could “annihilate” the country during a speech at the United Nations in New York on September 22. “Seven major cables run through the Strait of Hormuz, supporting global Internet operations and commercial data transmission,” one IRGC spokesperson said on October 20. “We remind the enemy of this.” The latest phase of the war has involved more limited US military strikes against Iranian targets, even as Trump has alternated between threats and touting the possibility of a diplomatic breakthrough. Meanwhile, Iran has increased drone strikes and other attacks on commercial ships to exert its own pressure on the United States, with October marking the highest number of ship attacks since the war began. Much damage has already been done. The limited fleet of cable-laying ships used for installation and repair has not been able to venture safely into the region since the war began, which forced the Meta-backed 2Africa cable to pause its planned expansion. The war has also compelled US tech companies and Gulf countries to accelerate efforts to develop alternative cable routes bypassing the Strait of Hormuz. The Middle East is not the only part of the world where threats to the Internet’s undersea backbone have intensified. Subsea infrastructure is increasingly at risk from sabotage and gray-zone warfare from the Baltic Sea to the Pacific Ocean.

Re-releases of Wing Commander, Ultima titles hit Steam and GOG

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-06

Many people are not big fans of game publisher EA right now for good reason, but for those who remember a better time in the 1990s, there’s a little good news. Twenty-five classic games from that EA era have been lightly remastered and updated, then placed for sale on Steam and GOG, including several Wing Commander games and nearly the entire Ultima series. Below is the full list of remastered or re-released games: In a lengthy blog post published this week, Jim Vessella, a senior producer at EA, described the efforts that went into remastering Wing Commander I-IV and Prophecy, Privateer 2, and Road Rash. It’s a long, detailed story that’s worth reading yourself, but here’s a top-level summary. After working on the mostly widely acclaimed Command & Conquer: Remastered Collection in 2019, Vessella writes that he learned about and visited a room at EA’s Redwood Shores campus that contained a large archive of poorly labeled or sorted physical media from ages past. In his free time, and with the help of others, he began cataloging this archive and discovered, among other things, that it contained virtually the entirety of the original masters of Wing Commander III and Wing Commander IV‘s full-motion-video cutscenes. The story that ensues involves building a small team of contractors, poring through the footage 17 hours a day on swing shifts to label and digitize it, and then coordinating with several key community members—particularly Matt Wells, who has made community enhancement patches for some of the Wing Commander games—to put together remasters of those games. The flagship offering of those remasters is much, much higher-quality full-motion video sequences than have ever been available before, but other enhancements include 60 frames-per-second gameplay, a plethora of newly available .INI tweaks, and full controller support (though looking at the Steam forums for the games, some folks are having trouble with said controller support). For Privateer 2—the little-remembered sequel to Wing Commander: Privateer—the crew couldn’t find the original, high-quality remasters of the FMV scenes, and ended up getting in touch with the game’s original cinematics director, Steve Hilliker, who had the tapes in his attic. Hilliker had always wanted to take a new color-grading pass, and he did just that, while also remastering and upscaling all of the cutscenes. Similar updates were given to Wing Commander Prophecy. The blog post also details GOG’s involvement in restoring and remastering Prophecy and Road Rash. For the latter, EA re-licensed the entire original soundtrack for re-release. The e collection is on sale on GOG and Steam right now; previous owners of the old versions of the games on GOG and the EA storefront get these remasters for free. None of the games cost more than $5 each. One good story doesn’t undo a lot of bad news and questionable decisions from EA, but this shows that a few passionate people can do something remarkable for game preservation, even if some of the work was done on a volunteer basis outside normal working hours. (EA committed some resources to the restorations as the project progressed, though.) While the remasters aren’t perfect, the gaming industry could use news like this. It’s worth reading the full story in the blog post for a journey down memory lane and a look at how involved a restoration process like this can be.

Blockchain analysis firm Chainalysis said that hackers have obtained sensitive personal information—including names, usernames, password hashes, and live tokens—for all of its users following a breach of its network security. The accomplishment could be a major coup for the attackers. Both Chainalysis and public sources have revealed a long list of high-profile customers, including government agencies such as the FBI, US Immigration and Customs Enforcement agency, and the US Air Force, as well as private companies such as Microsoft, Coinbase, and Barclays. It’s unclear if the data theft exposed customers to additional threats. Last Friday, Chainalysis said it had been the target of a cybersecurity incident in October that was similar to the hacks of other platforms during the same month. The external actor used AI agent-assisted offensive techniques to gain unauthorized access to Chainalysis systems and internal tools. An email the company sent to affected customers said some “non-sensitive customer data, such as user IDs, full names, email addresses, and organization role labels for users provisioned on our platform were accessed.” It’s not clear why the company considered such information “non-sensitive.” In a Wednesday update, the company said that part of the “ongoing multifront cyberattack” involved an “on-path attack” that exposed a subset of customer passwords, and that the attack vector had been identified and fixed. Also known as an adversary-in-the-middle attack, on-path attacks happen when a hacker gains access to a piece of infrastructure sending traffic back and forth between two parties. Chainalysis has yet to say where the infrastructure compromise occurred and if the data obtained from it was TLS encrypted. The company said later that day that an investigation two days earlier (on Monday) had revealed that “certain customer information including names, usernames, password hashes and live tokens for all Chainalysis users were exposed to a malicious actor.” It also said it had detected “abnormal activity” and, in response, took down its login page and know-your-transaction service, which monitors threats to cryptocurrency holdings in real time. The company has also removed access to its Reactor and Data Solutions services. In an update on Friday, Chainalysis said it had begun phasing in customer access to KYT and Address Screening and expected more customers to follow. It asked customers to submit their trusted IP addresses so they can be added to an allow list and to prepare to reset credentials once they regain access. The credential rotation is mandatory. The company says its investigation has yet to reveal any evidence that any investigation data has been accessed or Chainalysis attribution data has been manipulated. Several important questions remain unanswered. As noted, the source that made the on-path attack possible is still unknown. It’s not clear how useful the information obtained in such an attack would be unless the attackers also had the ability to decrypt the traffic they were monitoring. If that were the case, the attackers might have gained broad control of the company’s infrastructure. Chainalysis also didn’t explain why it waited until Wednesday to disclose the data theft of all customers’ personal information when its investigation discovered the exposure two days earlier. Further, the company has provided no details about the AI agents used in the attack and how they worked. A Chainalysis representative said the company wasn’t commenting beyond the statements in its blog post.

Earth's churning mantle may have created the Great Unconformity

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-06

Visit Original Website
In the Grand Canyon, there is a line in the rock where time seems to have gone missing. Ancient crystalline rocks mark its bottom, while sandstones laid down as life started to diversify during the Cambrian explosion mark the top. “It’s called the Great Unconformity, and it’s a gap in the geologic record,” says Nicolas Flament, a geoscientist at the University of Wollongong, Australia. “This gap can be anywhere between 200 million years, which is not that impressive, to more than a billion years.” In a recent Nature study, Flament and his colleague Santosh Dhungana argue that this gap was carved with help from deep within the planet. According to their models, slow currents of hot rock in Earth’s mantle pushed entire continents up for hundreds of millions of years, letting glaciers grind them down and then let them sink, allowing fresh sediments to bury the scar. The largest hole in the rock record might be a fingerprint of processes happening deep in the planet’s interior. The oldest sediments resting on the Great Unconformity in North America are between 635 and 482 million years old. Geologists have traced it across most of North America and parts of northern Europe and northern China. “Something seems to have happened, in particular in North America,” Flament says. The leading explanation was glaciation. Between roughly 720 and 635 million years ago, glaciers reached the tropics, and some scientists think the entire planet froze over, a scenario called Snowball Earth. “Ice sheets are very good at grinding, scouring, scraping the surface,” Flament explains. He said that glaciers are about 10 times more effective than rivers at eroding rock away. But if the whole planet froze, the Great Unconformity should be found everywhere. And it’s not. Other explanations included rifting, pulling and stretching of Earth’s crust, and a global drop in sea levels. But rifting acts mostly on the edges of continents, and a sea level change should have affected all continents alike. “There’s no doubt that there was extensive glaciation. There’s no doubt that there have been sea-level changes throughout time. The question is, was that enough to put the Great Unconformity puzzle together?” Flament says. A fourth idea held that the mantle itself was moving continents up and down, a process called dynamic topography. “It had been proposed, but it hadn’t been quantitatively tested,” Flament says. So, his team set out to do so. From roughly 100 to 200 kilometers beneath the surface down to nearly 3,000 kilometers, Earth’s mantle slowly convects. “An analogy would be a lava lamp, where some stuff comes up and some stuff comes down,” Flament explains. “Cold rock sinks and hot rock rises.” The mantle is solid, yet a piece of rock can slowly travel from the surface to the deep Earth over some 200 million years. That motion tugs on the surface. Where cold slabs of ocean floor sink into the mantle at subduction zones, the ground gets pulled down; where hot mantle rises, it gets pushed up. The amplitude of this up and down motion is still debated, though. “The short of it is several hundreds of meters, and it’s still to be worked out exactly what that [precise value] is,” Flament says. Dhungana and Flament ran six simulations of mantle convection going back 1 billion years, driven by reconstructions of how tectonic plates moved over that time. Then they grouped regions where the ground rose and fell in similar ways to find where the unconformity should have formed, and where it should have survived to the present. Flament, however, says the starting configuration of these simulations remains uncertain. Magnetic records in ancient rocks reveal how far continents were from the equator, but not their longitude. “It’s possible that some continents may be in the wrong hemisphere,” Flament admits. The ancient ocean floor, long since recycled into the mantle, had to be invented entirely from scratch in the models. Still, the simulations produced a consistent story. Laurentia, the ancient core of North America, had little or no subduction along its margins between about 930 and 510 million years ago, much like Africa today, so it sat above some rising mantle and was pushed up. Five of the six models showed this pattern of Laurentia seeing long uplift followed by sinking in the Cambrian. For northern Europe and North China, this pattern showed up in four. But the uplift alone, Flament’s team concluded, was too gentle to strip away enough rock to create the Great Unconformity. “The uplift could create a tilt for the whole continent, but it’s not great at creating a steep slope,” Flament says. The missing piece of the puzzle, he argues, were glaciers that ground through rock even on low ground and gentle slopes. The models show the uplift was still building when Snowball Earth hit. “This gentle uplift that’s continuous, and then that glacial erosion that comes on top, work together,” Flament explains. “They’re not coupled. They just happen to coincide in time.” When the continents sank in the Cambrian, seas flooded in and buried the eroded surface. The models made by Flament’s team also explain why the gap is so obvious in North America but hard to find elsewhere. Compared with records of sediment preserved on each continent, North America and Eurasia show one long rise followed by one long fall, which the researchers interpret as the ideal recipe for the Great Unconformity. South America and Australia, on the other hand, went through two cycles. “This would form potentially two unconformities, and then the Great Unconformity would be more difficult to see,” Flament says. Africa was a different case altogether—hemmed in by mountain belts at the time, its erosion and sediment were shaped by the mountains rather than by the mantle beneath. But if the mantle goes through such up and down cycles on a regular basis, the remaining question was why we can see only one Great Unconformity, and not many. The team says the Great Unconformity is unique in the geological record because of a combination of a rise-and-fall cycle lasting over 900 million years, a near-global glaciation that happened at the right moment, and rock records that get harder to read farther back in time. Flament suspects there may be an older candidate, tied to a glaciation 2.4 billion years ago, but rocks that old lack the fossils that make younger sediments easier to date. “Having life in sediments makes it a lot easier to have a sense of time,” he says. His work, however, may help explain what happens right after the Great Unconformity formed. The intense erosion that carved the Great Unconformity washed rock material into the oceans, possibly fertilizing them just before the Cambrian explosion, when calcium became abundant in seawater. “That’s when life learns to mobilize calcium and create skeletons and shells,” Flament says. He admits, though, that mantle dynamics likely wasn’t the only process that enabled the Cambrian explosion. “I think it would be misleading to say the mantle set the pace here. But I think it was one of the forces that worked together.” Nature, 2026.  DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-11156-5

A shipment of Nvidia hardware valued at $11.4 million vanished in transport between Oakland and Texas over the summer, and more than two months later, police have announced no arrests or recovery of the cargo. As reported by The Mercury News, six pallets of unknown Nvidia equipment left Oakland on August 29 on a truck bound for Fort Worth, Texas. The hardware was reportedly picked up as scheduled by a truck bearing the logo of a food transportation company before vanishing off the grid. The theft wasn’t noticed until early September, when the buyers realized that the shipment had never been received. Police attempting to trace the truck’s route found it had made several multi-hour stops after picking up the hardware before losing the truck somewhere in San Bernardino County, California. Oakland police confirmed that there was a grand theft report filed on September 13 and that the investigation remains ongoing. Authorities have not said what Nvidia hardware was stolen or exactly how the theft was carried out. The theft in Oakland is the latest in a wave of increasingly bold hardware heists. As Ars’ Ashley Belanger reported recently, thieves have made off with more than $100 million in similar shipments since June. According to investigators cited by NBC News, the criminals have shown planning and sophistication by employing techniques like GPS location spoofing and even arranging collisions with police escort vehicles. Stolen components can be stripped down and sold in gray-market transactions or shipped overseas where access to US tech hardware may be restricted. Nvidia’s hardware has been a specific target. In 2025, police investigated burglaries of Nvidia hardware, valued at more than $7.5 million, around Fremont and Union City, California. As reported by The Mercury News, police believe the thefts were carried out by a group that obtained vehicles for the heist in Southern California before traveling north to the Bay Area. Professor Olaf Groth of UC Berkeley’s Haas School of Business told ABC News that “if a chip costs about $550,000, then they go for about $1 million or more abroad.” Those margins, he said, may be incentivizing criminals: “Because cargoes are getting bigger. The heists are getting more complex and that will point toward a professionalization of the teams that are hijacking these.” Not every would-be heist crew gets it right, though. A theft in September, reported by Wired, saw two trailers marked with Nvidia and PlusAI logos stolen from outside of a warehouse facility in California. The trailers were recovered by police nearby, presumably after the criminals cut the locks to reveal they were each loaded with 20,000 pounds of sand. PlusAI is a company working on autonomous trucking solutions with Nvidia, and the sand was in the trailers to simulate freight loads.

The midterms didn’t stop the AI buildout. They simply repriced it—and OpenAI is already promising to pay up

The midterms didn’t stop the AI buildout. They simply repriced it—and OpenAI is already promising to pay up

Fortune | FORTUNE | 2026-11-06

Visit Original Website
Two days after what appears to be voters rejecting data centers in town after town, OpenAI went shopping for something it can’t build: permission. On Thursday, the company published a National Community Compact, in which the company pledged to pay for the electricity and grid infrastructure of the data center campuses it owns and operates. It also pledged to post financial guarantees before major spending begins, so other utility customers aren’t stuck with the bill if a project is delayed or canceled. The company told Fortune this has been months in the making and is something they’ve been pledging in their data center campuses already—the compact is simply the first time OpenAI put in writing a number of pledges it has already undertaken. Mining and energy companies have a name for what OpenAI is after: a social license. (If you’re interested, you can read my grad school capstone about social license in Peru, which has since been enacted into law). These are a type of Community Partnership Agreements (CPAs) that entails getting a community’s ongoing consent to operate: No zoning board grants it, and no tax abatement buys it. Instead, companies earn it by showing the people next door that a project leaves them better off—or at least no worse off. Lose it, and permits stall, lawsuits pile up, and local officials find reasons to say no. So following the Democratic wave on Tuesday, the AI industry found out how little of that license it had left. And distinctly, the midterm turnout wasn’t a cry from voters to stop the buildout, but simply to put a new price tag on it, and OpenAI is the first major AI company to agree to pay it since the votes were counted. Data centers were on the ballot in roughly 40 localities across the country. By Wednesday morning, about 28 measures had passed in 25 localities, all of them opposing data centers. Chief among them were the 18 municipalities in Ohio, which already has 125 data center moratoriums. Sunbury, Ohio, banned data centers over 25 megawatts, 79% to 21%, after Amazon proposed a $2 billion campus there. In Trenton, Ohio, where Amazon Web Services is already building a 250-megawatt data center, a ban passed 80% to 20%. In Hubbard, Ohio, a ban won 76% to 24%, and the mayor has since said a proposed 300-megawatt project is dead. But voters weren’t saying no to data centers: The ballot measures were to place limits on data centers, rather than full-out bans. In Perry, Ohio, 87% of voters required hyperscale developers seeking tax breaks to post financial guarantees and meet noise limits, the same safeguard OpenAI offered two days later. Grove City, Ohio, banned nothing. By 55% to 45%, it required a public vote on any project over 50 acres, 20 megawatts or 500,000 gallons of water a day. Most of the bans set a size threshold, meaning a modest server farm can still come to town, but a gigawatt campus can’t. This wasn’t limited just to the Democratic party: Former Ohio gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy pledged a moratorium on construction until the legislature acted, and Texas Gov. Greg Abbott had already paused new grid connections for data centers in August. The common complaint was the electric bill: More than 70% of Americans polled said they’d oppose a data center planned near them, with rising power prices the top reason. Corporations largely have benefitted from local/municipal/state/federal praxis: Get a tax credit for coming here to my jurisdiction. The examples are endless: Think of the $33 billion taxpayers have spent on getting a sports stadium built, of which they rarely see the full amount brought back into the community. Perhaps the most famous example of tax incentives proving futile is none other than Amazon’s HQ2 debacle, in which Newark, NJ, offered $7 billion in tax incentives to Amazon to locate their second headquarters there, and New York City offered “only” $3 billion. Amazon was always going to pick Queens, but the company was smart enough to play more than two dozen jurisdictions across the country against each other to gain the biggest tax reward. So the AI buildout is no different. For most of the boom, the industry’s offer to communities was simple: We bring tax revenue and construction jobs, you bring the tax breaks. But Tuesday showed that offer was no longer on the table. Now, hyperscalers are making a new offer, which looks a lot like a social license agreement. In January, Microsoft launched a “Community-First AI Infrastructure” plan, pledging to pay its own way on electricity, pay full local property taxes without asking for tax breaks, replenish more water than it uses, and offer free AI training in local schools and community colleges. A week later, OpenAI said every Stargate campus would get its own community plan. By this fall, Google, Microsoft, Meta, Oracle, xAI, OpenAI, and Amazon had signed a non-binding White House pledge to build or buy the power their facilities need. That’s repricing in action. Instead of asking towns for discounts, the industry now offers to cover the grid upgrades, the water, the training and, in OpenAI’s case, a guarantee against walking away. Previously, every one of those costs used to fall on the community. Bans don’t make demand for computing power disappear. They move it. Harwood, the lone town that voted the industry’s way, sits near a data center that Applied Digital is already building. The projects pushed out of Sunbury and Hubbard will look for counties that still say yes, at whatever price those counties set. While it is absurd to think of some newly-elected members of the Democratic wave advocating to put “a pause on advanced AI development, regulation of this out of control industry, and a ban on artificial superintelligence,” as Congresswoman Yassamin Ansari (D-Ariz.) posted on Election Day, it isn’t all that obscene to think that voters came out to rethink how the AI buildout is working in their communities and for them. Elon Musk said Friday Tesla would be phasing out the production of the Cybertruck, the boxy-looking electric pickup he once pitched as the future of his EV maker. In academic discourse, use of the correct name is an important means of establishing professional credibility. For example, if within the fields of computer science and information science, a specific term of art, such as artificial intelligence, has gained widespread acceptance, the use of an alternative term—a term, like superintelligence, not widely accepted within the discipline—would seriously undermine the credibility of academic work (and the author’s more general standing within the field). President Donald Trump has notoriously attempted to rechristen the Gulf of Mexico as the “Gulf of America.” So too has he attempted to rename Lake Ontario to “Lake America,” and he is threatening to fashion new MAGA names for an ocean, a major river, and the state of New Mexico. Trump also recently signed an executive order requiring all federal government programs and communications to use the terms superintelligence and S.I. rather than the broadly accepted nomenclature artificial intelligence and A.I. One might shrug all of this off and say, “Well, so what? It’s a free country, and all of us can still use the generally accepted names of places (Gulf of Mexico) and things (A.I.).” Unfortunately, after Oct. 1, 2026, this is no longer the case for academics at state-sponsored colleges and universities in Alabama. H.B. 2 officially bans the use of the phrase Gulf of Mexico by any and all state employees. As a professor at the University of Alabama School of Law, I find this appalling. This bizarre law provides that “all state and local entities and their employees shall be required to … [r]efer to the body of water that borders the coast of Alabama as the ‘Gulf of America,’ ” including “in all newly created maps, documents, educational materials, websites, official communications, and other resources.” Section 3 contains a limited exception to this general rule: “Nothing in this section shall prohibit a public K–12 school or public institution of higher education from using the term ‘Gulf of Mexico’ within a larger course of academic instruction when reasonable for historical purposes.” Imagine, for a moment, a marine biologist at Auburn University preparing to present research at a major global academic conference on the marine mammals of the “Gulf of America”? Or a geologist at the University of Alabama writing about oil deposits in the “Gulf of America” in a scholarly research paper? H.B. 2 plainly renders unlawful the use of the term Gulf of Mexico in any and all professional endeavors, including teaching and scholarship, save for using the generally accepted name within national and international academic discourse solely for “historical purposes.” This is bad enough, of course, but future legislation could compel Alabama’s public college and university professors to abjure using Lake Ontario or artificial intelligence in both their classrooms and academic publications. Moreover, Trump’s “superintelligence” executive order significantly raises the stakes for compelled academic speech and does so on a nationwide basis. The federal government sponsors billions of dollars of academic research annually. Now government-funded research into A.I. will presumably require grant applicants to use the term superintelligence in both their grant applications and subsequent published research. (It bears noting that the leadership of Facebook and X, Mark Zuckerberg and Elon Musk, dumped artificial intelligence in favor of superintelligence in recent quarterly earnings calls with investors.) Efforts to control speech in Alabama’s educational institutions constitute just one part of a larger national effort to standardize speech and, by necessity, thought. Happily, however, the First Amendment not only protects the use of particular words but also extends to a person’s choice of language. Government efforts to compel speech are facially inconsistent with the First Amendment. Cohen v. California, which blocked California’s effort to criminalize the use of the word fuck in public, clearly establishes this rule. Writing for the Cohen majority, Justice John Marshall Harlan explained, “We cannot indulge the facile assumption that one can forbid particular words without also running a substantial risk of suppressing ideas in the process.” The power to control words involves the power to control thought, and “governments might soon seize upon the censorship of particular words as a convenient guise for banning the expression of unpopular views.” Cohen squarely forecloses government efforts to ban the use of particular words—presumably including Gulf of Mexico, Lake Ontario, and artificial intelligence. Under the First Amendment, neither the federal nor state government possesses the constitutional authority to put words in the mouths of citizens. Instead, the First Amendment “is designed and intended to remove governmental restraints from the arena of public discussion, putting the decision as to what views shall be voiced largely into the hands of each of us,” and this holds true “because governmental officials cannot make principled distinctions in this area.” What is true generally for all Americans has special force in the context of education. As Justice Felix Frankfurter explained, in his concurring opinion in Wieman v. Updegraff, “to regard teachers—in our entire educational system, from the primary grades to the university—as the priests of our democracy is therefore not to indulge in hyperbole.” But teachers, at all levels of our educational system, “cannot carry out their noble task if the conditions for the practice of a responsible and critical mind are denied to them.” Accordingly, “they must have the freedom of responsible inquiry, by thought and action, into the meaning of social and economic ideas, into the checkered history of social and economic dogma … to assure which the freedoms of thought, of speech, of inquiry, of worship are guaranteed by the Constitution of the United States against infraction by National or State government.” Forcing computer scientists to use the term superintelligence in federally sponsored research or environmental scientists to use Gulf of America in their teaching and scholarship squarely violates both general free speech principles and the more specific First Amendment protection afforded to academic freedom. Unlike the GOP, which has been in thrall to Trumpism for a decade, and despite Americans’ growing disenchantment with political parties in general, Democrats still behave the way an effective political party should: recalibrating after defeats, adjusting messages as the political environment shifts, backing quality candidates, and tolerating a broad spectrum of ideologies and voices under one umbrella. Parties exist to win elections, and on that score this donkey remains alive and kicking. I can hear the objections already: Didn’t Democrats fumble two presidential races against Trump? Democrats have proved that repeatedly during the Trump era, but particularly over the past two years. In 2020, Biden ran on restoring “the soul of America” and barely beat Trump. The latest 2.15 release of PyTorch includes a rewritten backend for Nvidia's Collective Communications Library (NCCL), designed to improve fault diagnosis and recovery when GPUs work together. This latest feature shows how its creators continue to advance the software from being a tool for researchers to being a cornerstone in production AI work. The interface to the networking library in PyTorch needed a rewrite – it was drowning in technical debt, said PyTorch core developer Tristan Rice, a Meta software engineer specializing in distributed computing, during a live YouTube livestream on Thursday. NCCL is pronounced "Nickel" and stands for the Nvidia Collective Communications Library. It provides PyTorch with a set of inter-GPU communication primitives, allowing GPUs to communicate within a single-node or across multi-node clusters, which is vital for running inference on large neural networks and for other tasks in building and maintaining LLMs. The previous PyTorch backend for NCCL, called ProcessGroupNCCL, could spawn dozens of communicators per process group, leading to intermittent deadlocks and unpredictable performance. The new version of the NCCL backend comes with a strict rule: One process group gets only one communicator during initialization. By default, this rule eliminates crosstalk between multiple communicators and all the complexities that entails. If a program requires parallelism, it will have to dispatch more process groups. The updated library also better handles stragglers by allowing for easier reconfiguration. If a GPU crashes or runs slowly, a third-party orchestrator can exclude the laggard and even assign a replacement worker. A “flight recorder” has also been added to give admins a better idea of what went wrong. Previously, if a cluster of GPUs stalled—perhaps from a timeout or deadlock—the user would just get a generic message. The new flight recorder captures all operations in a series of snapshots that can be inspected after a failure occurs. Rice noted the cleaned-up NCCL backend, also called ProcessGroupNCCL, will give developers a blank slate, making it easier to add new features. The legacy ProcessGroupNCCL is part of the 2.15 package as a rollback, but if the new NCCL backend works as expected, the older version will be removed in the 2.16 release, planned for early next year. Why, you may ask, does a machine learning framework need a specialized communications library? Like NumPy, PyTorch supports numerical computing, but it also provides tools for building and training neural networks on CPUs and GPUs. In these environments, workers must constantly exchange data such as tensors, gradients, activations, and weights. The standard TCP-based networking stack can introduce bottlenecks in large GPU clusters, so NCCL uses optimized communication paths to move data between GPUs while reducing CPU overhead. People on both sides of the political aisle have been pushing for this type of change for some years. The key question is whether this will cut greenhouse-gas emissions from the US energy sector or push them higher still. The federal permitting system is designed to protect the environment against industrial pollution from large projects like power plants, transmission lines, and natural-gas pipelines. Today, permitting is governed by a range of laws, including the National Environmental Policy Act and the Clean Water Act. And if there’s one thing you might know about the current state of permitting in the US it’s that it’s slow and incredibly complicated. So in its 417 pages, BAAJA attempts to tighten things up. The bill has provisions that narrow the sorts of projects that need to apply for federal permits and others that enact deadlines in hopes of speeding up a process that today can take years. (There are also specific provisions about transmission planning, mining, and the rest of the kitchen sink; this thing is dense.) One thing to know about this bill is that it’s largely technology neutral: It opens the doors for the buildout of projects involving both fossil fuels and renewables. So there’s a weird mishmash of legislators and outside groups supporting it. The bill’s four sponsors in the Senate are two Republicans and two Democrats. The White House has backed this permitting reform bill, with its Council of Economic Advisors finding that it would reduce review times and have economic benefits like boosting GDP. Clean energy groups like the American Clean Power Association have largely come out in favor of the bill too, with the group’s analysis finding that it could unlock 350 gigawatts of new clean energy for the grid. Some environmental groups, however, oppose the bill, or parts of it. An assessment from the Natural Resources Defense Council says the parts about transmission could have substantial benefits for the grid, but the narrowing of environmental reviews could be damaging for public and private lands. Many in the climate world have long been calling for permitting reform, arguing that renewables stand to benefit more than fossil fuels. The thinking is that solar and wind power are some of the cheapest sources available today, so if we unlock the ability to build more energy, they are likely to win out economically. A few early analyses have tried to quantify just what BAAJA would mean for emissions in the US.
