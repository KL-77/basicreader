UEFI bootkit infected fully patched Windows machines. Is Microsoft to blame?

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-03

Visit Original Website
In 2023, malware tracked as BlackLotus became the first publicly documented in-the-wild UEFI bootkit capable of bypassing Secure Boot on fully updated Windows 11 systems. With the industry-wide chain-of-trust mechanism subverted, the malware was able to run malicious code before Windows was fully loaded and many security defenses were operational. Microsoft had already patched the vulnerability that BlackLotus exploited in 2022. In 2023 and 2024, Microsoft issued additional updates. The first added a new certificate that no longer trusted the vulnerable boot managers. The second provided the means to revoke trust in the digital certificate used to sign older vulnerable Windows boot managers. The organizations infected by the new bootkit, which is tracked under the name BlackX, had installed all of these patches. So how did they fall victim? Before discussing the incident further, a brief review of BlackLotus and UEFI malware is in order. Short for Unified Extensible Firmware Interface, UEFI is firmware that runs immediately after a computer is turned on. One of its main jobs is to load system configuration settings, identify and initialize hardware components, and prepare the computer to start its OS. Another important UEFI function is Secure Boot, a security feature that establishes a chain of trust by verifying the digital signatures of each piece of boot software before allowing it to execute. Its purpose is to prevent attackers who gain control of a device, either physically or through a malware compromise, from running unauthorized boot code in place of legitimate software. One item that must be digitally signed is the boot manager, a component stored on a small dedicated partition on a drive. The boot manager is responsible for the initial loading of the OS. Both BlackLotus and BlackX exploit “Baton Drop,” a logic flaw in the Windows boot manager that allows attackers to bypass Secure Boot (and, under certain conditions, extract BitLocker encryption keys). The vulnerability, tracked as CVE-2022-21894, came to light in 2022. Microsoft fixed the vulnerability that same year, but it didn’t revoke trust in the certificate used to sign older vulnerable boot managers. BlackLotus exploited this gap and went on to load a malicious kernel-mode driver. What makes BlackLotus and BlackX so valuable to attackers is that they execute early in the boot process, allowing them to load malicious drivers into the Windows kernel before many OS and security defenses are running. UEFI bootkits can be difficult to detect without specialized security tools and may survive an OS reinstallation if the infected boot partition remains intact. While bootkits require attackers to first compromise a device, they are particularly valuable because they combine stealth, persistence, and extensive control over the OS. The discovery of BlackLotus marked a major milestone because it transformed bootkits that bypassed Secure Boot from a theoretical threat to a real one. The ability of BlackX to achieve the same feat is similarly notable. In May 2023—two months after the discovery of BlackLotus—Microsoft began a yearslong process aimed at closing the vulnerability once and for all. Having patched the underlying bootloader vulnerability in 2022, Microsoft’s May 2023 patch batch included security updates to prevent attackers from loading older vulnerable boot managers. The mitigation process included adding a new signing certificate, Windows UEFI CA 2023, to Secure Boot’s database of trusted certificates. In 2024, Microsoft introduced a broader mitigation that provided a way to add the old certificate, Windows Production PCA 2011, to Secure Boot’s forbidden-signatures database. The latter step would prevent older boot managers signed under the 2011 certificate from running. But as Ars reported at the time, this second patch didn’t revoke trust in the older vulnerable boot managers either. Fear of rendering potentially millions of devices unbootable if they hadn’t received the necessary updates prevented Microsoft from immediately enforcing the revocations. Unlike most patches, the updates alone didn’t provide complete protection. This state of purgatory has existed ever since and is what allowed BlackX to bypass Secure Boot three years after BlackLotus. “This patch deployment practice creates a problem—as it is extremely unusual for a system administrator to take extra steps after installing an update,” a researcher from Kaspersky, the security firm that discovered the BlackX infections, wrote recently. “Usually, when applying patches, system administrators take no further action unless an installation failure occurs. These instructions for activating the patch are buried deep on the Microsoft website, and we assess that they are quite hard for an average IT administrator to notice.” A Microsoft representative said in an email that the company is advising customers to follow guidance in this Knowledge Base post published two weeks ago. Nowhere in it, though, does Microsoft warn that the patches released to date do nothing to protect customers from Baton Drop attacks. As Kaspersky notes, Microsoft leaves out that after installing the patches, most administrators must take an additional action described in this 2023 Knowledge Base article. (It’s not clear from this article what the status is for home Windows users.) The BlackX incident underscores the difficult balancing act Microsoft must perform. Revoke the key before the new one has been installed, and a machine becomes little more than an expensive paperweight. Allow the old patch to be trusted for four years and counting, and successful exploitation is almost inevitable. The middle ground, it would seem, would be for Microsoft to be more explicit about the risks that result when the 2011 certificate isn’t manually revoked. Last week’s Knowledge Base article would have been an ideal place to do that, very possibly to the benefit of threat actors. “It is likely that the threat actor behind this campaign took into account that few organizations realized the importance of manually activating the Baton Drop patch,” Kaspersky said. “As such, we believe they decided to invest time in understanding how BlackLotus works and creating a copycat of this bootkit.”

But even though automated review detected the error within an hour, Stone acknowledged that additional hurdles kept admins locked out. State and federal lawmakers—as well as a coalition of 14 attorneys general—have asked Meta to further explain its actions. In a letter to No Kings, Meta offered to arrange a meeting with a technical expert who could better explain the mistake. But No Kings rejected the response, saying it failed to address any of the group’s ongoing concerns and requesting a meeting with Meta senior leadership instead. The group stressed that the issue goes beyond its Instagram account, saying that Americans who rely on Meta’s platforms need assurance that voting content won’t be suppressed ahead of or on election day. Among eight questions, the coalition asked Meta to publicly disclose any communications with the Trump administration about the No Kings account or the historic early voting day event. On October 30, Meta executives met with No Kings. According to the coalition’s account of the meeting, Meta said it has “no plans” to change policies that lead to harmful over-enforcement, a problem that even the independent Oversight Board, which reviews Meta’s content decisions, worries could result in suppression or censorship of No Kings and other pro-democracy content during this critical period. “Meta acknowledged that it made a mistake, but an apology without action does nothing to ensure that this kind of censorship won’t happen again,” No Kings said, calling Meta’s laissez-faire attitude “unacceptable.” Ars has found no evidence that the Trump administration asked Meta to restrict the No Kings account or that anyone at Meta intentionally targeted the account for political reasons. EPA throws in the towel on policing air pollution from factory farms

The Environmental Protection Agency late last week announced it would end a 20-plus-year effort to develop methods to monitor air pollution from factory farms, effectively shuttering the agency’s stop-and-start attempts to regulate the potentially pollutant-spewing facilities under the Clean Air Act. The announcement came just days before the midterm elections and was characterized by the agency as a long-awaited reprieve for farmers, a historically important constituency for Trump and the Republican Party. When tens of thousands of animals are packed into one place, pollution from their waste is blown from ventilation fans or emanates from giant lagoons of liquid effluent. Livestock facilities emit fine particulate matter, ammonia, nitrogen oxides, sulfur dioxide, and volatile organic compounds, all contributing to a toxic mix. Research has shown that emissions from these operations are a major cause of air pollution, causing nearly 12,700 deaths in the United States a year. Strictly speaking, concentrated animal feeding operations, or CAFOs, are required to get permits under the Clean Air Act and report emissions of these pollutants under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation and Liability and Act and the Emergency Planning and Community Right-to-Know Act. But through a deal crafted in tandem with the EPA and the livestock industry, they’ve been given amnesty from doing so for more than 20 years. The arrangement goes back to the early 2000s when the agency attempted to prosecute CAFOs for exceeding limits on some of these pollutants. The industry pushed back, arguing there was no adequate way of measuring them. So the agency worked with industry groups to develop what was dubbed the Air Consent Agreement, through which the EPA granted thousands of CAFOs safe harbor from legal action in exchange for their participation in a study that was funded by the industry. The goal of the study, the agency said, was to develop tools and models that CAFOs could use to “determine whether they emit pollutants at levels that require them to apply for permits under the CAA or submit notifications under CERCLA or EPCRA” and “produce a scientifically sound basis for measuring and estimating air emissions” from these facilities. But since the adoption of the agreement in 2005, the agency has failed to produce any air “emissions estimating methodologies,” even prompting a 2017 report from the agency’s Office of Inspector General that faulted it for slow-walking the process. Meanwhile, the Trump administration and Congress have also gutted reporting requirements for factory farms to report air pollutants under CERCLA, also known as the Superfund Act, and EPCRA. “As EPA has been saying it’s proceeding with this plan, it’s been dismantling all of its own tools to regulate emissions,” said Tarah Heinzen, the legal director for the advocacy group Food & Water Watch, which has tracked the proceedings from the beginning and filed dozens of lawsuits attempting to push regulators to control pollution from CAFOs. “Now it has announced it has no intention, basically, of ever holding factory farms accountable under the Clean Air Act, either.” In its October 30 announcement, the agency noted that in the two decades since the study, known as the National Air Emissions Monitoring Study, was conducted, many farms have modernized their barns and manure systems. “Good science requires good data, and these data are too old and too limited to support methods that farms could rely on,” said Abigale Tardif, the acting assistant administrator for the agency’s Office of Air and Radiation, in a statement. “We would rather tell farmers that plainly than issue numbers we cannot stand behind.” Rob Brenneman, president of the National Pork Producers Council, which has long pushed for the decision, said in a statement: “This move confirms what farmers have long known: Every farm is different, and there is no one-size-fits-all model to apply to them.” Heinzen noted that the agency’s decision simply means that this administration has no intention of regulating air emissions from CAFOs, but for now, the Clean Air Act still requires permits for facilities that exceed emissions thresholds. The amnesty granted to these facilities will no longer be in effect now that the agreement has concluded. “There’s no EPA-issued methodology to estimate emissions,” she said. “That doesn’t mean that there aren’t good studies out there of emissions from different types of animal feeding operations that can be used.” Heinzen pointed to California, which has models for livestock emissions that it uses to enforce its own air regulations. “We just don’t have one set of national emissions factors from EPA,” Heinzen said. “That does not absolve these factory farms from their obligation to comply with federal law.” Still, under this administration, it’s clear air emissions from factory farms won’t be measured and monitored any time soon. “No one’s going to estimate anything,” Heinzen noted. “As EPA said in its press release, we’re certainly not about to try to enforce the law.” The Air Compliance Agreement did not extend to methane. The US livestock industry is the single-biggest source of methane in the country, but Congress effectively blocks livestock facilities from reporting methane emissions in annual spending bills. Do screens in cars represent luxury? Judging by the latest car interiors, the answer would seem to be an emphatic “yes.” Screens aren’t just getting larger; they are multiplying across the dashboard and morphing into different forms and concepts. Indeed, an interior’s screens are a pretty good indicator of what brand made the car—or at least how much the owner paid for it. But is this actually what customers want? Are more screens really better? And even if they are, luxury car features inevitably trickle down to cheaper, more mainstream offerings, and screens can only get so big. That leaves luxury automakers with another question to answer: “What’s next?” Mercedes-Benz is working on a new display alternative that could provide an answer. For now, it’s known as the holographic display. Forget the images from Star Trek and Star Wars that just popped into your head, though. It’s effectively a more advanced form of heads-up display, or HUD. Whereas a traditional HUD projects images onto the windshield glass, the holographic display has two main components. It still uses a projector, but its key element is a special transparent film laminated into the glass. The film uses holographic optical elements, or “super-small mirrors,” as Mercedes development engineer Lena Hees described them, that selectively reflect the light emitted by the projector, keeping the image visible even in direct sunlight while hiding it from the driver’s view. Today, the passenger-side touchscreens in Mercedes and other luxury vehicles rely on driver-monitoring cameras or screen polarization to keep the driver from seeing what is playing on the passenger side. Speaking of passenger screens, the holographic display is clearly being considered as a future alternative or replacement for this technology. Although screens in cars are increasingly ubiquitous, a recent JD Power study indicated that car owners classify passenger screens as “not necessary” and have generally negative opinions about them. Mercedes has clearly observed similar opinions but for slightly different reasons. “We have customers who like the Hyperscreen,” said development engineer Maximilian Hilger of the pillar-to-pillar screen found in an increasing number of Mercedes models. “But there are others who want to maintain luxury materials.” In other words, the massive screen design doesn’t seem as luxurious as the pillar-to-pillar bands of wood or other materials found in previous-generation models. And that’s not an opinion isolated to Mercedes’ customers. New Mercedes chief design officer Bastian Baudy recently told German magazine Auto Motor Sport that “Materiality and haptics must remain tangible—with switches, buttons, and intuitive controls. Real materials are at the heart of Mercedes: metal, wood.” Mercedes CEO Ola Källenius went further, grouping interior quality and materials among the brand’s “forever values,” along with safety and maintaining what a Mercedes is like to drive. That presents conflicting priorities, however. “Yes, you need to have all the [technology] that a customer could ever dream of and wants,” Källenius told Ars. “But you need to package it in an aesthetically pleasing way. That’s also part of it.” That brings us back to the holographic display. Keep in mind that the photos in this article show early prototypes I saw at Mercedes’ research facilities in Sindelfingen, Germany. The prototype projector sits inside a rather crude, trapezoidal hole dug into the dash of a right-hand-drive, pre-2027 S-Class. It’s also loud, with a cooling fan whirring like a laptop visiting too many junk-filled websites. The production version would have a much smaller projector opening than those of the prototype and existing heads-up displays. Manual Holstein, a manager working in Mercedes research for future displays, indicated that the projector could be small enough to be hidden behind wood or metal trim pieces. Holstein also said you won’t hear a fan in the production model and that the resolution would improve. The resolution is certainly considerably better than any current head-up display, including Mercedes’ own, which features augmented reality elements. Full-color, commercial-like videos of the C-Class Electric played during the demonstration, and the display was sufficiently bright, even though Holstein said the prototype was operating at just 20 percent of its maximum brightness. Issues remain, however. The resolution may be good compared to the arrows, script, and graphics of a current HUD, but it’s poor when compared to a passenger touchscreen. The video’s contrast was also too high, and as you can see in the photos, the picture was distorted into a narrow height due to the angle of the windshield. Frankly, no one would choose to watch anything on it over a phone, tablet, or conventional passenger screen. It needs to improve by orders of magnitude before becoming a viable alternative. Again, though, this is just a prototype. Holstein said the holographic film size, or “eyebox,” can be made much bigger and be raised higher than the HUD-like height of the prototype. He also dismissed the distortion as a problem unique to the prototype. Imagine nearly half the windshield transformed into a virtual screen that’s visible only to the passenger. Beyond videos or other entertainment, Mercedes envisions the holographic display highlighting points of interest in the view through the windshield. A similar display could be placed on the driver side, showing situational information, enabling video calls, or providing entertainment when parked. In the future, it could even let drivers watch things other than the road when Mercedes’ Level 3 autonomous driving system allows them to take their eyes off it. At the very least, the technology could replace today’s HUDs and their larger projector boxes. Having experienced the prototype while the car was moving, I also think the holographic display could help prevent the motion sickness that can come from staring at a passenger screen while the car is moving. The higher screen angle and ability to see more of the outside world beyond created a notably different visual experience. Of course, the question remains: “Is this what customers want?” If the JD Power survey found that customers don’t care about passenger touchscreens, will they be any more enthusiastic about holographic video displays? That seems doubtful. But that’s just as much of an open question as whether the production version will actually live up to its promises. There’s also another matter: What, exactly, defines luxury? Before Bastian Baudy took over as Mercedes’ chief design officer this year, his predecessor and former boss Gordon Wagener echoed Källenius’ thoughts about the direction of the brand’s interiors. He was considerably blunter, though. “Screens are not luxury,” Wagener told ABC News. “You have a better—and bigger—TV at home, right? And every car has a big screen. So we have to create luxury beyond the screen. That’s why I talk about craftsmanship and sophistication.” If the holographic display lets automakers emphasize traditional design and materials while still offering unique technology, it could be a promising way forward.

In many mammalian species, larger creatures tend to have longer lifespans. Mice, for instance, live an average of just two years, while some whales can last for as long as 200 years. Dogs, however, buck this pattern; larger dogs tend to age faster and die younger than smaller ones, which makes canines an ideal model for biologists to study to learn more about aging in general. Arizona State University scientists report that these lifespan differences seem to be due to so-called “jumping genes,” according to a paper published in the journal Science. The authors used data from 864 dogs collected in the Dog Aging Project to map patterns of DNA methylation (which influences how much certain genes are turned on or off) across the entire genome. It’s part of the epigenome, whose markers can change over time in response to stress and other environmental factors. Genetic sequences, by contrast, are largely fixed. So DNA methylation is a good chemical indicator of aging and DNA regulation. Their analysis showed that aging in dogs is linked to the decrease in the regulation provided by DNA methylation, particularly in regions where there are “jumping genes” that can move from chromosome to chromosome. The authors identified a specific class of jumping genes called LINE1s that were responsible for the different rates of aging in dogs. The team also found that severe injuries—the kind that need surgery or time in the intensive care unit—were about twice as likely when a powered vehicle like an e-bike or e-scooter was involved, compared to regular bikes and scooters. In the world’s most populous country, sheer scale makes addressing each of these daunting, and their convergence even more so. Climate change and artificial intelligence add more uncertainty, making many of these problems more acute in the next two decades. Yet none can be adequately addressed unless the principal actor shaping India’s development—the Indian state—becomes more competent. The meta development challenge for India will face over the next few decades is the quality, competence and integrity of the state, without which progress elsewhere will remain elusive. Despite its multiple political and social divisions, India’s default ideology is statist: the governing Bharatiya Janata Party on the right, the communists on the left, and nearly everyone in between share a belief that the state should have a central role in controlling the economy and directing social change. Why, then, is there so little effort devoted to improving how the state functions, even as challenges mount? In India’s vast federal system, responsibility for development is divided among different levels of government, each of which struggles to fulfil its obligations. Over the past decade, more than 1.5 million people, mostly of working age, have been killed in road accidents in India, at a fatality rate much higher than the global average (per distance traveled). Roughly one-third of the victims have been pedestrians, often killed because Indian cities fail to construct and maintain something as elementary as sidewalks. The decrepit state of sidewalks exemplifies the depressing state of urban India. It reveals the extreme weakness of urban governance and the determination of state governments to retain control over lucrative urban land. The private sector is the engine of economic development, but it runs on foundations supplied by the state ranging from law and order to health and education. While India has established a considerable degree of order, it flails on establishing the rule of law. Violence has declined, but the police remain as much predators as protectors. Tens of millions of cases languish in overwhelmed courts, turning the legal process into principal punishment for all involved. If businesses cannot uphold contracts, women cannot walk safely after dark and minorities live with pernicious insecurity, the foundations of development will remain dangerously fragile. India may not possess vast natural riches, but it has a cornucopia of people. Turning their potential into human capital would be an invaluable national asset, but the failures of the Indian state, especially its state governments, are nowhere more evident. Indians live longer than they once did, but not necessarily healthier lives. High levels of malnutrition and stunting diminish the prospects of tens of millions of children. More children attend school but learning outcomes in public schools remain disheartening. Children do not vote; teachers do, and hiring teachers is  a lucrative source of rent and political patronage. The middle class, let alone the wealthy, would rarely consider sending its children to public schools, depriving the system of an influential constituency for reform. In higher education, where the federal government plays a larger role, ideological control and partisan appointments often take precedence over preparing young people for work, leaving millions with degrees that confer neither skills nor opportunity. India’s checkered development odyssey has much to do with the ailments of the Indian state. Contrary to its overbearing image, the Indian state is relatively small when measured by the number of public employees per capita. And these public employees are frequently moved through the “transfer raj,” which has become another a source of rent for bureaucrats and politicians. Training is usually reserved for elite civil servants. In a generalist civil service overseen by politicians who prize loyalty over competence, expertise becomes an orphan. These pathologies of the Indian state—a dense thicket of rules and procedures, extreme centralization and rigid hierarchy—are rooted in mistrust and an intensely status-conscious society. The result is an Indian state with the engine of a bullock cart and the brakes of a Rolls-Royce. In recent years, as India’s politics has grown more autocratic, centralization has become more acute within the federal government and in its relations with the states. Yet even as the federal government in New Delhi lords over the provinces, they do the same to local governments. Unlike China and the United States, which employ many more people at the federal and local levels and fewer at the province level, India concentrates public employment in its states. This concentration of public employment and administrative control in the hands of state governments rather than local governments and municipal bodies has deprived India’s cities and towns of authority and agency. All of this is widely known. Official reports have documented these failings for six decades. These pathologies persist because while everyone believes in the state and blames it the country’s afflictions, there is no impetus to build a stronger, more competent state. The state provides immense rents to the political class and bureaucracy, while public employees form a powerful constituency against reform. Administrative reforms excite neither politicians nor intellectuals, and their rewards lie beyond the electoral cycle. A weak state is also easier to capture: politicians can use it to browbeat opponents and the ruling and business elites exploit it to extract rents. This central development problem of weak state capacity is very unlikely to disappear by 2050. But India can augment capacity even without major administrative reforms. Technology offers the obvious opportunity. India has built a world class digital public infrastructure, which has improved taxation, public procurement and welfare payments. But the very strengths of that system have also made transferring money to voters remarkably easy, encouraging competitive populism, even as efforts to strengthen core public goods have faltered. To invert the familiar adage, the Indian state prefers to offer citizens fish rather than teach them to fish, preserving their dependence on its largesse and, with it, their pliability as voters. States can also improve their capabilities by drawing more effectively on civil society and business. Elinor Ostrom, the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in economics, observed that many public goods are “co-produced” by state and society. But such cooperation requires the state to trust civil society instead of seeking to control and muzzle it for political reasons, as is the case in India now. Contracting infrastructure construction or sanitation services to private companies can also help, though its benefits are limited by the persistent danger of rent extraction. The condition of the Indian State offers little confidence that it recognizes the magnitude of the coming challenges and is gearing itself up to meet them. From the bureaucracy’s ability to formulate and implement policy predictably to the ability of courts, regulators and the Election Commission to enforce rules impartially, the core institutions of the Indian state are weakening. This “strategic weakness” is not merely an accidental outcome of imperatives of electoral competition crowding out important long-term reforms. It is a deliberate choice by India's political class. Unless that choice changes, it is difficult to imagine India meeting its development goals in the next few decades. Amicus’ “By the People” series concluded last month with a capstone conversation taped live in front of a virtual audience of Slate Plus members. Dahlia Lithwick: I would love to hear from each of the three of you—what was the moment when it became clear to you that the Supreme Court is not going to save us, and further, the moment the court stopped behaving like the stewards of American constitutional democracy? When did you realize it was time to stop believing that these nine justices can claim sole ownership over the Constitution? Stacey Abrams: I structure my thinking about this around DEI. We tend to dismiss DEI as a sort of corporate HR issue, but in the United States—and the right has understood this for a while—DEI is the container for all of the rights, privileges, and defenses that we’ve created since the birth of this nation, that guarantees access to citizenship and to the rights and prerequisites of being an American, and not just being an American, but of being in America. If you look at the arc of history, the things that go into that container are the 13th, 14th, and 15th amendments, the 19th Amendment, also the Americans with Disabilities Act, and the Immigration and Nationality Act. All of these things are DEI. There are three things DEI protects in our democracy: education—what you know, the economy—what you do, and elections—who’s in charge. The first attack on DEI under this regime happened when they went after the Voting Rights Act in Shelby County v. Holder in 2013. Education was Students for Fair Admissions, and the economy was their attack on Roe v. Wade, because it stopped women from controlling their bodily autonomy. But all of that was predated in 2013 with the attack on voting rights, which underpinned the ability of Americans to have a voice in how this country works. Daphna Renan: I started to think about this at the beginning of the Obama administration. I was a young lawyer in the Justice Department. President Obama and members of Congress had just won a landslide election on the argument that our healthcare system was broken, and we needed to fix it, and had spent all of the political energy, momentum, and capital they’d gained in enacting a landmark Affordable Care Act. And I remember seeing that all of a sudden, everything that mattered was what five justices were going to say about this statute when it was challenged for its constitutionality in federal court; and that it no longer mattered that there had been this massive political coalition-building and compromise and debate in enacting the law. It could all collapse under very controversial theories that five justices held.And at the same time the court was starting to really aggressively build out its unitary executive theory—the idea that the president could violate federal laws long understood to rein in abuses of executive power. Ilyse Hogue: My point of no return was in the months I spent between July and October of 2018 fighting Justice Kavanaugh’s Supreme Court nomination. We had almost never seen a citizen uprising opposing a nomination as powerful, durable, and sustained. And yet no one stood up—not Justice John Roberts, not Senate Majority Leader Mitch McConnell—and said, “Actually, the investment of faith in the American public in this backstop of justice is more important than this one man achieving a life goal.” I felt a total collapse of trust in all of the systems that are the pathways for ordinary citizen engagement in our democratic processes. Without that trust, we cannot function as a democracy. I have spent the better part of the last year on the road in communities around the country. We’re hearing from the fastest-growing segment of the American population that political scientists call “double-haters.” They don’t like either party. They don’t have a lot of faith in the institutions that are supposed to serve us. What we’re experiencing in these rooms with people from all walks of life, all political persuasions, is that they still have a deep belief in the promise the constitutional principles outlined: separation of powers, separation of church and state, equality, freedom, rights. How do we expand the court to reflect the composition of the country we currently have? We have not done that update in decades. We do not have a court that’s reflective of the needs and values or the lived experiences of the people they are adjudicating. If you were to ask someone on the other side of the ideological aisle, “Do you think things are moving fast enough?” Depending on what their lived experiences are—they may not necessarily think it’s the Supreme Court, but they will have a conversation about expanding the lower courts. I’m in favor of expanding the Supreme Court, expanding the lower courts. I’m also in favor of thinking about a term limit conversation at the Supreme Court level. But you cannot have those conversations unless you walk people through the entirety of the process. When people know more, they demand more, and the capture of the courts has relied on ignorance—on people not knowing what they should be entitled to. It’s largely reliant on the fact that people are so used to things being broken, they don’t know what it feels like when it works. Daphna Renan: When we talk about court reform, I think the question is, to what end? What is the proper role we want the court to play in American constitutional democracy? If you look at Callais this past summer, you have a court that believes it can decide that a landmark law enacted and reauthorized over and over by overwhelming votes in Congress, signed into law by presidents of both parties, a law vindicating a vision of the 15th Amendment—five justices get to say whether that can be our vision of the 15th Amendment, whether that can be the Constitution that belongs to us. This judicial supremacy puts the court’s understanding of the Constitution over any other law the people may elect representatives to enact, no matter how misguided, how dangerous, or how problematic that interpretation of the Constitution is. So when we talk about court reform, we should also be talking about the tools available to Congress, through federal laws, to restrict that totalizing power of the Supreme Court. One idea that’s been on the table over and over again throughout history, and actually passed the House of Representatives during Reconstruction, is a statute that would say you need a consensus among the justices before they can say a federal law is unconstitutional. It shouldn’t be that five or six justices on a thin, controversial reading of the Constitution can reject what a whole movement has built in coalition to transform into a landmark federal law. Are the people still in charge? Is our grand experiment in democracy still working?” Stacey Abrams: We live in a deeply divided country, and the divisions are so visceral that at this moment, a constitutional convention is not actually a solution set. We should not be so myopically focused on one solution set. We should look at all the tools we have. The challenge with all these tools is that they don’t necessarily look like they will build what we want. My work is really focused on how we excavate and investigate those tools. Then you wrap that together with Daphna’s work that says, “Here’s what we’ve done before,” and Ilyse’s work that says, “Here’s what people want to build.” I’ve spent much of this election season working on state and local elections. State elections can have more impact in this moment than a constitutional amendment, because we have the ability at state and local levels to start to tweak the things we need. Instead of waiting for a federal solution set, which is what the Constitution provides, I’m looking at how to reverse-engineer what opponents to democracy have often utilized. They don’t fight at the federal level alone. They go to the state and local level and dismantle democracy at its base and let the infection and the rot rise. That means we can start the planting at the base. It does not mean we don’t have a democracy. It does mean we have to fight harder for it. We are in a competitive authoritarian regime. Daphna Renan: The Constitution is not what blocks our ability to pass laws that limit the role of dark money in our elections. What stands in the way of that is the current interpretation by the Supreme Court, which is a deeply controversial interpretation. He argued for the need to construe these amendments in the light of humanity, “in the light of progress and civilization” that we want to see. The abolitionists built out this idea that if you give the people the power to define the Constitution through our politics, through our organizing, through our coalition-building, through our building political parties, through our voting, and through our legislating, that there’s an alternative vision of the Constitution we can bring into life. Ilyse Hogue: We are not nearly as divided as the authoritarians want you to believe. Division is their fuel. Unity is our fuel. People have not given up. They may have given up on the two-party system. They may have given up on the idea that politics as usual and the status quo is going to deliver what they want. But they are resilient, and they want to fight if we’re offering them pathways to fight for this vision of universal rights, freedoms, and opportunity. A new approach known as “reverse game theory” aims to control powerful AI models by designing systems that incentivize them to stick to the rules. As AI models become more capable, safety researchers are scrambling to ensure they are “aligned” with human desires—to make sure, in other words, that rogue agents don’t break the law, hack into unauthorized servers, or release poison gas into major cities and kill us all. Until now, this work has mostly focused on trying to peer into an AI’s “brain” to understand what it is “thinking,” or training models to hew to values that we hold dear. Claude, for instance, has its constitution—a sort of moral code. The trouble is, these techniques don’t seem to work particularly well: Models have been shown to deliberately underperform on tests, hide their intentions, and mislead their evaluators. Now some alignment researchers believe that reverse game theory, also known as mechanism design, might get better results. Where game theory tries to predict how rational actors will behave when given a particular set of rules, reverse game theory attempts to design rules that will elicit the kinds of behavior we want to encourage. An example of mechanism design in the real world might be the classic strategy for splitting a piece of cake in two: One person cuts the cake and the other gets the first choice of pieces. This incentivizes the cutter to make the pieces as even as possible. Other examples include surge pricing in ride-hailing apps, which encourages drivers to be available at busy times, and second-price auctions, where the winning bidder pays the price of the second-highest bid instead of the highest. Platforms use these auctions to sell online advertising because they encourage buyers to bid close to the maximum they are willing to pay. In AI, mechanism design starts from the premise that we may never be able to understand enough about the internal workings of frontier models to make sure they are aligned. This means we should design mechanisms that incentivize agents to behave in ways that we want, regardless of what’s actually going on under the hood. A recent preprint paper by Stephen Morris, an economist at MIT, along with colleagues from Yale and Columbia suggested some potential mechanisms that could stop agents from “sandbagging” tests (pretending to be worse than they actually are—for example, to avoid triggering safety mechanisms). Instead of being rewarded only for how well it does on a test, a model could be rewarded for how well its answers predict what a second model will say. Deliberately playing dumb becomes a losing bet. “AI agents have a very good understanding of what other AI agents can achieve,” Morris says. “So it does seem like a context where this cross-checking could work more practically. The paper also suggests pitting agents against each other, creating a competitive dynamic that incentivizes them to perform their best, or pairing agents that are known to be biased in opposite directions and rewarding them depending on how closely their outputs match, thus incentivizing them to reach an optimal answer. A second approach, as envisioned by Elad Hazan, a professor of computer science at Princeton, is to design mechanisms that take inspiration from the ways human society keeps people “aligned”: social norms, the justice system, the monetary incentives of free-market capitalism. “We want to align superintelligent AI,” he says. “Why don’t we first talk about humans? How do we align humans?” Hazan’s suggested mechanism involves having AI models take turns as either “solvers” that tackle problems or “auditors” that check the others’ work. “One agent acts like law enforcement; the other agent acts like the citizen,” he says. During training, the models are rewarded depending on outcomes: Solvers that get caught cheating are punished, but so are auditors that fail to catch the cheaters. In situations where there’s a disagreement between auditors and solvers, the case can be sent to “trial”—perhaps a human expert will decide who is right. There is, Hazan admits, a risk that agents will collude with each other—as auditors have colluded with the businesses they’re meant to be auditing, or corrupt police officers have colluded with criminals. But that problem can be solved by rewarding the auditors well enough, so that being caught isn’t worth the risk (a lot easier in the world of AI training than the real one, where money for police salaries is tight), and by auditing the auditors using techniques akin to employing mystery shoppers. Hazan points to Nobel Prize–winning work by the economist Gary Becker, who introduced the idea of the “rational offender” in the 1960s. Criminals don’t break the law because they’re inherently bad people, he said. Instead, they do it when it’s the most rational choice given the incentives on offer. Crime makes sense only when the proceeds outweigh the potential punishment. Alignment shouldn’t be thought of as a property of a model, he argues, but as a descriptor of its behavior at a particular moment. “You are, like, a nice person,” he says. “Are you aligned? I don’t know. Probably you are aligned most of the time, but there could be a situation where you’re not.” One potential issue with applying mechanism design to AI: It relies on the assumption that AI agents behave rationally. Mechanism design has a mixed record with humans, who often fail to behave the way economic models say they should. It’s ironic: Economists have spent decades trying to reduce human behavior to mathematics, and now, in large language models, we’ve built mathematical systems that run on human language and could be just as inscrutable. AI models exhibit behaviors that seem more human than machine, and they can be swayed by subtle factors that are difficult to understand and explain. “Is it even reasonable to assume that they will respond to incentives?” asks Helen Qu, a research fellow at the Flatiron Institute, a nonprofit dedicated to advancing scientific discovery through computational methods, who has written about mechanism design and AI safety. “Obviously, AIs are not rational,” Morris says. “They’re just algorithms at work.” However, as economists have found with humans, treating them as if they’re rational could still be useful if it helps to predict and control their behavior. Qu sees mechanism design as just one layer in a “defense in depth” strategy for keeping AI models aligned—alongside interpretability, regulation, and training, not instead of them. But there are a lot of questions to be answered before the approach can be implemented in actual frontier models. It’s unclear, for instance, how these theories will apply to real models, or whether mechanisms designed for one task will carry over to another in practice. When a developer gives an AI agent a coding task, the agent does more than answer once. It plans, generates code, runs tests, and repeats the process to correct errors. A single task can use 10 to 50 times more tokens than a basic chat prompt. The more useful the agent becomes, the more calls it makes. With per-token pricing, the workflows creating the most value can also become the most expensive. The financial pressure is already visible. Uber reportedly exhausted its entire 2026 AI budget by April after expanding its use of Claude Code. GitHub Copilot moved from flat-rate pricing to metered credits. Gartner predicts that AI coding token costs will surpass average developer salaries by 2028. The natural response is rationing usage caps, seat limits, and approvals before developers launch agents. But blanket limits can restrict the workflows delivering value. They also optimize the wrong number. Cost per token measures consumption, not results. Some spending reflects waste, including retries and runaway loops. Some represents the work of shipping software. A meter cannot distinguish them, and neither can a spending cap. A better measure is cost per merged pull request: total AI-assisted development cost divided by the number of changes that pass review and are merged. Track rework and defects alongside it so cheaper changes do not conceal lower quality. If that cost falls as AI usage rises, without compromising quality, developers are converting more consumption into useful software. The question is not how many tokens they use, but how much finished work the investment produces. Improving that equation requires two changes: reserve expensive inference for tasks that need it, and lower the cost of routine work. Agentic coding does not require the most powerful model at every step. Use a frontier model, or an on-premises flagship when policy requires it, for difficult planning and novel reasoning. Route suitable work, including code generation, test creation, and repeated refactoring, to open-weight models you operate yourself. A gateway can route requests according to task complexity, data sensitivity, cost, and latency. This reserves premium inference for the moments that justify it rather than applying premium pricing to an entire workflow. Meter the exception, not the workload. Open-weight models make this division increasingly practical. Software-engineering benchmarks show that leading open-weight models can approach frontier performance on some tasks. That does not establish equivalence across every repository or workflow. Evaluate candidates against your own code, tests, and acceptance criteria before assigning production work. Next, decide who owns the capacity. With rented inference, every call adds to the bill. With owned capacity, most of the cost is committed upfront, so each additional call gets cheaper as usage grows. The economics are simple: per-token pricing makes heavy use more expensive, while owned capacity becomes more efficient as teams use it more. Ownership is not free, though. Power, operations, maintenance, and depreciation all must be considered. The number that matters is the fully loaded cost per merged pull request. Owned capacity also delivers a type of value that never appears on an invoice: control over sensitive code. When code generation and testing run on-premises, proprietary code stays inside the enterprise. Only approved planning prompts and governed exceptions go to external models, through a gateway the organization controls. That opens the sensitive repositories where security usually stalls adoption, and where the biggest gains are waiting. If power users grow from a small minority to half the workforce, monthly spend can rise about 4.4 times with flat headcount. Your mix will differ, which is exactly why you need to measure it. Second, it tracks cost per merged pull request, because it connects AI spending to software that ships. Baseline both costs before changing infrastructure. Then run a complete development cycle on owned capacity. If both metrics do not improve as usage grows, your current model may still be the better choice. Owned capacity is not for everyone. Low or unpredictable usage favors APIs, with no capital tied up in idle infrastructure. Ownership pays off when sustained demand keeps it busy. Most enterprises will run hybrid: frontier APIs for demanding or intermittent work, owned capacity for repeatable volume. The Next Web | 2026-11-03

Visit Original Website
Adobe is adding fully licensed music from Universal Music Group and Warner Music Group to Premiere mobile, its free video editing app, the company said on Tuesday. The deal brings songs by some of the world’s most-streamed artists into the editing process. UMG’s roster includes Taylor Swift and Billie Eilish, while WMG represents artists such as Ed Sheeran and Dua Lipa. Creators will be able to add up to 60 seconds of a track directly to their videos. They can browse trending songs, preview tracks before selecting them, filter by genre or length, and explore collections curated by the labels. Favourite songs can also be bookmarked for later. The new features are coming soon, although the licences cover personal, non-commercial use only. Creators who need music for commercial projects will have to turn to Adobe’s Stock Audio library instead. Adobe is also introducing templates that come with music already paired to the edit. Finished videos can be exported directly to licensed social platforms. The app’s existing tools, including its Enhance Audio noise cleaner and Firefly-powered AI features, will remain available. “We’re giving creators new ways to build their stories around the songs they love for the first time,” said Bryan Stone, SVP of business development at Universal Music Group. He added that the agreement gives the label’s artists and songwriters another way to reach new listeners. “Our partnership with Adobe Premiere mobile integrates WMG’s catalog directly into the creative process,” said Rachel Scarpati, VP of digital strategy and business development at Warner Music Group. The way people engage with music now extends well beyond traditional listening platforms, she added. The labels have had a more difficult relationship with some creator platforms. In 2024, UMG withdrew its catalogue from TikTok for several months during a dispute over royalties, before the two sides reached a new agreement. This deal gives the major labels another route into short-form video, with licensing built into the editing process rather than handled only after a video reaches platforms such as TikTok or Instagram Reels. It adds to the industry’s growing number of licensing agreements, which now span AI startups and creator apps. Adobe launched Premiere mobile as a free iPhone app in September 2025. It brings a streamlined version of the company’s desktop editing tools to mobile devices, with projects transferable to Premiere Pro on desktop. The app is free to download, although some AI features require paid credits. Adobe has not disclosed how artists and labels will be compensated for the music’s use, or when the new features will become available.
