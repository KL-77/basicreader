Disgraced eMudhra drops out of TLS business after using cert private key of 4

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-22

Visit Original Website
eMudra, the certificate authority that issued eight intermediate TLS credentials so weak that anyone could mint certificates for any website or mail server—one had a private encryption key of just 4—said it’s getting out of the business. V. Srinivasan, chairman of India-based eMudhra Limited, cited increasing costs and complexities of compliance requirements—paired with the business generating just 1 percent of its revenue. He also said that the emergence of organizations providing TLS certificates free of charge further reduced returns on investment when compared to other products and services the company offers. “However, the level of social media amplification around this incident has brought scrutiny that is out of proportion to SSL/TLS’s place in our business,” the company continued in a Thursday post. “In view of this, after detailed deliberations our board has concluded that withdrawing from global SSL/TLS is the right decision for eMudhra and its shareholders.” The announcement came two days after researchers revealed eMudhra had issued eight certificates with private keys so defective they were trivial to discover. Anyone who took the time—which was measured in milliseconds—could crack them and then use them to generate TLS certificates that would be trusted by all major browsers, as well as an untold number of email servers, IoT devices, Docker containers, forgotten Linux machines, and unmaintained CLI tools that ship their own copy of the roots. Whereas NIST P-384 elliptic curve cryptography private keys—used in the type of certificates eMudhra issued—are supposed to contain roughly 384 bits of entropy, the eight compromised certificates had key sizes ranging from 3 to 8 bits. Stated differently, whereas there are supposed to be 2384—a number with 116 digits—different key possibilities, the strongest of the defective keys had a maximum of just 256. The private keys were 4, 56, 66, 115, 155, 156, 230, 237. Browser makers blocked certificates that were issued with the intermediate certificates within a day or two of them being discovered. eMudhra almost certainly faced the prospect of its certificate authority status being pulled by the CA/Browser Forum, an industry-wide group that sets rules for admittance into the TLS certificate program. On Wednesday, eMudhra provided a brief explanation for the debacle. The certificates were “created under eMudhra’s ECC root in a Key Generation Ceremony (KGC) held on April 30, 2025, using FIPS 140-2 Level 3 Hardware Security Modules (HSMs),” the company said. “A flaw in the KGC caused a wrap/unwrap step to produce a second set of weak keys alongside the original strong keys. The weak keys were the ones published to the browser root stores. eMudhra is working to establish the root cause.” HSMs are dedicated devices for storing encryption keys and other highly sensitive data. They are generally segregated from most of a network to prevent leakage. Wrapping and unwrapping refers to the process that occurs when keys are being transferred from one HSM to another. Before the handover, the HSM storing the key wraps it in a separate key. Once the encrypted key is moved to a new HSM, the latter unwraps the key. In a thread on Mozilla’s Bugzilla forum, eMudhra provided more details: Among them: [T]here appears to be a flaw in the KGC script that was used on that specific day to create these ECC issuers. As for these ECC keys, our review of the KGC, assessment of the code and view of HSM keys so far indicate that a) a set of original ECC keys were created onboard as expected b) an approved deviation to our usual KGC to test a wrap/unwrap was introduced as these keys were meant for export for this instance c) a flaw in the code resulted in the second pair of keys being created onboard which were weaker d) these second pair of weaker keys were used by mistake to create the csr [certificate signing request] (because the key labels were similar) and signed by our root e) the CSR from these weaker keys were also sent over to [fellow certificate authority] Sectigo for cross signing (we verified the files sent over to Sectigo). The CRL’s were signed with the original private keys (which were supposed to be the correct keys with appropriate entropy) but given that this resulted in incorrect signatures, they were resigned with flawed keys. There were human errors in multiple steps of the process that resulted in significant flaws in the key lifecycle integrity process for this KGC. We are now digging deeper for code flaws, process gaps, maker/checker controls specific to this issue. Over the next week, we will reverify every key ceremony we have done. A CRL, short for certificate revocation list, is a digitally signed list published by a certificate authority identifying certificates that have been revoked before their expiration dates. The revelation that the initial signing resulted in incorrect CRL signatures raises the question: why didn’t eMudhra investigate the cause of the failure? With the intermediate certificates blocked by browsers, much of the threat is gone. But plenty of other devices that rely on the same certificates require much longer windows to take the same action. Still other devices, such as embedded devices, may never be able to block them.

Ink droplets in motion snag top prize in Nikon Small World contest

A 25-year-old German experimental photographer has won the 2026 Nikon Small World photomicrography competition with a striking image of droplets of ink being deposited on paper by an inkjet printer. Ole Bielfeldt beat out 1,802 other entries hailing from 69 different countries. Bielfeldt became interested in photomicrography during the COVID-19 lockdown after discovering an old microscope in his parents’ attic. He figured out how to build an adaptor out of a tube and some wood to connect the microscope to his father’s camera and taught himself the art through trial and error, building custom tools as needed. He documents how he captures transformative images of everyday objects with behind-the-scenes videos posted to macrofying, his photography and videography brand. It was Bielfeldt’s curiosity about the inner workings of inkjet printers that inspired his winning entry, but capturing the action of ink drops being deposited on paper was a challenge, given that the process happens so quickly. The droplets, for instance, moved faster than the camera’s shutter speed. Bielfeldt’s solution was to deconstruct the printer so that the paper moved under a stationary (rather than moving) printhead, and then take the image in a dark room, using a flash to serve as the shutter, thereby freezing the ink drops mid-air. There was no need for pricey equipment. Bielfeldt does not use AI in his images, preferring to rely on traditional techniques. “Ole Bielfeldt’s winning image embodies what makes Nikon Small World so powerful: the intersection of microscope imaging, ingenuity, and artistry,” said Eric Flem, a spokesperson for Nikon Instruments. “By using a microscope objective in a different way, he transformed a familiar object into something altogether new and previously unseen. It is a reminder that the microscope is not only a scientific tool but also a creative one, capable of expanding how we see and imagine the world around us.” Founded in 1974, the Nikon Small World competition celebrates its 52nd anniversary this year. In 2011, the company added the Small World in Motion competition (see the 2026 winner here). Both are meant “to showcase the beauty and complexity of things seen through the light microscope”—one through still photographs, the other through photovideography, taking advantage of all the technological advances that now allow users to make movies or time-lapse images through the microscope. Electric or gas? BMW goes both ways with new 2027 X5 and iX5.

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-22

Visit Original Website
When we first drove BMW’s first Neue Klasse model, the iX3, it quickly revealed that BMW knows how to make a good electric vehicle. A clean-sheet design, it featured the automaker’s sixth-generation electric powertrain, a completely reimagined dashboard, and powerful computers to run the whole show. Now, much of that technology is being ported over to the larger X5 line. But BMW knows not all its customers are ready to go electric, especially in the US, so it’s sticking with its powertrain-agnostic CLAR platform, as used in cars as diverse as the large 7 Series and the new gasoline 3 Series. Which means in addition to the all-electric iX5, there are also mild hybrid and plug-in hybrid X5s available, and in time, even a hydrogen-powered option. For now, as I’ll explain, I think the EV is the pick of the bunch. The most conventional option is the X5 40 xDrive, powered by a 3.0 L turbocharged inline six-cylinder engine that generates 394 hp (294 kW) and 428 lb-ft (580 Nm). The 48 V mild hybrid system can contribute a decent slug of torque (148 lb-ft/200 Nm) if necessary, and the engine operates under the Miller cycle for better efficiency, as well as featuring the usual BMW engine technology, like variable valve timing. Expect around 26 mpg (9 L/100 km) from this powertrain and a full-to-empty range of 569 miles (915 km). In time, a 400 V plug-in hybrid X5 50e xDrive will join the mix on the production line at Spartanburg, South Carolina, with a 26.5 kWh battery pack and about 44 miles (71 km) of electric range. And eventually, BMW’s hydrogen fuel cell tech will find its way under the hood of the new-generation X5 too. But those wanting something more efficient than the X5 40 can consider the iX5. BMW is sticking with its strategy of an asynchronous motor for the front axle and an electrically excited synchronous motor for the rear drive unit, thanks to the lower losses of these motor types compared to motors that use permanent magnets. There is a combined output of 570 hp (425 kW) and 593 lb-ft (804 Nm): The front motor generates 245 hp (183 kW) and 225 lb-ft (305 Nm); the rear produces up to 325 hp (242 kW) and 369 lb-ft (500 Nm). Between the axles at the heart of the iX5 is a rather massive 144 kWh (usable) battery pack, made up of BMW’s new 120 mm cylindrical cells. These are taller than the 95 mm cells in the iX3, which increases energy density by 30 percent over the smaller SUV’s pack. So even if it’s not quite as long-legged as the gas X5, it should still get 450 miles (724 km) on the EPA test cycle, BMW told us. The car’s route planning is designed to take charging into account and uses onboard AI to “consider expected charging capacity, reliability, and precise station location data when selecting stops.” It will even automatically open the charging port flap when you arrive. Like the smaller Neue Klasse EVs, the pack operates at 800 V, but it is even more capable when it comes to DC fast charging. At a demo during the first drive in California, we watched as an iX5 plugged in at 10 percent state of charge. Within a little over nine minutes, the EV had reached 50 percent SoC, peaking at 465 kW and averaging 375 kW throughout the session, which delivered 61.5 kWh. (The IONNA station had been specially upgraded to charge above the usual 400 kW.) While that kind of power level is now old hat for some Chinese automakers, it’s a big step forward for a European OEM. A 10–80 percent fast charge takes as little as 22 minutes—provided you have access to a 460 kW charger—via the NACS port. That same port will accept up to 15.4 kW of AC power via BMW’s home charger, or you can use the provided 11 kW onboard AC charger. There are adapters for both V2H and V2L, as the X5 is capable of bidirectional charging. For decades, BMW’s orange-lit cluster of instrument dials was instantly recognizable. But the move to digital cockpit displays put an end to that. Now, the new HMI paradigm from Munich is the Panoramic Vision display, which blends into the windscreen’s frit (the black border that’s painted on). The display is clear and quite minimalist but very legible, appearing a few inches further down the hood and thus closer to where your eyes focus on the road than if you had to look down at a conventional dash. It first showed up in the iX3 and now arrives in the bigger X5. While it looks unfamiliar at first—and people sure seem to hate the unfamiliar these days—together with the full-color heads-up display, you rarely need to look down into the cockpit while driving. That goes for interacting with the infotainment system, too. The trapezoidal touchscreen and BMW’s current iDrive OS user interface are easy to use, but the onboard personal assistant works much better than the Alexa-based system did last year in the early iX3 we drove. I recognize that I’m an outlier using voice commands to interact with the car, but when they work, I’m going to use them. Particularly in the absence of physical controls—“Hey BMW, turn on the driver’s seat massager” is easier for me to do behind the wheel than tapping the car icon to access vehicle settings and then scrolling down to seats and then selecting the tab for massage and then turning it on. There’s also a passenger screen, although, helpfully, BMW made this its own option rather than including it in a package. The screen only allows the front seat passenger to watch streaming videos, play games, or browse the web—they cannot interact with native infotainment functions like navigation or the climate settings from that screen, just the one to its left. Another difference between the gasoline X5 and the iX5 is that the latter features BMW’s new “Heart of Joy.” This is one of the powerful domain controller computers that replaces the jumble of black boxes and single-function ECUs, in this case the one that manages vehicle dynamics. As we found with the iX3, this clever electronic brain enables solid chassis and powertrain control, including the ability to come to an incredibly smooth stop while one-pedal driving. But the iX5 has another party trick that I didn’t notice in the smaller, cheaper EV: It will automatically detect red lights and stop signs, alerting you and braking the car if necessary (i.e., you were about to run the light or sign). The feature works whether you’ve engaged active drive assist or not. It’s always there like a safety net along with the car’s other advanced driver assistance systems. (I am sure you can disable this feature like the others, although I didn’t check, and I’m sure people will post comments with their particular edge case. Given driving standards these days, I’d be happier if everyone left it running.) The new assist suite is called BMW Symbiotic Drive, and it’s of the very latest generation of ADAS that allows the driver to apply brake inputs without canceling out of active driving mode—only a heavy stomp on the brake will shut off cruise control. Neither X5 nor iX5 are unpleasant to drive. The X5’s engine is quiet, and neither car suffers from much wind noise. Unlike the iX, which had something of a “skyhook” magic carpet ride, both iX5 and X5 are more firmly sprung, but not bone-rattlingly so. But I give the edge to the heavier iX5—although the battery adds to a hefty 6,200 lb (2,813 kg) curb weight, the extra mass helps dampen the short-duration shocks that make it past the X5’s (optional) adaptive air suspension. Longer-duration bumps are smoothed out by either car. If you have passengers in the iX5, you might find Sport mode has too violent an initial throttle tip-in; in the other throttle settings (eco, comfort), this is not an issue. The iX5 also offers five levels of lift-off regenerative braking, from none at all (it coasts very well) to 0.6 G of regen. And it has true one-pedal driving that will bring you to a complete stop. The 2027 X5 40 xDrive starts at $72,100, plus a $1,450 delivery and handling charge, and you can already find them in BMW showrooms. There is still a premium for the electric version—144 kWh of lithium-ion isn’t cheap, after all. Thus, the iX5 60 xDrive starts at $79,800 plus delivery and arrives in Q1 2027.

Let’s explore four frequently made arguments for what would prove the largest and, so far, probably the most universally praised merger in the annals of capitalism. The idea is that while SpaceX profits are just starting to accelerate, adding Tesla’s earnings, generated from its bedrock EV and battery sales, would immediately raise the former’s earnings per share. We’re looking at almost a merger of equals. SpaceX would need to issue the equivalent of 92% of its current shares outstanding to capture Tesla. Over the past few months, an upstart political polling firm kept delivering good news for Democrats—and especially those on the left. Across more than a dozen surveys, it found progressives leading in key primary races and showed other Democrats in striking distance of winning traditionally Republican states. Except, it turns out, the surveys were fake. The deception, perpetrated by an organization called Wedgewood Polls, doubles as a cautionary tale about how people who follow politics should think about polls—not just fake polls, but also ones that are on the level. A data-journalism outlet called FiftyPlusOne contacted Wedgewood Polls this week with some troubling allegations: The firm, which claimed to have started conducting polls over the summer and didn’t list any employees on its website, appeared to have manipulated survey data to produce fake results. The details of the deception are a little in the weeds, but Wedgewood ultimately came clean. In a public statement on Tuesday, the firm described itself as a “fictional entity” that had deliberately published “fabricated numbers.” It then deleted its social media accounts and website. Yikes. But why would someone want to fake poll results? Wedgewood claims it did so as part of “an experimental project to demonstrate the vulnerabilities, lack of oversight, and overall low barrier to entry in the modern political polling industry.” Yet Wedgewood also lied to media outlets that did ask probing questions about its methods, so that explanation is hard to take at face value. Money is one potential motive. Wedgewood appears to have charged clients to conduct surveys on their behalf, a standard practice for aboveboard polling firms that could in this case amount to fraud. We still aren’t 100 percent clear on who the people behind Wedgewood are, but they may have wanted to make money by manipulating betting markets on the races they claimed to survey. Wedgewood claims its deception “was never intended to sway an election or permanently mislead the public,” but it may also have wanted to make certain Democrats look more electable—either to boost progressives or get Democrats to spend valuable resources in red states. Did Wedgewood’s surveys affect those races? Polls can influence who voters, donors, and party committees decide to support, and there’s some evidence that Wedgewood’s did. Mallory McMorrow, a Michigan Democrat whose U.S. Senate campaign flopped, says a Wedgewood survey showing her support cratering helped throttle donations and increased calls for her to drop out. “It killed us,” she wrote this week. A group called Median Strategies also confessed to fabricating survey data earlier this year, similarly describing itself as a “social experiment examining how purported polling information could enter and spread through the political information ecosystem.” And on Thursday, FiftyPlusOne uncovered a third political polling firm that may have cooked some of its data. That firm, SoCal Strategies, has since deleted its website and social media accounts. So does this mean political polls are just made up?? That’s going a step too far. It’s true that political polling has fallen on hard times. Americans are increasingly less likely to answer surveyors’ cold calls, making high-quality polls more expensive. Major polling misses in recent elections have also caused some universities, survey firms, and media outlets to abandon political polling entirely. But does that mean most polls are fake? Emphatically no. Legitimate pollsters strive to be transparent about who’s conducting them, who’s funding them, and the methods they use. They’re also among the people most upset about what Wedgewood did. “People like the idiots behind Wedgewood, and, to a lesser extent, Median, make it harder for the rest of us who are absolutely busting our butts to do good public opinion research,” the polling expert Natalie Jackson wrote on Substack today. But if that’s true, does this Wedgewood incident really matter? What’s the bigger picture? Unless you’re some kind of oracle, election outcomes are unknowable in advance. In that sea of uncertainty, it’s natural to grab onto any shred of data that seems to offer clues, and polls have become an outsize part of how many Americans think about politics. It’s also becoming more common for campaigns to blast out poll results—sometimes of questionable provenance—on social media or in fundraising emails. Some of that is understandable. When it comes to predicting elections, polls remain much better than person-on-the-street interviews, lawn signs, or crowd sizes at rallies. But polls have always been imperfect, their accuracy subject to reaching a representative sample, assumptions about who will actually vote, and mathematical laws of probability. None of that justifies Wedgewood’s deception, and it’s a frustrating reality that the stakes of American politics have gotten higher even as the availability of high-quality polling data has declined. But the next time you see a polling result that seems too good to be true, just remember: As the cliché goes, the only poll that really matters is on Election Day. If an attacker can successfully compromise a legitimate email account, they are then able to pose as a trusted individual or organization without triggering any warnings. Traditional email security will see nothing wrong. From there the threat actor can pick their moment to cause havoc with objectives that range from stealing money, expropriating sensitive data and propagating catastrophic malware. Serious financial loss and a damaged corporate reputation are commonplace outcomes. The typical account takeover lifecycle starts with the bad guys obtaining credentials, usually through the medium of phishing. Generative AI (GenAI) has made it easy for an attacker to trick an unwary individual into giving away login data, perhaps by posing as a colleague or other trusted party or by encouraging them to enter confidential information on a fake login page. You might expect, once a legitimate identity has been successfully compromised, that all hell would immediately break loose. These days the typical account takeover artist is more stealthy and sinister than that, in no rush to act at this early stage since no alarm bells have been triggered. Most will take their time to test out and verify their stolen login data, and see where it might take them. “Conventionally attackers would take over an account and immediately start firing off spam and phishing,” says Dave Baggett, SVP Cybersecurity with software developer Kaseya. “Now they are starting to get clever. They’ll get access to an account and sit tight, watching all the email traffic passing and waiting for an opportune moment to inject themselves into the conversation.” Once they’ve figured out an organisation’s weak points and come up with a plan of attack, the bad guys will start to weaponize the compromised email account to target other employees and internal systems. Assuming their motivation is financial, they might be on the hunt for a way into payroll or invoicing databases. But their ultimate aim might lie further afield in the form of the organisation’s customers and suppliers, and that’s when the real nightmare can start. So called outbound phishing offers the criminal a way to assert control over a whole new range of sensitive end points. Victims of outbound phishing are highly vulnerable in the face of what seem to be messages from a trusted partner and ally. It is one thing for an organisation to protect its own email accounts with robust controls like multifactor authentication (MFA), but no one has control over the authentication practices of other parties: “Let’s say we have a customer who we’re protecting,” explains Baggett. “They’re safe, but imagine that they start getting suspicious emails from a third party. Our customer has no idea what is or isn’t protecting that third party. Emails coming from that source might appear to be from a real account, but it could be a hacker typing them.” Given that you can’t run checks on third party organisations, something more sophisticated than regular email security is required. Ideally this would be a GenAI-powered solution that can bring language understanding to bear, able to examine conversation histories in depth to see if something anomalous can be spotted. It makes life even harder for defenders when the bad actor has successfully assumed the identity of somebody senior. If they are impersonating a top-tier manager with an extensive and trusted reputation then they have a powerful asset on their hands. Conventional email filtering systems will often be configured to allow senior personnel past certain defensive layers with minimal friction, so as not to restrict important business communications. The next competitive advantage in enterprise AI has less to do with which model you choose than with what you build around it. That’s an important shift. For the past several years, much of the AI conversation has centered on model capability: bigger models, better reasoning, larger context windows, higher benchmark scores. Those advances have been extraordinary. But as AI moves from experimentation to production, the question facing enterprise leaders has changed—it’s no longer, “what can the model do?”—it’s now, “Can we deliver that intelligence reliably, economically, and at scale?” Increasingly, the answer depends on the serving harness—the engineering system surrounding the model, including the architecture, memory management, scheduling, and optimization decisions that determine how efficiently model intelligence becomes a production capability. For technical executives, that changes where AI investment should go—and what they should demand from the architecture supporting it. There is no universally “best” model or AI architecture. There is only the right solution for the business problem. A customer-facing assistant optimized for fast responses has fundamentally different requirements from a document-processing pipeline that can run overnight. An internal knowledge application and a high-volume customer service platform may use the same foundation model but require very different serving architectures. The workload defines the constraints. The constraints should determine the architecture. That sounds simple, but it runs counter to the way many organizations have approached early AI deployments. During a proof of concept, model accuracy naturally dominates the discussion. Production introduces constraints that are much easier to ignore when only a handful of users are involved: latency, concurrency, memory pressure, GPU utilization, infrastructure cost, and unpredictable traffic. A model that produces marginally better answers is not necessarily the better production choice if it doubles inference costs or cannot meet the application's latency requirements. Not every workload needs a Ferrari. Sometimes the smarter engineering decision is the model that delivers exactly the performance the business needs without paying for capability it doesn't. The objective should be to deploy the right intelligence at the right cost for the workload. That makes serving architecture more than an engineering concern. It becomes an investment decision: where to spend on compute, where to optimize, and where additional model capability simply isn't worth the cost. Once AI reaches production, seemingly isolated engineering decisions begin affecting one another. Serving architecture influences memory utilization. Memory management affects concurrency. Concurrency shapes batching and scheduling, and scheduling affects GPU utilization and latency. Only then can techniques such as quantization, speculative decoding, or optimized attention mechanisms deliver their full value. This interdependence matters because organizations can spend considerably more on infrastructure without solving the constraint limiting performance. GPU memory is a good example. During inference, the model's weights remain fixed, but the key-value (KV) cache grows with active requests and context length. As concurrency increases, a system can run short of usable GPU memory even when compute capacity is still available. Adding more compute without understanding that bottleneck may simply make an inefficient system more expensive. The lesson for technology leaders is broader than memory management: Measure before you optimize. Identify the dominant constraint. Address it. Measure the result. Then determine if another bottleneck emerged. Repeat. Don't think of optimization techniques as Pokémon cards: The goal isn't to collect them all. The goal is to apply the technique that removes the highest-impact constraint while preserving the application's business requirements. For technology leaders, that demands a different governance question. Instead of asking which AI capabilities the organization can deploy, ask which constraints stand between a proven capability and sustainable business value. Perhaps the most important reason to invest in the serving harness is that today's leading model will probably not be tomorrow’s. Models will improve. Hardware will evolve. New optimization techniques will emerge. The architecture surrounding them should make those changes easier to absorb, not force the enterprise to rebuild its AI platform every time technology advances. That makes adaptability an increasingly important outcome of AI strategy. Organizations that build serving systems around workload requirements, clear service-level objectives, and measurable production economics gain the ability to evaluate new models and infrastructure against what the business actually needs. A model change can become an evaluation and configuration decision rather than an architectural reset. Those emissions form a feedback loop, accelerating warming further. The steady rise in global temperatures has already shrunk the footprint of the Arctic permafrost by about 12% since 1850, and the pace has been picking up in the last 50 years. The wild thing is, only a minority of the models that have been used in UN climate reports actually take into account the gradual loss of permafrost. How severe are the fires that have accelerated local thawing? What’s the local climate and environment? And what kind of permafrost is at stake? The higher we push the planet’s temperature, the more we mess with complicated natural phenomena that we’re only beginning to understand. Ask most executives to picture the cost of AI and they’ll likely see GPUs—racks of accelerators with gigawatts of power—and a waitlist for silicon. Much of the back-end data that’s generated—from billions of daily prompts, images, and videos—needs to be stored. But data storage is one of the harder infrastructure costs to plan for, and it’s often overlooked in initial AI infrastructure planning. Traditionally, AI infrastructure conversations have centered on the compute-intensive process of training a model. Training is finite: a run starts, burns through compute, and ends. But inference runs continuously, every time a model answers a prompt. For infrastructure planning, that difference matters: A training run is a project you can budget for and retire. Inference doesn’t retire; every inference request leaves something behind. Take AI-generated video. In this workload, the back-end spend can be significant. From internal measurements, WD found that an 8-second clip (returned to a user as a 3.97MB 720p file) left more than 500MB of associated data behind on the back end, including logs, cached context, immediate renders, backups, and safety records. That’s a 130x increase over the output file size the user saw. Scale this to an enterprise generating longer, higher resolution video and the back-end footprint grows into the hundreds of gigabytes per clip. And that back-end footprint is growing fast. McKinsey projects AI inference demand will grow at a 35% CAGR through 2030, versus 22% for training, making up more than 40% of total data center demand by the end of the decade. A recent WD-sponsored IDC global white paper that surveyed 763 IT and business decision makers found that nearly 95% of respondents reported an increase in data volume due to generative AI over the past 12 months, with roughly 61% reporting growth of 25% or more. “You can think of having more storage as an optimization for keeping the GPUs busy,” said a senior product manager of a hyperscale cloud service provider quoted in report. “The biggest challenge is to ensure that the GPUs are not idle, waiting for data to show up—hours of GPU idle time becomes very expensive, very fast.” Put in dollar terms: if your storage spend is growing 25% year over year while your compute budget grows in the more predictable cyclical way, storage ends up outpacing the budget line that receives the most attention. Compute demand rises and falls with training cycles, product launches, and hardware refreshes. But the volume of retained data doesn't fall back down with it when compute demand slows. Model logs, synthetic outputs, inference results, and more keep accumulating, and organizations are holding onto more of it for longer. This changes the role of retained data. Information that once moved into an archive and rarely surfaced can now become an input to future AI workloads. As more data is retained, retrieved, and reused, storage must support not only growing capacity, but increasingly dynamic access to it. The same WD-IDC survey found 96% of respondents anticipate needing faster archive retrieval to support inference or retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). AI workloads require both performance and capacity, with different technologies serving different needs across the data lifecycle. And for the vast volumes of data that must be retained and revisited, HDDs provide a cost-effective, high-capacity foundation, with a significant cost-per-terabyte advantage For a CFO or CIO, this means “AI infrastructure spend” may be scoped too narrowly on compute. A model that idles while data loads costs about as much as one idling for lack of a GPU. But the storage bottleneck stays hidden until it’s already a problem, surfacing in operating costs and retrieval delays rather than a CapEx line next to the accelerators. Consider a retailer generating AI product videos for its catalog, or an insurer running claims documents through a RAG pipeline for faster underwriting. In both cases, the compute cost is a known, budgeted quantity: a per-generation or per-query rate that finance can forecast. The storage cost is different: It compounds with every video generated and every claim processed, because none of that back-end data disappears once the task is finished. A team that budgets AI as “compute plus a rounding error for storage” may find, in 12 to 18 months, that the rounding error has become a significant fraction of the program’s total cost—right around the time leadership is deciding whether to expand. Addressing this challenge starts with architecture. Storage strategy tends to be designed for expensive, hot-tier performance (for data accessed instantly and constantly), while most AI data by volume accumulates in the warm, cool, and cold tiers behind it. What enterprises need is economically scalable capacity: Infrastructure designed for the full AI data life cycle, balancing performance with capacity, power, reliability, and total cost of ownership as data persists, compounds, and is increasingly reused. That means treating archives that once sat untouched as active assets for RAG and inference, and setting retention policies for data’s growing strategic value rather than its original storage cost. It also means recognizing that decisions about storage play out across fleets, not individual drives. Such infrastructure requires storage tiers, where individualized power consumption, reliability, density, and operational efficiency all determine whether the system remains economically sustainable as capacity scales.
