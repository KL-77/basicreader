Binance says hackers who stole billions in crypto spent months planning heist

The hackers who stole billions of dollars in cryptocurrency from the Binance exchange spent months working on the theft and used both social engineering and novel malware in the process, the company said Tuesday. The heist, which both the FBI and the TRM Security firm attributed to North Korean actors, initially targeted $6.34 billion in cryptocurrency from Binance wallets. The exchange has since frozen or intercepted $2.85 billion of that sum. Binance said it has set aside funds required to cover the loss and provided this link to a Proof of Reserves page. The $6.34 billion represents the biggest sum ever targeted in a cryptocurrency theft. Binance said the people behind the heist began no later than June and continued until last Thursday, when they successfully compromised an engineer’s laptop and withdrew assets from several wallets. Like just about every organization that suffers a public, high-consequence hack, Binance said the threat actor was sophisticated and resourceful. “Our current investigation indicates that this was a sophisticated, multi-stage campaign,” a report on the company’s investigation to date said. “It was not a single isolated breach or a simple phishing incident. The attackers combined a range of techniques, including social engineering by fake recruitment interviews, malicious software downloads, compromised internal AI tooling, endpoint compromise, and an advanced transaction-authorisation method.” Binance said that the first incident related to the compromise likely occurred in June, when an engineer downloaded a tampered version of Homebrew. The company linked to this incident reported in May, and its security team detected the compromised laptop, but only after the malware operators obtained information about the roles of the engineer and his related colleagues on the Binance wallet infrastructure team. In September, a separate engineer received messages through a legitimate job-hunting platform he was using. The message came from an individual posing as a project director looking to hire new employees. The purported project director arranged a first-round interview with a supposed HR representative and a second-round interview with the purported CEO of the fictitious company. During the first interview, conducted over a video platform, the purported HR representative shared a social media account that included personal photos and videos. The second interview took place over a social chat app. During it, the engineer was instructed to download an “assignment” that linked to a code repository containing malicious code that eventually executed on the engineer’s device during the setup process. “The malware employed sophisticated obfuscation and evasion techniques, including replacing a legitimate development tool component with a malicious version to execute commands and establish communication with an attacker-controlled external server,” Binance said. “The 0-day malware was not detected by the endpoint security protection tools at the time. The attacker also took steps to conceal the malicious activity and remove traces of the compromise, making detection and subsequent investigation more challenging.” On October 20, the attackers used the compromised engineer’s computer and an internal AI tool to take control of two other engineer devices, including the one previously targeted with the trojanized Homebrew installer. That engineer’s wallet contained a token granting access to a container deployment. Nine days later, between 17:15 and 19:56 UTC, the threat actor logged in to the third engineer’s device, used the stolen credential, deployed a container, and activated a script to initiate the transfer. Among other things, the attackers extended their unauthorized access to an internal wallet signing service to complete transactions outside the normal withdrawal process. Binance hot wallets on Solana and Optimism sent approximately 970,500 SOL and 44.2 million OP to attacker-controlled addresses. Binance then received an internal alert regarding large-volume transfers and immediately began investigating. Binance hot wallet sent 18,270 BTC to an attacker-controlled address. The attacker then used the signing service to exploit Ethereum’s EIP-7702 authorization mechanism. EIP-7702 allows an account to delegate certain execution rights to a smart contract without exposing the private key. In this case, the attacker used that mechanism to give an attacker-controlled smart contract partial execution rights over a Binance hot wallet. Binance immediately initiated multiple containment measures, including suspending withdrawals to restrict further outflows, coordinating with partners to freeze affected tokens, and taking steps to revoke unauthorized permissions. These efforts froze a total of $2.85 billion in assets. However, suspending withdrawals alone could not stop all unauthorized asset movements. The malicious EIP-7702 authorizations existed on-chain and operated independently of Binance’s normal withdrawal infrastructure.The attackers submitted 50 signing transactions to manipulate the account nonce and establish malicious delegation authorizations. Once established, these authorizations enabled the attackers to continue moving assets independently of Binance’s normal withdrawal infrastructure. Binance needed additional transactions to revoke these on-chain authorizations. The containment process involved additional technical complexities. Because the hot wallet used Multi-Party Computation (MPC), there was no single private key that could be used to immediately revoke the malicious EIP-7702 authorizations. The team therefore developed and executed a specialized procedure to revoke these authorizations, which required additional time. Furthermore, suspending withdrawals and shutting down hot wallet services did not automatically invalidate existing on-chain authorizations. Separately, TRM Security said the attackers used various means to launder successfully transferred assets through the TRON and Ethereum blockchain networks. The methods included “a self-minted token and liquidity pool, a lending receipt token passed between wallets, and cross-chain swaps chained through two bridges.” TRM is tracking the North Korean hacking group under the name TraderTraitor.

If my 45-minute drive yesterday morning is anything to go by, I think Slate Auto will have quite the hit on its hands. The startup had one of its prototype trucks in town and asked if I wanted to check it out—no jumping on a plane or having to spend yet another night in an interchangeable hotel room, just a drive from southwest DC to Rockville, Maryland. Easy-peasy. The prototype truck had been well-used, with almost 6,000 miles (9,656 km) showing on the small and minimalist but very legible digital display that also shows your speed and, if you engage reverse, the backup camera. And while the cabin layout was representative of the production version, the surfaces most certainly weren’t—the prototype instead uses a lot of hard and shiny black plastic, with strips of Velcro here and there left over from when the interior had to be camouflaged when the vehicle was out in public. Getting acquainted with the Slate was refreshingly easy. The Morris Marina-like door handles open with a pleasing mechanical action. The seat and steering column adjust manually but with plenty of reach, something many budget models used to leave out to save on cost. The key fob slots into the dash to turn it on, and there’s a column shifter to select from drive, neutral, park, and reverse. Set your mirrors first—also by hand—and it’s time to go. Visibility from the two-seat cab is good, and even though the Slate is on the smaller side, it’s as tall as most other crossovers at 68 inches (1.7 m), so you still sit relatively high up, certainly compared to a sedan. I still remember how to use a manual window winder, and the three dials on the dash for making it hotter, colder, and more or less windy inside approach the platonic ideal for car climate controls. Had we needed navigation, a phone mount on the dash would have done the job, but the area they now call North Bethesda is familiar ground from my past life in science policy, and the roads were mostly where I remember leaving them, so I could just concentrate on the drive. I’d have to describe it as refreshingly simple. That word again, but I can’t help it—the Slate really is the antithesis (in a good way) of so much of what we are being offered by the automotive industry right now, especially here in North America. The steering is direct and well-assisted for the truck’s 4,048 lb (1,836 kg) curb weight, and the suspension controls the body well. It’s easy to maneuver and park. It doesn’t have an especially supple ride, but the De Dion rear axle and coil springs must be able to cope with up to 1,500 lbs (680 kg) of cargo in the bed. With a zero to 60 mph (97 km/h) time of 8 seconds, acceleration is a little slower than a Chevrolet Bolt. But the instant pickup from the 181 hp (135 kW), 195 lb-ft (264 Nm) motor means it’s still very zippy. Right now, there’s a fixed amount of lift-off regenerative braking and default one-pedal driving. At low speeds, the amount of lift-off regen braking was more aggressive than any other EV I’ve driven recently, bringing the Slate to a halt tens of feet earlier than you initially expect, although this was easy to calibrate my driving to after a few minutes. Whether it was the black interior or the lack of frills, driving the Slate most reminded me of the few days I spent earlier this summer in Italy with a Fiat Panda rental car. Yes, the Fiat rolled a lot more when cornering. And it kept things even more old-school with a six-speed manual transmission that felt like stirring a bucket of rocks with a rubber spatula (while still adding to the fun). But both share a simple honesty and embrace the utilitarian rather than making any pretentious claims to luxury. And they’re both charming as a result. Of course, having a compelling product is only part of the challenge for Slate; mass production still needs to happen. As long as the company can pull that off, I think American drivers will like what it’s selling.

The DEA banned a drug some opioid users called a miracle

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-04

Visit Original Website
In 2012, as the driver of deaths in the opioid crisis began moving from prescription pills to heroin, Laura Bohn was tasked with finding safer drugs to manage pain. Funded by the National Institute on Drug Abuse, in 2017 she and other scientists revealed in the science journal Cell that several compounds—including a particularly promising one called SR-17018—could relieve pain in mice without causing respiratory depression. That’s a big deal, because respiratory depression is typically what causes people using opioids to stop breathing and die. Bohn, senior associate vice president of research at University of South Florida Health, says her team filed a patent on SR-17018. They went on to discover that mice using the chemical did not develop a high tolerance for it and that mice already dependent on morphine—a powerful opioid—could consume SR-17018 to reset their tolerance without suffering from painful withdrawal symptoms. It’s no longer just mice who use SR-17018. On Reddit and Discord, people who use drugs frequently refer to the compound as a godsend that’s helped them quit using opioids they’re addicted to. Until very recently, the drug, though not regulated by the US Food and Drug Administration, was relatively easy to purchase online, as it was not a federally controlled substance. Bohn, meanwhile, has been in the process of trying to conduct clinical trials on humans, which has never been done. But all of that came crashing to a halt when the US Drug Enforcement Administration emergency-scheduled SR-17018 as a Schedule I controlled substance in late August, putting it in the same category as heroin with “no currently accepted medical use and a high potential for abuse.” For Bohn, the move will make it much harder to do the research she’s been planning. “It’s a real hassle,” she tells WIRED. “Right now, I would like to have it tested for additional safety studies, and I can’t have it made, I can’t ship it, I can’t touch it. I can’t do anything.” For drug users who hope to use it to wean off opioids—despite the lack of clinical evidence that the drug is effective in humans—it’s the removal of a potential off-ramp for their addictions. Jim Bates has been hooked on various opioids, including prescription pills, heroin, and fentanyl, since he was 25. About two years ago, the 53-year-old Duncan, Oklahoma, kitchen manager got addicted to 7-OH, a concentrated compound found in kratom that activates the brain’s opioid receptors. “That shit blew my head off,” he says, noting he was introduced to it at a vape shop. “It was a whole lot like heroin.” He eventually graduated to a similar chemical, called MGM-15, which felt more like oxycodone—the effect he prefers. (The DEA placed both 7-OH and MGM-15 in Schedule I over the summer.) It got to the point where MGM-15 was “all I was thinking about,” Bates says. About 11 months ago, he started scouring the Discord and Telegram groups he was in for advice on how to quit. “I got introduced to SR-17, which was a freaking miracle,” he says, using a common shorthand for the chemical. It took him about three weeks to taper off MGM-15, using SR-17018. Previous attempts to get off 7-OH had caused him “anxiety like you wouldn’t believe” along with restless legs during withdrawal, he says. But with SR-17018, he didn’t experience any physical withdrawal symptoms. “They’ve never created anything like this,” he says, adding that he didn’t experience a high or “euphoria” from taking it. Michael, a fellow former 7-OH user, used SR-17018 to kick the habit in January. He also describes it as a “miracle drug.” Michael, who does not want his last name used to protect his privacy, says he’d tried in earnest to quit using 7-OH at least five previous times—each involved “three days of hell” and another week of feeling “low.” While he says SR-17018 didn’t take away every negative side effect of coming off opioids, “the anxiety and the sweating and the restlessness and all that stuff was gone.” He says he was able to keep working, with “no huge disruption in my life.” Both men have used gold-standard drugs for getting off opioids—buprenorphine and methadone—but say they prefer SR-17018 because it doesn’t have to be used long-term. Getting off buprenorphine and methadone can also cause intense withdrawal symptoms. Michael speculates that the feds may have banned SR-17018 because they saw it as a “potential disruptor to a multibillion-dollar industry.” “I think that that’s why they’re freaked out about SR becoming widely known and available, because it is going to help a lot of people get off of these maintenance drugs that they’re stuck on.” When Denver attorney Robert Rush caught wind that the DEA was planning to emergency schedule SR-17018, he filed a request to stop it, arguing that it has a lower risk profile than the other synthetic opioids being banned at the time. He tells me the DEA never responded. “It was a very reasonable, well-thought-out, grounded-in-scientific-evidence argument, and they just completely ignored it,” says Rush, who is executive director of the nonprofit Rights and Reason Project—which advocates for drug policy reform—and represents Bohn. In response to a request for comment, the DEA referred WIRED to its notice of intent to schedule SR-17018 and three other synthetic opioids. The document says the agency had found two samples of the drug in two states since 2025 via the National Forensic Laboratory Information System, which analyzes samples of drugs seized by law enforcement. It also said a separate drug monitoring system, Rapid Drug Analysis and Research, had detected some samples of SR-17018 on the West Coast. “The four synthetic opioids have no currently approved medical use and, based on positive identifications of these four substances in forensic drug exhibits and toxicology samples, are likely to be trafficked and abused similarly to other synthetic opioids,” the notice of intent says. It also says that because SR-17018 can reset a person’s opioid tolerance, it might leave them more vulnerable to overdose the next time they use—though the same is true for abstinence. Rush says the DEA’s use of temporary emergency scheduling powers in this case is “draconian” and shouldn’t apply, because SR-17018 hasn’t been implicated in emergency room visits or overdose deaths. The emergency scheduling will last two years, with a potential one-year extension—but the DEA could also permanently schedule the drug in that time period, a process that requires public and scientific input. During the emergency scheduling period, Rush says, there’s no opportunity for a judge to review the DEA’s decision. He says that the DEA banning gas station opioids like 7-OH while simultaneously getting rid of potentially helpful substances like SR-17018 “is setting up a perfect storm for another overdose crisis.” “I cannot find a compelling reason to shut down a couple of decades’ worth of research into something that could either be or lead to the development of next-generation opioid dependency treatment,” he says. California is accepting $1.42 billion in broadband grant funding from the Trump administration, but the state is not accepting the Trump administration’s demand that it stop enforcing a net neutrality law and other telecom regulations. California’s stand against the Trump administration could result in multiple legal battles involving the state, federal government, and Internet providers seeking government funds. Although California told the federal government it would accept the money, it is reserving the right to challenge the condition imposed by the US. The state could delay or cancel plans to distribute broadband grants depending on how the process unfolds. The National Telecommunications and Information Administration (NTIA) decided last year that states must agree not to enforce any rate regulation or net neutrality rule on ISPs that receive money from the $42 billion Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment (BEAD) program. California had to decide whether to accept its share of the fund despite that restriction. California is slated to spend $1.42 billion to deploy broadband to 270,571 locations and may ultimately receive up to $1.86 billion from BEAD for a mix of deployment and non-deployment projects. State officials argue that California should be able to obtain the money and keep enforcing its laws, because the Trump administration’s demand would violate the state’s constitutional rights. The California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) made its view clear in a carefully worded letter yesterday to Assistant Secretary of Commerce and NTIA Administrator Arielle Roth. “The CPUC cannot be bound by any terms or conditions that are unconstitutional or not authorized by law and it expressly does not waive or concede any arguments or challenges concerning the legality, interpretation, or applicability of any term or condition,” CPUC President John Reynolds told Roth. Reynolds’ letter also said the CPUC cannot prevent other California agencies from enforcing state laws, suggesting that the attorney general would maintain the right to punish misbehaving Internet providers. Reynolds’ letter acknowledged that the NTIA may not agree with the CPUC’s stance and said the state thus will not distribute any funding to ISPs until after the state and federal agency sort out any differences. If the NTIA refuses to change its position, California could sue the Trump administration on the grounds that the condition is unconstitutional. The NTIA does not appear likely to bend. “As noted in the Department’s response to CPUC, the terms and conditions of the award constitute the entire agreement between NTIA and CPUC. No part of the letter outlining CPUC’s concerns is incorporated into or made part of the award,” an NTIA spokesperson told Ars today. Consumer advocacy group Public Knowledge supported CPUC’s position. The Trump administration’s demand to preempt state laws is “brazen and meritless,” the group said. “This administration’s preposterous demand that industry be given a blanket waiver from affordability requirements, net neutrality and privacy protections, and any other commitments to connect and serve Americans has no basis in the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA), which established BEAD. And, as the commission points out, the NTIA has no authority to alter California state law or require California agencies to not enforce the law,” said Raza Panjwani, senior policy counsel at Public Knowledge. The CPUC voted to ratify the funding plan in September. But Reynolds said in yesterday’s letter to the Trump administration that his agency’s acceptance of the funds is “based on the CPUC’s best understanding of the effect of Section 50” of the BEAD terms and conditions. Section 50 of the BEAD rules says each state: shall commit that it will not enforce any law, regulation, order, contracting requirement, or other enforceable obligation that directly or indirectly regulates the rates, terms, and conditions of broadband Internet service… or imposes net neutrality rules, open access, or other utility-style rules on broadband Internet service, against a Subgrantee or its affiliates anywhere it provides service within the State (i.e., both BEAD and non-BEAD locations), while that Subgrantee has any subgrant that is still within its period of performance, extended period of performance, or federal interest period. The BEAD rules specify that a net neutrality rule is one prohibiting ISPs from “blocking content, throttling speeds, imposing data caps, or engaging in paid prioritization.” ISPs obtaining BEAD funding could be exempt from such laws for up to 14 years. Reynolds’ letter said the NTIA-imposed condition is “vague and ambiguous as to its scope and its relationship to Section 47 U.S.C. § 1702(g)(2)(A)(i) of the BEAD statute,” the US law that created the BEAD program. He wrote: The CPUC does not interpret Section 50 to require the CPUC to waive, release, or otherwise forfeit any rights, claims, arguments, or defenses concerning potential implications of Section 50 that may violate applicable laws. Further, the CPUC’s acceptance of funds under this award should not be understood as agreement either to refrain from compliance with mandatory legal duties or obligations, or to constrain other State of California entities in the exercise of their authorities to enforce state law, which is outside the scope of the CPUC’s authority. Moreover, California’s net neutrality law expressly provides that “any waiver of the provisions of this title is contrary to public policy and shall be unenforceable and void.” Likewise, many of the CPUC’s own programs that touch on broadband Internet access service are mandated by the California Public Utilities Code, and are neither discretionary nor waivable. The NTIA today updated its BEAD progress dashboard to reflect that California has signed its final agreement with the federal agency. California was the last state or territory to turn in its final agreement. “All 56 states and territories have signed and returned their award agreement, finalizing the process,” the NTIA’s updated dashboard page said. California could face legal problems if and when it distributes the money, which is to be given to Internet service providers in exchange for deploying broadband in unserved and underserved areas. ISPs could insist that they don’t have to follow state law and sue California or simply decide to violate consumer protection laws and defend against any lawsuits the state attorney general might file against them. Reynolds apparently hopes to avoid that outcome by holding discussions with the NTIA to resolve any disagreement over the enforceability of California state law. “To the extent that NTIA and the CPUC disagree about the CPUC’s obligations under Section 50 and other terms and conditions, the CPUC will execute any subgrant agreements if and when the CPUC and NTIA are able to resolve such disagreements and any other applicable conditions are met,” Reynolds wrote. “To that end, we will direct our staff to work diligently and cooperatively with NTIA staff to find such a solution.” Whether California’s strategy works “depends on what happens next,” Stanford Law professor Barbara van Schewick, a longtime proponent of net neutrality and other telecom regulations, told Ars today. “The letter doesn’t change the terms of California’s award: Condition 50 is still in it, and only a court or NTIA agreeing in writing can take it out,” she said. “The letter puts California’s position on the record before any contracts with providers are signed and preserves California’s legal arguments against Condition 50.” Attorney General Rob Bonta’s office declined to comment when contacted by Ars today. A spokesperson for California Gov. Gavin Newsom said California is not giving up its right to enforce state laws against Internet providers. “California is accepting federal broadband funding to bring high-speed Internet to hundreds of thousands of homes and businesses, many of them in rural and underserved communities,” a governor’s spokesperson said in a statement provided to Ars. “We’re ready to get these projects built with our local and federal partners. Connecting these communities is the goal, and so is protecting them. California’s laws, including our net neutrality protections, still stand.” California’s net neutrality law prohibits ISPs from blocking or throttling lawful traffic and says ISPs may not require fees from websites or online services to deliver or prioritize their traffic to Internet users. The first Trump administration lost an attempt to preempt state net neutrality laws, and California defeated ISPs that challenged the state law in court. California also has a law that prohibits mobile providers from throttling first responders during emergencies. That law was passed in 2019, a year after Verizon throttled an “unlimited” data plan used by Santa Clara County firefighters during a wildfire. Local wildfire-preparedness groups warned that the BEAD condition could prevent California from enforcing the throttling law and requirements for battery backups on cell towers. According to van Schewick, “California has strong arguments that Condition 50 is illegal. Congress designed BEAD to get broadband built while making sure the providers who take the money follow the law: the statute directs states to ensure funded providers can carry out their work ‘in compliance with all applicable Federal, State, and local laws.’” But van Schewick said the letter is just an initial step. “The real test comes the first time a BEAD provider blocks a competitor’s app, throttles firefighters, or walks away from a price commitment,” she said. “If the state enforces its laws when that happens, the letter will have protected Californians. If the state looks the other way, Condition 50 will have worked exactly as the providers intended, without a single court ever ruling on it.”

The restriction reflects legal requirements designed to prevent what antitrust lawyers call “gun-jumping,” when companies begin improperly coordinating their operations before a merger officially closes. The Federal Trade Commission has warned that merging companies must safeguard competitively sensitive information, including pricing, costs, and business strategies, until a deal closes. Update your preferences in Account Settings When Bobby Pulido, a well-known Tejano singer, decided to run for Congress in Texas’ 15th congressional district, many dismissed his candidacy as a long shot. When he decided to make playing at quinceañeras a cornerstone of his campaign’s voter outreach strategy, there were many naysayers. On Tuesday, Texas voters proved them wrong. Pulido won about 62% of the vote, shifting the recently-redrawn 15th congressional district 42 points toward Democrats compared with the 2024 presidential election, and unseating incumbent Trump ally, Rep. Monica De La Cruz. During her campaign, De La Cruz dismissed Pulido’s background, saying "this isn't about who you would want to perform at your niece's quinceañera." Pulido flipped the attack on its head, proudly stating he had performed at 75 quinceañeras while on the trail. When speaking to voters, he kept a tight focus on the economy, healthcare, and immigration. Pulido’s overwhelming victory on election night suggests that meeting voters where they’re at and engaging with them at cultural events that are deeply meaningful is an excellent way for politicians to connect with and mobilize Latino voters across the political spectrum. An estimated 75% of voters in the district Pulido won were unknown voters, and his strategy was effective because it made people feel seen. That is what we have been hearing from Latinos in focus groups for a number of years now—they are struggling daily to make ends meet, and no one seems to care enough to do anything about it. Latino opinions in this regard are not all that different from those of other Americans. What is different is the degree of economic hardship Latinos are facing and the extent to which those struggles, along with concerns about immigration enforcement, may have moved Latinos away from the Republican Party. Since 2020, polling has consistently shown that affordability—concerns about prices for basic necessities, housing, and stagnant wages—are what concern Latinos most. In a 2025 Equis poll Latinos surveyed reported high levels of economic precarity, with 29% reporting they had “trouble meeting expenses” and 32% that they “could just meet expenses with nothing left over.” Similarly, an October 2025 Pew Research poll found that 48% of Latino respondents had struggled to pay for groceries, medical care, or their housing costs in the past year. Latinos who worked full time were just as likely as those who were unemployed to say they have struggled to pay for food, medical care, and housing. Given that, it is not surprising that 61% of Latino poll respondents said that their economic situation had worsened since President Donald Trump took office in 2024. When asked by pollsters what their top concerns are, Latino respondents rarely include immigration on that list, but this misconstrues the fact that immigration is a key Latino concern. The truth is that Latinos are much more likely to be connected to the immigration experience than other Americans. Forty-four percent of Latino adults in the United States are immigrants. The October 2025 Pew Latino survey found that 52% of Latino respondents said they worry a lot or some that they, a family member, or a close friend might be deported. Fifty-nine percent said that they had seen or heard of ICE raids in their communities over the past six months. The violence around immigration enforcement has left its mark on many Latino communities. One of the most powerful images to come out of Texas on Election Day was the hundreds of “I Voted” stickers affixed to the shrine in honor of Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, a Mexican immigrant who was killed by ICE on July 7, 2026. In addition to the stickers, people wrote statements, saying they were voting for Lorenzo, for a better future, and a future without hate. According to Equis Research, from 2016 to 2024, Latinos shifted more in favor of Trump than any other U.S. racial group: a 16-point gain. It is important not to over-interpret those changes. It seems clear that there were aspects of Trump’s message that resonated in important ways with Latino voters. But there is much political discontent in the community. In the 2025 Pew survey, 68% of respondents said that Latinos’ situation in the U.S. was worse than a year ago. This was the first time in over two decades of Pew surveys that a majority of Latinos said their situation had worsened. Among those who voted to give Trump a chance in 2024, polling suggests that many have lost faith in President Trump’s promises, particularly around economic issues. The election results from Texas’ Rio Grande Valley suggest another shift may have happened, back to the Democrats. Over 90% of the valley is Latino, and Democrats swept all Congressional districts, including those that had been drawn to favor Republicans. Again, overinterpretation is a danger, particularly the assumption that electoral shifts of this type will be durable. That remains to be seen. Latino poll respondents have long indicated that they do not trust either party; the fastest-growing party identification among Latinos is no party preference. Few candidates or party operatives, in either political party, invest the time needed to authentically engage targeted Latino voters around what matters to them. As a result, either outreach does not happen or, in the rare cases that it does, it is symbolic rather than substantive. Latino voters can tell the difference. It seems like every electoral cycle, the question is whether the Latino vote will “swing.” This framing is reductive. It suggests that Latino voters are not voting based on their substantive concerns, but rather that they can be won over by one Spanish-language advertisement. But the polling makes clear that the stakes are real. Latino families are struggling every day to keep food on the table and a roof over their heads. They are trying to ensure that they have access to medical care when they are sick. And they are worried their loved ones might be deported at any moment. These are grave concerns, with potentially dire consequences for families and the community if they are not addressed. Until political candidates begin to take these issues seriously and find solutions, Latino voters will continue to search for a stable and supportive political home. Update your preferences in Account Settings Cheryl Groskopf, a marriage and family therapist in Los Angeles, once lived next door to a very loud little dog. It barked for hours at a time, including as she worked from home and tried to sleep at night. She initially chalked up the barking to puppyhood, but it kept getting worse. “The tiniest little jingle of the keys would set it off,” she says. Eventually, she stopped barbecuing in her backyard—her favorite pastime—because her presence there triggered the dog. But then she realized the absurdity of the situation. “Wait, no,” she recalls thinking. “I should be outside.” She finally texted her neighbor to ask if everything was OK. Her neighbor’s response: “Oh my God, I didn’t know it was this bad.” By then, Groskopf had developed a less charitable explanation. “She doesn’t care about her dog,” she recalls thinking. “She doesn’t care about her neighbors.” Having a therapist’s tools hadn’t made her immune to resentment—and it didn’t make the prospect of speaking up any less awkward. “You want to maintain a relationship with someone you have to see regularly,” she says. So how do you ask for quiet without making every subsequent encounter uncomfortable? We asked experts what to say—and how to follow up if the barking continues. If you decide it’s worth speaking up, choose a calm moment. (Not an afternoon when you’re fired up from a constant soundtrack of barking.) Enter the conversation with an open mind. Your neighbor might not have any idea how much of their dog’s day you’re hearing—or how much it’s disrupting yours. That can feel like a particularly generous interpretation when you haven’t had a decent night’s sleep all week. “You’re exhausted, you’re frustrated, and there’s 15 arguments in your head about why this person sucks,” Groskopf says. Before sending that pivotal text, she poured her unfiltered feelings into her Notes app first—cuss words included—then waited an hour before drafting something she could actually send. If you’re going the face-to-face route, ask if it’s an OK time for a conversation before launching into a complaint, advises Sarah Gershman, an executive speech coach who teaches communication at Georgetown University and Johns Hopkins University. She likes this wording: “I was hoping we could talk about something that’s been bothering me. I’d love to share my perspective, hear yours, and see if we can figure out a solution together.” Then explain what’s happening on your side of the wall or fence. Gershman suggests: “I’ve been waking up at night because of your dog barking, and it’s been hard to get back to sleep. I realize you may not even know this is happening. I’m wondering if we can figure something out together.” Ask whether they’ve noticed the barking and what might be setting it off. “You don’t know what their intent is,” she says, “but you do know the impact it’s having on your life.” A few specifics can help keep everyone from barking up the wrong tree. Instead of complaining that the chihuahua next door howls “all day,” provide exact times and durations, suggests Kate LaSala, a dog trainer and behavior consultant in New York who specializes in fear, aggression, and separation anxiety. Perhaps the dog barked from 9 to 9:42 a.m., then again from 10:10 to 10:20. Those details can help the owner connect the noise to a delivery, a visitor, or being left alone. If you’re concerned about the dog, say so. LaSala suggests explaining that “your dog sounds like they might be in distress or panicked.” You’re giving the owner information they can use to help their pet, which could improve life on both sides of the fence. Your neighbor might have a simple retort to your complaint: “Dogs bark.” Resist the urge to bark back. Before explaining why this particular barking is a problem, show them that you understand their perspective, Gershman suggests. If you live in a pet-friendly building, for example, acknowledge that they’re allowed to have a dog and that some barking is inevitable. “They’re more likely to listen because they feel understood,” she says. Then bring the conversation back to what you need. Groskopf suggests saying: “Of course, I completely understand that dogs bark, and I’m not expecting total silence. I’m just hoping we can figure out something that helps with the barking in the mornings because it’s really affecting my sleep.” You can be understanding without downplaying how much it’s bothering you. If the exchange keeps getting heated, give yourself permission to end it. “This might not be the best time,” Gershman suggests saying. Offer to revisit the conversation later, when you both have a better chance of hearing each other. Maybe your neighbor was apologetic and promised to work on it, and you returned home feeling hopeful. A week later, you’re still getting an unsolicited 6 a.m. wake-up call. Follow up without assuming they’ve forgotten—or decided your sleep is expendable. Groskopf suggests: “Hey, I really appreciated you talking with me about this last week. The barking is still happening around the same time, so I wanted to check in about what else we could try.” If the owner is working with a trainer or veterinarian, allow time for progress. The cause of the barking matters, LaSala says: A dog reacting to deliveries needs different help from one who panics when left alone. “Behavior change takes time,” she says. Ask what they’re trying, and talk about what might reduce the disruption in the meantime. Depending on the situation, that could mean keeping the dog inside during early-morning hours or finding someone to stay with them while the owner is out. Resist the urge to bang on the ceiling to make your point. Doing so can startle the dog and make the barking worse, LaSala says. If repeated conversations get you nowhere, bring your record of barking times to your landlord, building manager, or homeowners association, LaSala suggests. If you don’t have one, check which local office handles noise complaints. You can also use earplugs or a white-noise machine for some immediate relief while you pursue a solution. And remember that being considerate doesn’t require giving up ordinary pleasures in your own home. Groskopf eventually moved, after the initial improvement following her conversation gave way to more problems. Only afterward did she recognize how much she’d been adjusting her life around the neighbor’s dog. These days, she reads on her porch every day. “Just having that back is everything,” she says.

Instead, Trump spent much of his 53-minute speech talking about construction projects rather than the struggles of the nation he had been elected to serve. It was a bewildering capstone to a strange campaign. Allies marveled at Trump’s inability to stay on message as he crisscrossed the country. He boasted that oil companies were making a fortune as Americans struggled with energy costs. Video stores and photo labs are practically extinct in the age of camera phones and streaming. On the whole, I’m glad to give up balancing a checkbook and waiting in several lines every week. But as my conveniences pile up and tasks turn into taps on a screen, I increasingly feel like I’m missing something other than a treacly lollipop. Errands were a burden, sure, but they were a reason to leave the house. Without the obligation to go places, it’s easy to spend all day alone, switching from paying bills on my phone to watching videos on my phone to passively scrolling on my phone, ignoring the outside world and the people in it. Even the indulgences running errands allowed—a gourmet coffee or a fast-food combo meal—can be ordered ahead or dropped off. Some of these high-tech offerings let us do our errands from anywhere, while others insist we stay home. Miss a grocery delivery, and you risk warm yogurt or potential theft. For nonperishable deliveries, a doorbell camera lets you avoid going to the door to greet a stranger. You can stare through a virtual peephole all day, monitoring when packages arrive and clandestinely recording any passersby who look at them. These replacements are sometimes easier, but not always better. Many meals don’t travel well, and even the most reliable personal shopper might not pick out the same fruit you would’ve chosen. For a delivery devotee, what was once a series of social interactions is now a chain of one-tap transactions, which can reshape how we see other people: not as human beings occupying a shared space, but as hirable servants in a world made just for us. “It gives us this false sense of control and individuality if we’re just staying in our little box and having things come to us,” says Dr. Gillian Sandstrom, an associate professor of psychology at the University of Sussex and the author of Once Upon a Stranger, a book on the value of small interactions. Sandstrom cites research showing that people who have a wider variety of encounters—a polite nod with a stranger, a short conversation with a clerk, or a deep chat with a friend—tend to be happier. Like everything else, many of these small social interactions have gone online, and again, replacement doesn’t mean improvement. I can see hundreds of strangers in a short scroll through TikTok or Instagram. But I see them on a curated feed that’s mine and mine alone. And unless I also make content, they don’t see me as anything more than a metric: a like or a view. Parasocial relationships with influencers or podcast hosts may have some value, but the socialization remains one-way. Outings also give us a chance to practice how we interact with the world. There’s a reason they’re called social “skills,” Sandstrom notes. “How are you going to make a new friend? How are you going to go to a job interview?” she asks. “It feels like a really horrible idea to not have all these little opportunities to practice.” The smartphone has undoubtedly made it easier than ever to stay in touch with our real-life friends. But here, too, the interaction has changed. Texting gives me a drip of connection throughout the day, which maintains a feeling of socialization without giving me the full experience of hearing someone’s voice or seeing their body language. Virtual hangouts are like the grocery delivery order that’s just-about right: good enough, yet still a little disappointing. Errands could be a time for deeper socializing: an opportunity to catch up with a friend who had to do the same chores or who was bored at home and might come along. The places where we did chores and the pit stops in between served as de facto “third places.” As patronage drops, these centers of casual social interaction can’t stay open. Big box megastores and, later, e-commerce have led to empty storefronts in many towns’ main streets. High urban rents and a more isolated population make the business model for third places increasingly precarious. The places that do remain are built more for people who aren’t in them. With the rise of delivery and online ordering, coffee shops and fast casual restaurants increasingly serve mobile customers. The result: workers are slammed even when there’s no physical line, takeout orders pile up, and a constant stream of people pass in and out. Now, the in-person customer is often secondary to the virtual one. Sometimes the in-person customer becomes a virtual one, as businesses push visitors to order from digital kiosks. Some commercial restaurant space has been taken over by ghost kitchens—restaurants that only serve customers who order online and get their food delivered. Yes, errands were boring. I’m sure I told my mom this about 100 times as we went from store to store, aisle to aisle. But I’m not less bored now, I’m just bored in a different way. The two-screen experience is just distracting enough to keep me from thinking about what else I could do with my time. There was more excitement and novelty out in the real world: an odd interaction with a stranger, an overheard conversation in an aisle, or any of the little dramas of daily life that play out in public. It’s a little dystopian to imagine using every replacement for errands at once—streaming and scrolling while waiting for groceries and a fast-food delivery; sitting alone and being waited on by gig workers and digital assistants. Not everything needs to be done in-person, but as technology advances, the analog alternatives become harder to find, and we’re pushed to predictable interactions on screens instead of the softly social and occasionally surprising world outside. The upside of digitization, perhaps, is that without the need to run as many physical errands, we could devote more time to activities that are more rewarding. That’s if we can find a place for them and if we can recognize what we need, instead of just getting whatever we want. A new approach known as “reverse game theory” aims to control powerful AI models by designing systems that incentivize them to stick to the rules. As AI models become more capable, safety researchers are scrambling to ensure they are “aligned” with human desires—to make sure, in other words, that rogue agents don’t break the law, hack into unauthorized servers, or release poison gas into major cities and kill us all. Until now, this work has mostly focused on trying to peer into an AI’s “brain” to understand what it is “thinking,” or training models to hew to values that we hold dear. Claude, for instance, has its constitution—a sort of moral code. The trouble is, these techniques don’t seem to work particularly well: Models have been shown to deliberately underperform on tests, hide their intentions, and mislead their evaluators. Now some alignment researchers believe that reverse game theory, also known as mechanism design, might get better results. Where game theory tries to predict how rational actors will behave when given a particular set of rules, reverse game theory attempts to design rules that will elicit the kinds of behavior we want to encourage. An example of mechanism design in the real world might be the classic strategy for splitting a piece of cake in two: One person cuts the cake and the other gets the first choice of pieces. This incentivizes the cutter to make the pieces as even as possible. Other examples include surge pricing in ride-hailing apps, which encourages drivers to be available at busy times, and second-price auctions, where the winning bidder pays the price of the second-highest bid instead of the highest. Platforms use these auctions to sell online advertising because they encourage buyers to bid close to the maximum they are willing to pay. In AI, mechanism design starts from the premise that we may never be able to understand enough about the internal workings of frontier models to make sure they are aligned. This means we should design mechanisms that incentivize agents to behave in ways that we want, regardless of what’s actually going on under the hood. A recent preprint paper by Stephen Morris, an economist at MIT, along with colleagues from Yale and Columbia suggested some potential mechanisms that could stop agents from “sandbagging” tests (pretending to be worse than they actually are—for example, to avoid triggering safety mechanisms). Instead of being rewarded only for how well it does on a test, a model could be rewarded for how well its answers predict what a second model will say. Deliberately playing dumb becomes a losing bet. “AI agents have a very good understanding of what other AI agents can achieve,” Morris says. “So it does seem like a context where this cross-checking could work more practically. The paper also suggests pitting agents against each other, creating a competitive dynamic that incentivizes them to perform their best, or pairing agents that are known to be biased in opposite directions and rewarding them depending on how closely their outputs match, thus incentivizing them to reach an optimal answer. A second approach, as envisioned by Elad Hazan, a professor of computer science at Princeton, is to design mechanisms that take inspiration from the ways human society keeps people “aligned”: social norms, the justice system, the monetary incentives of free-market capitalism. “We want to align superintelligent AI,” he says. “Why don’t we first talk about humans? How do we align humans?” Hazan’s suggested mechanism involves having AI models take turns as either “solvers” that tackle problems or “auditors” that check the others’ work. “One agent acts like law enforcement; the other agent acts like the citizen,” he says. During training, the models are rewarded depending on outcomes: Solvers that get caught cheating are punished, but so are auditors that fail to catch the cheaters. In situations where there’s a disagreement between auditors and solvers, the case can be sent to “trial”—perhaps a human expert will decide who is right. There is, Hazan admits, a risk that agents will collude with each other—as auditors have colluded with the businesses they’re meant to be auditing, or corrupt police officers have colluded with criminals. But that problem can be solved by rewarding the auditors well enough, so that being caught isn’t worth the risk (a lot easier in the world of AI training than the real one, where money for police salaries is tight), and by auditing the auditors using techniques akin to employing mystery shoppers. Hazan points to Nobel Prize–winning work by the economist Gary Becker, who introduced the idea of the “rational offender” in the 1960s. Criminals don’t break the law because they’re inherently bad people, he said. Instead, they do it when it’s the most rational choice given the incentives on offer. Crime makes sense only when the proceeds outweigh the potential punishment. Alignment shouldn’t be thought of as a property of a model, he argues, but as a descriptor of its behavior at a particular moment. “You are, like, a nice person,” he says. “Are you aligned? I don’t know. Probably you are aligned most of the time, but there could be a situation where you’re not.” One potential issue with applying mechanism design to AI: It relies on the assumption that AI agents behave rationally. Mechanism design has a mixed record with humans, who often fail to behave the way economic models say they should. It’s ironic: Economists have spent decades trying to reduce human behavior to mathematics, and now, in large language models, we’ve built mathematical systems that run on human language and could be just as inscrutable. AI models exhibit behaviors that seem more human than machine, and they can be swayed by subtle factors that are difficult to understand and explain. “Is it even reasonable to assume that they will respond to incentives?” asks Helen Qu, a research fellow at the Flatiron Institute, a nonprofit dedicated to advancing scientific discovery through computational methods, who has written about mechanism design and AI safety. “Obviously, AIs are not rational,” Morris says. “They’re just algorithms at work.” However, as economists have found with humans, treating them as if they’re rational could still be useful if it helps to predict and control their behavior. Qu sees mechanism design as just one layer in a “defense in depth” strategy for keeping AI models aligned—alongside interpretability, regulation, and training, not instead of them. But there are a lot of questions to be answered before the approach can be implemented in actual frontier models. It’s unclear, for instance, how these theories will apply to real models, or whether mechanisms designed for one task will carry over to another in practice. Models could find loopholes that no one anticipated. (Anthropic and OpenAI did not respond to requests for comment on whether they are working on this.) Whatever these mechanisms look like, they will ultimately have to slot into the biggest mechanism of all: the free market. “We play by the rules of economics,” says Hazan. A more aligned model may be good for the world, but if it’s slower or more expensive than a less aligned one, does our current system of rules and regulations actually incentivize any of the frontier labs to release it? “It’s a mechanism that’s responsible for a lot of prosperity for humankind,” he says of the market, “but it could be ultimately responsible for something very terrible.” Perhaps the entities really in need of a better mechanism design are the labs themselves.
