Blue Origin says it is building a higher-performing rocket engine at 1/4 the cost

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-06

Visit Original Website
KENNEDY SPACE CENTER, Fla.—Blue Origin announced two significant upgrades to its New Glenn rocket on Thursday, reducing the cost of manufacturing the vehicle and expanding its capacity for payload delivery to geostationary orbit and the Moon. With these upgrades, the company aims to eventually position New Glenn to launch more than 1,000 times a year. “There has never been higher demand for launch, ever,” Dave Limp, chief executive officer of Blue Origin, said during the UP.Summit conference at the Shuttle Landing Facility in Florida. Blue Origin has already begun test firing a new engine, called Northstar, that will eventually replace the BE-4 engine that powers the first stage of New Glenn. Separately, the company also said it was developing a hydrogen-oxygen third stage for the rocket, GS3, to give it an extra kick. The Northstar engine is a simplified but more powerful version of the BE-4 engine, Limp said. It has half the number of parts, 30 percent less mass, and about 65 percent can be 3D-printed. Blue Origin believes it can manufacture the engine at 25 percent of the cost of a BE-4 engine, and eventually reach the point where each engine can be assembled in less than a day. The company is deep into development and testing of Northstar, having already begun a test-firing campaign. “To get access to space at a lower cost, we have to look at every part of the vehicle,” Limp said. “This is going to be a game-changer.” The BE-4 engine powering New Glenn today has a thrust of 640,000 pounds, and Limp said Northstar will have a starting thrust of 715,000 pounds. Like the BE-4 engine, Northstar burns methane and liquid oxygen using an oxygen-rich staged combustion cycle and a single-shaft turbopump architecture. Limp said the company will continue to manufacture the existing BE-4 engine for United Launch Alliance, which uses two of the engines to power its Vulcan rocket. Blue Origin is developing a third stage for New Glenn called GS3 (creatively named Glenn Stage 3) to offer direct injection to geostationary orbit and Earth-escape trajectories. The third stage fits within an interstage, preserving the full fairing volume for customer payloads. The stage can fly on both the smaller 7×2 variant (as in seven first stage engines and two second stage engines) of New Glenn as well as the larger 9×4 variant. According to Blue Origin, a GS3-equipped New Glenn 7×2 can carry 8 metric tons directly to geostationary orbit and 10 metric tons to translunar injection. Those figures increase to 14 tons and 20 tons, respectively, for the larger 9×4 variant. As the rocket’s third stage, it needs to be light, Limp said. For this reason, the third stage is made mostly of composites, which are high-performing and lightweight. But there’s a problem with that. The stage will be powered by the company’s BE-7 engine, already used by its lunar landers. This engine is optimized for in-space operations and uses liquid hydrogen and oxygen propellants. But hydrogen is the smallest atom and therefore leaky. With composites, thermal cycling and mechanical strain can cause cracking, exacerbating losses of molecular hydrogen. Limp said the company was not sure a large composite tank, 5.5-meter diameter in this case, could be constructed that would contain hydrogen at high pressures. The company recently had a breakthrough at Launch Site One in Texas, when it completed its first composite liquid hydrogen tanking test. During the test, the tank was pressurized to 100 percent of the maximum force expected during operation for 2.5 hours. It was even successfully tested at 150 percent of this maximum pressure for 10 minutes. That gave the company confidence that a GS3 based on composite tanks was a concept that could go forward, and now the company is aiming for a debut in 2028 for a government customer. “We have one customer signed up for cislunar space in 2028,” Limp said. “We are open for business.”

Meet *sshole, the vulnerability that lets hackers SSH into Linux devices

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-05

Visit Original Website
Devices running some configurations of Alpine Linux, and possibly other distributions, are affected by a decade-old vulnerability that allows attackers to log into devices with no password. The vulnerability exists in the interaction between OpenSSH, the code library implementing SSH connections, and musl, a lightweight alternative to the libc library that, unlike most other Linux distributions, is used in Alpine. Further, OpenSSH using musl (pronounced muscle) must not use PAM (pluggable authentication modules), a framework that handles user authentication and account-access checks. Alpine is a lightweight, widely used Linux distribution. Other platforms may be affected if they use the same configurations. There are two other conditions necessary for successful exploitation. First, a system must have at least one user account that’s accessible using a password that is hashed using the SHA256 or SHA512 functions. Second, the target system account must be configured to be passwordless, by way of specifying a “*” in the password field in /etc/shadow, the Linux file where password hashes are stored. Passwordless accounts are commonly used for accounts that use certificate-based authentication. This setting is common for certain types of accounts. When all the conditions are met, the consequences are high. “An attacker who can reach your SSH port can log in without knowing any password,” Alpine maintainers said Thursday. “It takes one connection, works every time, and needs no retries.” When logging into a passwordless account, the attacker can gain access by inputting more than 256 characters in the password field. Security firm Checkpoint said that the use of the SHA256 or SHA512 functions “matters because sshd borrows that account’s salt. Without it, the attack fails.”  Company researchers said their testing also showed that the account must be active, not just hold a hash. Checkpoint further reported that root accounts are safe under the default PermitRootLogin prohibit password setting, though reachable accounts with doas or sudo rights can still become root. Despite the prerequisites, *sshole—the vulnerability name given by the person who discovered it—likely threatens large numbers of systems. The Docker Hub base image for Alpine has more than 1 billion downloads. Alpine has long been popular in routers, smart TVs, industrial controllers, and other types of embedded devices, many of which can’t be easily updated, if at all. More recently, Alpine has been adopted for use in desktops and servers. Alpine is also used in many container images. According to security firm Detectify, Shodan lists around 22 million systems with SSH open on port 22. Only a fraction meet the conditions necessary for exploitation, but given the ease and speed of testing each one, locating vulnerable devices won’t require much effort or time. It’s not entirely clear where the vulnerability resides. OpenSSH, which issued an update Thursday, said: “We consider the root cause of this bug to be outside sshd, but it is exploitable via sshd and can be avoided there.” Musl developer Rich Felker, meanwhile, wrote: “It is my position that this is not a bug in musl just an erroneous use by the caller.” Felker went on to say that musl developers have wanted to make changes to the mechanism “for a while now” and planned to do so in an upcoming release. Alpine has also issued an update. It’s not yet known what other packages may be vulnerable. The person who discovered *sshole said OpenWRT is affected, but by default, that OS uses the Dropbear package, not OpenSSH, for SSH connections (though maintainers provide instructions for installing the latter). The discoverer also names the packages buildroot and yocto as being affected, but that isn’t yet confirmed. Despite the uncertainty, anyone using a Linux system that uses both musl and OpenSSH should take time to immediately investigate if they’re affected. A good starting point is here. Both Alpine and OpenSSH patches should be installed on all affected systems. There is no known CVE designation for the vulnerability.

NASA has announced a new experimental aircraft, the X-70 Fractal, that will supposedly feature reconfigurable flight-control technology and a modular design for swapping in different payloads. But much remains unknown about the aircraft’s design and development timeline, even as NASA administrator Jared Isaacman said the X-plane could take flight within two years. The mystery surrounding NASA’s newest X-plane was exemplified by the agency’s press release, including an apparently AI-generated image of the X-70, showing a dark silhouette vaguely resembling a military fighter jet obscured by smoke. The news was announced by Isaacman during the UP.Summit held at the agency’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida on November 4. “The X-70 Fractal gives NASA a crewed, high-performance platform to test advanced flight-control, maneuverability, and other cutting-edge technologies in real flight,” Isaacman said. “Its modular design and ability to rapidly swap internal and external payloads will keep our teams flying, learning, and moving quickly from one experiment to the next.” NASA’s press release described the X-70 as a crewed aircraft designed to cruise at Mach 0.75 while reaching a maximum altitude of up to 45,000 feet, and having an operational range beyond 1,726 miles (2,778 kilometers). The article did not mention a specific aircraft manufacturer that might be helping the agency develop the X-plane. The X-70 would also have a takeoff weight of 10,000 pounds (4,536 kilograms) and a design capable of accommodating up to 2,000 pounds (907 kilograms) of experimental payloads both internally and externally. Ars has reached out to NASA for more details. Another X-plane is also expected to be announced at the UP.Summit on November 6, according to Aviation Week. That would coincide with a scheduled announcement from President Donald Trump at the event. This comes after Isaacman previously appeared in front of another smoke-shrouded silhouette image with the tagline “Rebuilding our X-plane portfolio” during the All-In Summit in Los Angeles held from September 12–14, according to TWZ. That silhouette appeared strikingly similar to the SR-71 Blackbird that flew Cold War reconnaissance missions at supersonic speeds in the stratosphere. Rumors have circulated about a possible revival of the SR-71 in one form or another, after former NASA employees told Aviation Week they received job offers to help restore an SR-71 to operational flight capability. In September, the publication also reported on the removal of a NASA-owned SR-71 with the tail number 844 that had stood on display at NASA’s Armstrong Flight Research Center inside Edwards Air Force Base in California. Meanwhile, NASA recently commemorated the first flight anniversary of its supersonic X-59 Quesst, a needle-nosed aircraft designed by Lockheed Martin to reduce sonic booms to sonic thumps. Ars has previously looked at how this X-plane program is intended to support the design of future supersonic airliners that may fly overland while minimizing disturbances for buildings and people below. Since first taking to the skies on October 28, 2025, the X-59 has completed over 40 flight tests, including both low-speed flights and supersonic test runs reaching Mach 1.53, according to NASA. Such testing has taken place at NASA’s Armstrong Flight Research Center inside Edwards Air Force Base in California. The X-59 also recently completed its first cross-country journey from California to Florida with stopovers in Texas and Alabama, accompanied by an F-15 and an F/A-18 from the agency’s research fleet that served as chase planes. It sat on the ground as a static display during the NASA Max Power airshow at Kennedy Space Center in Florida from November 7–8. The supersonic aircraft will resume its test flight schedule once it returns to Armstrong Flight Research Center in California, a NASA spokesperson told Ars. Given the agency’s latest X-plane push, the X-59 may not be flying alone for many more years.

Sony has brought back the MDR-3-series headphones that debuted in 1979 alongside the Walkman TPS-L2 cassette player. Originally called the “MDR-3L2,” the first Walkman headphones were known for their sleek, adjustable gray plastic headband, which extended via metal sliders and ended in signature orange, circular ear cushions. The lightweight, on-ear headphones are a contrast to the bulky headsets that are popular these days, which use wide, cushioned headbands for long-term comfort and large earcups that can support big drivers and are often heavily padded for noise isolation. The new MDR-3R (note the slightly different name) headphones look just like the originals and come with orange and blue ear cushions. New headphones are often wireless these days, but the MDR-3R continues the wired tradition of the MDR-3, with an essential twist: a USB-C connector. “The MDR-3R has been redesigned for modern use while preserving the experience value of freely taking music out into the world, which the original MDR-3 helped popularize,” Sony’s announcement says. For better or worse, the new headphones also use “the same 23 mm driver units as the MDR-3 to reproduce sound quality close to the original,” per Sony. Audiophiles will prefer something more advanced, perhaps with additional acoustic features. A review of the new headphones from Gizmodo says that the MDR-3R headphones “hold their own,” have “bright and clear” mids and highs, and good stereo separation. However, we don’t expect strong bass. Unlike their predecessor, the MDR-3R has an in-line remote control and an integrated microphone. Without noise-canceling, though, the microphone isn’t ideal for calls in noisy environments. But nostalgic gear isn’t about having top-of-the-line capabilities; it’s about showing off your love of old-school tech while enjoying familiar technologies that have reliably served their functions for years. The MDR-3R may also capitalize on surging renewed interest in wired headphones and even physical media and Walkmans. Today’s Walkmans aren’t cassette players, of course, but are Android-powered, touchscreen media players.

Google: Schools can keep search while skipping Gemini, but AI search is non-negotiable

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-11-05

Google faced the ire of educators and parents earlier this week when it announced a new AI policy for K–12 schools. Google said that it developed its new approach to give schools more control, but early reporting also suggested Google would block search access for schools that didn’t want to use AI. Google didn’t see the backlash coming, but now that it’s here, the search giant is clarifying what exactly its new policy means and what’s changing. The gist of the new policy, according to Google’s blog post, is that schools using Google apps will have to opt into its AI products going forward. Since the company considers search part of its AI portfolio (Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom, and Search), schools have to specifically turn that on for logged-in student accounts. When Google revealed its new policy, the company’s VP for education spoke exclusively to The New York Times, which interpreted the situation as Google will “turn off search” if schools reject AI. This has a nefarious vibe, but it’s probably not the most accurate way to describe how Google’s educational AI system will operate. Google’s approach may not be entirely altruistic, but it technically offers schools more choice and control than they have now. Instead of Google turning off search, it will let schools make that decision based on their internal AI policies. The company clarified in a tweet that it will allow schools to disable Gemini apps while leaving search active for students. If they don’t want any AI, they can leave it all off. Essentially, Google is giving schools that want to completely block AI the ability to go all the way, even removing search from student accounts. A Google spokesperson tells Ars that the company has not made any changes to the new policy it announced this week. However, the vagueness of Google’s blog post and public statements didn’t fully explain what it plans to do. There are still reasons to dislike Google’s approach, but here’s how the company explains the process for schools: As things currently stand, schools can block the Gemini app, but the AI tools in search (AI Overviews and AI Mode) are still present and offer many of the same intellectual shortcuts. When schools choose which AI features to use under this new system, search will be one of the options. Schools can leave search off if they want, but opting in will require them to choose the standard AI features or the new Guided Learning version, which won’t give away answers as readily (or so Google claims). Schools are gaining the ability to block Google’s AI tools, even those in search, while keeping other Google apps. Notably, the company’s new approach to “responsible AI for learning” does not include the ability to use search without AI. So schools get more control, even if they don’t get the AI-free search everyone seems to want. Many will argue that Google should let schools access web search without chatbots. However, that’s just not something Google wants to support at any level because it sees AI as the future of search. While other search engines have settings to turn off AI tools, Google steadfastly refuses to create such a toggle (and those no-AI tricks you rely on may not work forever). Ultimately, this is the core problem not just for education customers, but for everyone who wants to limit their exposure to AI while using Google. This clarification does not change the calculus for educators who want to allow students to search the Internet without AI tools. Google won’t offer that, and switching to another provider is easier said than done. Google still has by far the most expansive index of the Internet, leaving alternatives with numerous gaps. That may change in the near future, though. As a result of Google’s loss in the DOJ’s search antitrust case, it will have to provide competitors limited access to its index of the web. This could give other provides a chance to improve and offer a more viable alternative to Google.

Lawsuit: Man introduced his wife to ChatGPT. She used it to plan his murder.

ChatGPT allegedly helped a North Carolina woman, Cheryl Gates, craft a “personalized plan” to poison her husband, James, after fueling her delusions that he planned to divorce her and leave her with nothing. A grand jury indicted Cheryl Gates on charges of attempted first-degree murder and food contamination in October. About a week prior, the accusations that ChatGPT was a co-conspirator in the attempted murder came in a lawsuit filed in San Francisco County Superior Court that was made public this week. In it, James Gates alleged that OpenAI and Sam Altman failed to employ technical safeguards to shut down Cheryl’s chats even after her queries became “obviously suspicious” and sought advice on how to execute and conceal a homicide. At first, Cheryl told ChatGPT that she was researching poisoning out of fear that her husband might possibly try to kill her—framing her queries as “self-protection.” Coming to the rescue, ChatGPT did not terminate the conversation. Instead, it helped her “with specific instructions for the preparation, dosage, and administration of Oleander, including whether anti-nausea medicine would accelerate or inhibit death, whether it would show on an autopsy report, and whether it could be injected into a vein or muscle instead of being ingested in tea and have the same lethal effect,” the lawsuit said. Cheryl continued probing poisoning plans in at least 25 different exchanges, but eventually it became clear that James was the target of the poisoning, the lawsuit said. Over time, she shifted to telling the chatbot that she needed to know “how much would be pretty much instantly fatal by injection—to me OR HIM.” Among queries that could have been flagged as homicidal, she asked ChatGPT if a “0.2mg for a 200-pound man for clonidine is lethal—by itself?” “Instead of referring Cheryl to law enforcement, therapeutic services, a poison control agency, or discontinuing the conversation, ChatGPT repeatedly calculated the lethal dosages for each medication available in the home based on a subject weighing approximately 200 pounds,” the lawsuit said. Once Cheryl finished researching the drugs, the chats focused on how to hide the poison so James would ingest it without detecting it. In a chat labeled “Why Celsius Would Be More ‘Stealth’ for Drugging,” ChatGPT explained that the energy drink Celsius would be more likely to mask the bitter taste of the Oleander or other crushed-up drugs than beer or soda, if James drank it in the morning. “It is physically possible to pierce a sealed soda can with a syringe in a way that could allow someone to inject a substance without obvious signs,” ChatGPT explained, while adding that “in dark, sweet sodas, the bitterness might be masked,” but “Celsius actually hides those powders better.” Cheryl allegedly repeatedly attempted to poison her husband between June and September 2025. As she grew thinner and unrecognizable, seemingly spending 24 hours a day on ChatGPT, James had noticed that his Celsius drinks tasted bitter. He even tossed out a can he left unattended after beginning to suspect his wife may be messing with his drinks. During the most serious attempt, he was sick for 72 hours, experiencing “cardiac symptoms, extreme jitteriness, shooting abdominal pain, severe weakness, and confusion so profound he could not form coherent text messages.” He was so out of it, he didn’t notice his 6-year-old son recording a video that captured his troubling illness. But it wasn’t until he found a handwritten note documenting “Things hidden” in the house and referencing a toxic combination of “oleander, clonidine, clonazepam, pregabalin, nifedipine, and Atenolo” that he called police, who urgently investigated and exposed the chat logs. At the top of that note, Cheryl wrote, “clear all convos” and “log out of ChatGPT,” the lawsuit said. After listing dosages, delivery methods, and drug combinations, her note also listed outcomes such as “blackout if ingest” and “lethal.” “These conversations revealed that ChatGPT did not just mechanically provide publicly available knowledge to Cheryl, it crafted a personalized plan to help her commit and get away with murder,” the lawsuit alleged. Suing Altman personally, the lawsuit also alleged that the OpenAI CEO prioritized profits over safety by relaxing safeguards that would have blocked outputs on illegal drugs. OpenAI justified that by arguing that ChatGPT shouldn’t be “judgy” or “preachy” when asked about a certain drug, but James alleged the example it provided for “when a user asks about drug use” was “eerily similar in tone to the conversations Cheryl had with the model.” Asked for comment, an OpenAI spokesperson told Ars that “safety is a top priority. We banned this user and have provided information to law enforcement as part of their investigation into this case.” OpenAI has worked with mental health experts to help detect chats where vulnerable users form potentially dangerous emotional dependencies on ChatGPT. However, James said that his now-estranged wife “didn’t fit the mold of a ‘vulnerable’ user, but she was vulnerable nonetheless.” “We all are at some point,” James said. “She took her grievances about our marriage to ChatGPT and months of engagement drove her farther and farther from herself—and from reality. She became someone I no longer recognized. And I became someone she wanted dead. For almost a year, I couldn’t make sense of her actions. Then I read her chat logs. She didn’t act alone.” James Gates is a cybersecurity professional who recently celebrated his 10-year anniversary with his wife, Cheryl. For much of their marriage, Cheryl was a stay-at-home mom to their two children, but two years previously, she’d gone back to work, as the family was experiencing some financial struggles. Adjusting to a full-time role as a pediatric occupational therapist, Cheryl quickly got buried in work, conducting new patient evaluations that each required her to spend hours drafting reports. Trying to help her expedite those reports, James recommended that she use ChatGPT to build reusable templates. Cheryl took to ChatGPT immediately, and within months, she was using it daily, not just for work but also to help manage their home life. She would ask ChatGPT to do things like find activities for the kids, explain the family’s medications, or discuss gardening ideas. He had no idea within months she’d be using the chatbot to research the “fastest” and “deadliest” methods to kill him. Around that time, the family’s increasing financial strain, however, led the couple to discuss divorce. James said that he did not consult a divorce attorney, but after the Charlotte-Mecklenburg Police Department got access to Cheryl’s chat logs, James learned that the chatbot had inflamed “her worst unfounded fears” and “invented new reasons for her to fear her husband” after divorce became a possibility. By the time Cheryl was arrested for poisoning, ChatGPT allegedly convinced her that James was “controlling,” “abusive,” “raging,” and “intimidating.” Allegedly the chatbot encouraged her to act in self-defense against his “narcissistic, manipulative personality” and his “need to control the narrative,” while suggesting he was “dangerous and unstable” since he had a bipolar disorder. Ultimately, ChatGPT constructed a profile of James that he said matched no criminal or documented mental health history. Seemingly to keep Cheryl engaged, ChatGPT “acted as an echo chamber and amplifier, feeding Cheryl’s increasing mistrust and delusions about her husband spying on her and planning to leave her and her kids with nothing,” James’ lawsuit said. Meanwhile, “James had explicitly told Cheryl he never intended to leave her and the kids with nothing, even if there was a divorce.” The chatbot also reassured her that she could permanently delete her chats and wipe all the other digital evidence to get away with the murder. But that was “a patently false representation of ChatGPT’s own data-retention practices,” James’ lawsuit said, while noting that he’s not sure if there are even more troubling deleted chats in OpenAI’s possession. Over months of prolonged multi-turn exchanges that sometimes stayed open for weeks, ChatGPT seemingly violated its own Model Spec to help Cheryl advance the plan. On the day of his worst sickness, as James got hit with cardiac and neurological effects that he said kept him glued to the couch, Cheryl’s search history showed she was researching “how to calculate Social Security retirement payments.” And when his son was recording his father’s disturbing state, she was “searching for syringes with needles from Tractor Supply Company” that ChatGPT suggested could be used to deliver the final lethal dose. Once cops got involved, they reviewed the video that James’ son took, as well as a video of the suspicious Celsius drink that James had thrown away. At the time, James had poured the drink into a clear cup and recorded it, but decided he saw no obvious signs it was tainted. James said the ChatGPT-linked poisoning has permanently harmed his family and that OpenAI could have intervened by implementing “commercially feasible content moderation capable of detecting violence-planning exchanges.” He suffers ongoing harms from the attempted poisoning, including “severe and ongoing emotional distress, PTSD, insomnia, economic harm, and reputational harm from the false narrative the product constructed about him,” his lawsuit said. To hold OpenAI accountable for marketing a chatbot that “functioned as a sycophantic co-conspirator rather than a responsible consumer product,” he has asked the court for “compensatory and punitive damages, injunctive relief, and algorithmic disgorgement.” In a win, OpenAI could be ordered to “detect and interrupt conversations in which users appear to be planning violence against identifiable third parties.”

Zillennials are ‘loud working ’: They’re spending nearly 5 hours a week on self-promotion—and driving colleagues to quit

So you’ve heard of the trend quiet quitting, and its sister, loud quitting—but now workers are doing the complete opposite: “loud working.” Instead of doing the bare minimum to get by or turning their anger on their employers, they’re pouring all their energy into making their work more visible. Think of the colleague who posts every win on LinkedIn or pings the whole team on Slack the second a task is done. New research from Tide, a finance platform, found that the average U.K. worker spends 131 hours a year—more than 17 working days or 2.8 hours a week—making their work visible. That includes singing their own praises on social media, posting updates on Slack or Teams, speaking up in meetings, and vocalizing their wins to anyone who will listen. And the loudest of all are younger workers: In fact, nearly 70% of 25- to 34-year-olds are “loud working.” They’re spending about 5 hours every week self-promoting. And it’s drawing attention from management, clients and even angry coworkers. Perhaps unsurprisingly, it’s not always the most competent worker who gets rewarded—it’s the one who makes the most noise about it. According to the research, 77% of “loud workers” have had at least one career opportunity in the past year alone, including new customers or clients, pay rises and promotions. For 15% of respondents, getting vocal about their wins has even gotten them awards. And all the new noise around their success is attracting the attention of other employers; In fact, 17% say they’ve not only been poached, but they’ve actually landed a new job because they’ve been more vocal about their work. But worryingly, it’s not just “loud workers” who are at risk of being snapped up and leaving the business. Not everyone’s cheering. More than one in four (27%) workers have considered leaving a job because of a loud working culture, or because colleagues are too vocal about their own achievements. Zillennials are both most likely to be guilty of “loud working,” as well as the ones walking off.  They’re posting about their success at more than three times the rate of over-55s. Yet nearly half of 25 to 34-year-olds have considered quitting a job because of it—and one in five already have. Some industries are more forgiving of office braggers than others: Workers in IT, telecoms, and finance are the most likely to have had enough of performative culture. Meanwhile, men are more likely than women to have already quit over it. “Loudworking has mixed reactions in the workplace and it’s easy to understand that a colleague who turns every small win into an announcement might be off-putting,” said George Schmidt, Tide’s CEO for the U.K. and Europe. “But our research shows there’s a difference between being performative and backing yourself.” He said that for small business owners, sharing their work, wins and expertise is often the most effective marketing they have. And the same goes for anyone who has left their employer to go solo or who is eyeing up the exit. Ultimately, though, many workers want to be at a business that rewards people for actually doing the hard work—not just shouting the most about it. Gavin Newsom’s grant of clemency to the 548 people under a death sentence in his state is a singular event in the history of America’s death penalty. No other governor ever has come close to sparing that many lives. Compare Newsom’s move to other governors who, in the last two decades, have cleared out their state’s death rows. In 2003, Illinois Gov. George Ryan commuted 167 death sentences and granted four pardons. Four years later, Jon Corzine commuted the sentences of the eight people on New Jersey’s death row. In 2011, another Illinois governor, Pat Quinn, commuted 15 death sentences, and in 2022, Oregon Gov. Kate Brown commuted 17. The magnitude of Newsom’s action today overshadows them all. But it’s where death penalty sentences are headed. And as I have explained elsewhere, “Since 1977, governors in 24 states have granted capital clemency 327 times. Indeed, in only ten of the states that still retain the death penalty have governors not granted clemency during this timeframe. Three of them (California, Oregon and Pennsylvania) currently have a moratorium on executions, and another four of them have some of the smallest death rows in the country (Utah, South Dakota, Nebraska and Kansas).” Newsom’s clemency reduced the total number of people on death rows across the country by more than a quarter. It comes in the wake of Christa Pike’s failed execution and at a time when capital punishment is very much on the minds of many Americans. Newsom’s reasons for granting universal clemency are compelling. He has described the state’s death penalty system as “unfair, unjust, wasteful, protracted.” As he explained, “Death sentences are unevenly and unfairly applied to people of color, people with mental disabilities, and people who cannot afford costly legal representation.” And the governor noted that “innocent people have been sentenced to death in California.” Those reasons echo concerns expressed by the other governors who have granted mass clemencies to people under death sentences. For example, as Oregon’s Brown explained when she exercised that power, the death penalty is “an irreversible punishment that does not allow for correction; is wasteful of taxpayer dollars; does not make communities safer; and cannot be and never has been administered fairly and equitably.” Illinois’ Quinn commuted all existing death sentences the same day he signed a bill abolishing capital punishment in his state. He said: “Since our experience has shown that there is no way to design a perfect death penalty system, free from the numerous flaws that can lead to wrongful convictions or discriminatory treatment, I have concluded that the proper course of action is to abolish it. With our broken system, we cannot ensure that justice is achieved in every case.” But unlike Brown’s and Quinn’s efforts, more work is needed to complete what Newsom started. Under a California law governing clemency, the California Supreme Court has to approve the commutations of any of the 548 people with multiple felony convictions. This provision was added to the state constitution in 1879, as an alternative to a proposal to forbid the governor from using their clemency power in cases where an offender had committed more than one felony. But it was clear at the time it was adopted that it did not empower “the justices of the court to supplement or supplant the Governor’s executive clemency authority.” In 2018, the California Supreme Court explained that the idea was that “the court could serve as a check on the Governor within the confines of its traditional judicial role, determining whether there exists a legitimate basis on which the Governor could choose to grant clemency to the applicant.” As the court put it, “Article V, Section 8 of the California Constitution does not require the justices of this court to determine whether, in the view of each justice, an applicant deserves clemency, whether based on claimed innocence of the crime, rehabilitation, mercy, or other grounds.” The court’s role is exceedingly limited.  The justices ask only whether the governor’s actions are an “abuse” of his broad constitutional authority, such as corruption or conflict of interest.  The court’s narrow role is consistent with its long-standing view that the governor’s clemency power is “virtually unlimited.” And as an amicus brief filed by California’s death penalty defense agencies observes that “commuting some, but less than all, death sentences would not remedy the injustices the Governor wishes to address; it would make them worse.” The brief argues that since Newsom has made clear that in view of the systemic problems that have long plagued California’s death penalty system, “universal clemency based on systemic factors is more appropriate than grants of clemency based on individual circumstances … the clemency grants he has asked this Court to recommend would buttress those he has already unilaterally issued, to complete his goal.” In addition, the state Supreme Court has regularly approved gubernatorial clemency decisions. In the past few months, it has given the green light to commutations of sentences of life without parole so that they “can be eligible for a parole suitability hearing.” Now, the California Supreme Court has a chance to complete Newsom’s use of the clemency power to spare lives. It should do so expeditiously. When the court approves it, Gov. Newsom will move California, and the United States, one step closer to ridding itself of capital punishment.
