Think you can save the Hubble Space Telescope? NASA wants to know.

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-10-31

Visit Original Website
The Hubble Space Telescope still shines in the eyes of astronomers after 36 years in orbit, but it is not as nimble as it once was. Left alone, the observatory will plunge back into the atmosphere in the early-to-mid-2030s, closing the book on one of the most famous scientific instruments of the century. NASA last raised the orbit of Hubble in 2002, when the Space Shuttle Columbia visited the observatory for a servicing mission. The final shuttle servicing flight in 2009 did not reboost the telescope’s orbit, and with the retirement of the shuttle fleet two years later, Hubble has steadily lost altitude with no way to stave off eventual destruction. The space agency released a Request For Information (RFI) on Thursday asking commercial companies for ideas on how to reboost, service, or a controlled disposal of Hubble. Responses are due November 30. Specifically, NASA is looking for information about companies’ current and near-term capabilities for capturing Hubble and boosting it to an altitude of at least 373 miles (600 km), enhancing the telescope’s performance through, or guiding Hubble to a controlled reentry. “The requirement includes that the boost and/or servicing mission must not harm the HST and that this mission is expected to occur by 2032,” NASA wrote in the RFI. The contractor would be responsible for providing the launch vehicle, servicing spacecraft, mission operations, and “crew if applicable.” NASA is not committing to buying a Hubble rescue mission, but officials want to know who might be interested in mounting such a mission, how much it would cost, and which companies would have a realistic shot at reaching Hubble before it falls out of orbit and burns up on reentry. Orbital dynamicists currently predict that it will likely happen in 2033 or 2034 without intervention, but reentry forecasts can change based on solar activity. It took 24 years for Hubble, which lacks onboard propulsion, to glide from its 2002 altitude of 362 miles (583 km) to its current orbit some 292 miles (470 km) above Earth. A reboost to NASA’s requirement of 373 miles (600 km) would likely keep Hubble in orbit for at least 25 more years. You’ll be forgiven if you’ve heard this before. Jared Isaacman, a billionaire commercial astronaut, approached NASA in 2022 about a servicing mission to Hubble with a team of astronauts on SpaceX’s Dragon spacecraft. He proposed funding most of the mission, which would have reboosted Hubble by at least 30 miles (50 km). Engineers also studied using Dragon to attach a rescue pod onto Hubble with new gyroscopes to overcome problems with the telescope’s debilitated pointing system. NASA also released an RFI in 2022 for other teams to share how they might boost Hubble’s orbit. The agency received eight responses, including a joint robotic reboost proposal from Astroscale and Momentus to pair their satellite servicing technology and in-space transport capabilities. Agency officials announced in 2024 they would pursue a reboost mission after reviewing the responses. Mark Clampin, the head of NASA’s astrophysics division at the time, said the servicing plans from Isaacman and SpaceX “were really just notional concepts.” Fast forward to 2026. Isaacman is now NASA administrator, and the satellite servicing industry is maturing. Northrop Grumman launched the world’s most sophisticated robotic servicing satellite earlier this year in a partnership with DARPA, the Pentagon’s research and development agency. Smaller startups like Astroscale and Starfish Space have contracts for servicing and refueling demos with the US Space Force and NASA. So it’s not unrealistic to think the commercial industry could reboost and service Hubble by 2032, given enough time and resources. That was one of the lessons learned from NASA’s last-minute bid to rescue one of its smaller space telescopes, named Swift, from an impending reentry. NASA awarded a $30 million contract to a startup named Katalyst Space Technologies last year and gave the company just nine months to develop a mission to fly up to Swift, grab onto it, and propel it to a higher orbit, extending its useful lifetime. Remarkably, Katalyst built a rescue satellite and launched it by the deadline, and got within about 6 miles (10 km) of Swift. Ground teams decided not to move closer after problems with Katalyst’s power and reaction control systems. The Swift rescue attempt showed a servicing mission doesn’t have to cost billions of dollars, as one of NASA’s now-canceled servicing demo projects was predicted to cost. But Hubble is a significantly more expensive asset than Swift, so if NASA goes forward with a rescue mission, the agency would like to give a contractor more than $30 million and nine months. NASA does currently not have any funding allocated to procuring a Hubble reboost or servicing mission. “Hubble still has important science ahead of it, and we should be looking at every reasonable opportunity to keep it delivering for as long as possible,” Isaacman said in a NASA statement. “If there are innovative and cost-effective ways to extend the life of an asset already on orbit, we owe it to the American taxpayer and the scientific community to explore them. Extending Hubble’s life would mean more discoveries, more science, and more opportunities to expand our understanding of the Universe.” A crewed servicing mission like the one Isaacman envisioned in 2022 is unlikely to happen now. SpaceX aims to retire its Crew Dragon fleet by 2030. In a post on X, Isaacman wrote that he expects the new RFI will “draw interest from robotics companies.” NASA’s newer flagship observatories, sometimes described as successors to Hubble, are not actually one-for-one replacements. Hubble is tuned to observe the Universe in visible and ultraviolet light, while the Webb and Roman telescopes peer deeper into the infrared Universe. Astronomers will likely have to wait for the launch of NASA’s Habitable Worlds Observatory in the 2040s for an improvement on Hubble’s visible and ultraviolet vision. “Commercial industry has rapidly evolved in recent years to offer new capabilities for in-space satellite capture and servicing,” said Nicky Fox, NASA’s associate administrator for science. “Given the time we have until Hubble’s anticipated reentry, we are eager to explore the breadth of possibilities to boost Hubble to a higher orbit while we develop the flagship mission to follow.”

Flesh-eating screwworms spread to third state and are attacking pets

For a genuine fright this Halloween, you can turn your gaze to the southern border, where ravenous, flesh-eating maggots are festering and have taken to eating people’s pets. This week, the country’s New World Screwworm (NWS) infestation spread to a third state, Arizona, which joins New Mexico and Texas in being plagued by the horrifying parasites. To date, 74 animal cases have been identified in the US since the flies re-invaded the country in June. The flies lay hundreds of eggs in wounds and moist orifices of animals, and the resulting voracious maggots feast on their victims. The maggots resemble screws and tortuously bore and twist into living flesh, causing severe, sometimes fatal wounds, before dropping to the ground to develop into adults. Because of their ability to ravage livestock, the flies were eliminated from the country in the 1960s through aerial releases of sterile male flies, which thwart successful breeding. For years, they were held at bay using a biological barrier along the Darién Gap at the border of Panama and Colombia. But the barrier was breached in 2022, and the flies have made their way back to the US through Central America. While they are largely seen as a threat to the livestock industry, they can also devastate wildlife, domestic pets, and in rarer cases, humans. According to the latest data from Mexico’s Ministry of Health, the country has confirmed 806 cases of human screwworm infestations since the flies reemerged. Nine people died from the savage maggots. In the US, most of the 74 confirmed cases have been in livestock. But some of the cases have been in domestic pets, mainly dogs. Just this week, the US Department of Agriculture recorded four new cases in domestic dogs—which includes the new detection in Arizona—as well as a domestic cat in New Mexico. That brings the pet total to 10 cases since the flies reemerged. The US has also detected one case in a wild elk, which was reported last week. Arizona’s agriculture department said this week that the state’s index case was identified in a dog in Cochise County, which borders both New Mexico and Mexico. A local veterinarian examined the poor pup on October 26 and found a wound on the canine’s back that was “heavily infested with maggots consistent with NWS characteristics.” “We remain prepared and vigilant, and we urge all livestock owners, pet owners, and other animal industry partners to continue to monitor animals closely and report any unusual wounds consistent with NWS immediately,” the state said in an announcement. The USDA confirmed the infection and said it was working closely with Arizona on a joint response to the expanded infestation.

Along with the rest of the world, we at Ars are mourning Sir Patrick Stewart—the beloved award-winning actor best known as Captain Jean-Luc Picard in Star Trek: The Next Generation and Professor Charles Xavier in the X-Men franchise—who died yesterday at the age of 86. A statement released by Stewart’s representatives said: We announce with great sorrow the passing of Sir Patrick Stewart yesterday at his home in Los Angeles, in the loving care of his beloved wife Sunny, following a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer’s Disease and Lewy Body Dementia. He has been held in the warm embrace of family and friends, surrounded by live music, prayer, tears, laughter, and the kind of profound emotion that only he could inspire after a lifetime of devotion to us all. We will forever cherish the peaceful ease and reflection on his face, an enduring reminder of his invincible grace. Through the actor’s X account, the family has requested that, in lieu of flowers, memorial contributions be made to the Johns Hopkins Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center Fund and the Alzheimer’s Foundation of America Caregiver Resources. Born in Yorkshire, England, in 1940, the acting bug bit Stewart at the age of 12, when his English teacher handed out scripts for Shakespeare’s The Merchant of Venice and asked young Patrick to read the part of Shylock—a role the adult Stewart would later play for the Royal Shakespeare Theater (RSC), among many other parts. His signature bald head emerged early, around 19, thanks to alopecia. He briefly worked as a cub reporter and a furniture salesman before winning a scholarship to the Bristol Old Vic Theater School, and he landed his first professional role in 1959, playing a marooned pirate in a production of Treasure Island for the Lincoln Repertory Company. Stewart worked regularly in theater and on British television, and his big break came in his late 40s. The actor was conducting master classes on Shakespeare at the University of California, Los Angeles, when a TV producer developing TNG heard him lecture and thought he would make a perfect captain for the Enterprise-D. Creator Gene Roddenberry wasn’t initially keen on the choice, but Stewart’s audition changed his mind. The rest is television history. On Instagram, fellow thespian Sir Ian McKellen—who first met Stewart when both were young actors with the RSC and co-starred as Magneto in the X-Men films—recalled the day Stewart told him about the Star Trek offer. “’But Patrick, think of your stage career in the UK,'” McKellen recalled saying. “‘Do you really want to risk—getting lost in Hollywood for years and years?’ Well, he took the risk and thank goodness for us all that he did.” Stewart himself never expressed anything but pride in the role. “For huge numbers of people, I will always be Jean-Luc Picard, and that’s fine,” he told The Independent in 2003. “I really believe we created an admirable individual.” TNG ended in 1994, but it wasn’t long before Stewart created another iconic role, starring as Professor Charles Xavier in 2000’s X-Men, alongside McKellen as Magneto. He reprised the role in six more films and voiced the character in multiple video games. He was a serious actor who never took himself too seriously, relishing Shakespeare, Ibsen, and Beckett but also throwing himself into comedic TV appearances on Saturday Night Live, The Daily Show (he was a “special correspondent” for several segments in 2012), and Sesame Street. Who could forget his timeless “Soliloquy on B,” channeling Hamlet? Or his daily readings of Shakespearean sonnets that helped us all get through the COVID-19 pandemic? He voiced Napoleon in 1999’s Animal Farm, CIA Deputy Director Avery Bullock on American Dad and, earlier this year, Sir Nitchfield in the delightful Sheep Detectives. In between acting gigs, Stewart found time for charity work, advocating for such causes as domestic violence prevention and PTSD awareness among members of the armed forces, drawing on his own childhood being raised by an abusive father. All of this made Stewart one of the most beloved actors of our time. Tributes to Stewart are all over social media today, as celebrities and regular folk alike reflect on everything the actor’s work has meant to them over the years, including these heartfelt words from his lifelong friend McKellen: Before everything else, Patrick was defined by being born and bred in Yorkshire, the proud county in the north of England which he loved to the end. You could hear it in his accented voice and in the stern honesty with which he told you what he thought and felt. Yorkshiremen have no truck with half-truths or avoidance of big issues. He wore his heart on his sleeve, which made him a man to rely on, to admire and in my case, love. Ars staffers share their own reminiscences about Stewart and what he meant to them below. Boom Supersonic CEO Blake Scholl recently declared “it’s time for a renaissance in energy and in supersonic flight” as the supersonic airliner company unveiled a Colorado factory dedicated to making gas turbines for AI data centers. But Boom may be facing turbulence after early backers for both ventures have questioned the company’s delivery timeline or backed out entirely. One of the company’s first customers, United Airlines CEO Scott Kirby, has expressed doubts about Boom’s original plan to deliver the first Overture supersonic airliner in 2029. Kirby’s latest frank assessment came during an interview with the Office Hours: Business Edition podcast that appeared on October 28, 2026. “‘29 is their official timeline,” Kirby told podcast host Monica Langley. “It’s not going to happen in ‘29—it’s going to take longer.” Boom first made headlines for flying its XB-1 demonstrator aircraft in 2024 and 2025 to demonstrate a “boomless cruise” concept that harnesses the Mach cutoff phenomenon. Such a feat involves flying just beyond supersonic speeds at higher altitudes under specific atmospheric conditions, so that the aircraft’s shockwaves refract upward and minimize the impact on the ground. The company’s plan to revive supersonic air travel has been backed by the Trump administration via efforts to ease regulations. In June 2026, the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) proposed a new rule overturning a long-standing ban on commercial supersonic flights over the United States, following an executive order from President Donald Trump. Boom’s press releases have previously described achieving aircraft certification and carrying the first paying customers by 2029. The company has conditional agreements with American Airlines, Japan Airlines, and United Airlines involving non-refundable deposits of unknown amounts that would allow the airlines to fully purchase the Overture aircraft once it becomes commercially available. But United CEO Kirby pointed out that Boom still has “a lot of hurdles to overcome” while developing Overture and getting the supersonic airliner certified by the FAA. “So it’s not a guarantee that they’ll overcome,” he cautioned during the podcast interview, even though he praised Scholl and the company in general. This follows Kirby’s earlier comments back in March that put the chances of Boom’s Overture airliner entering commercial service at “50-50.” Ars has reached out to Boom for comment. Although Boom completed building the factory intended for production of the Overture airliner in 2024, the facility located at Piedmont Triad International Airport in Greensboro, North Carolina, has mostly sat dormant since that time. That has raised questions for North Carolina state leaders who provided more than $50 million in taxpayer funding to help build the Overture factory. Since October 2025, NASA has separately been doing test flights involving the experimental X-59 with a long, needle-like nose designed to reduce the sonic booms from supersonic flight. But this is a completely different approach than Boom’s use of the Mach cutoff flights. Much of Boom’s recent focus has been on developing natural gas turbines to power AI data centers as an additional revenue stream. On October 22, the company unveiled another factory in Centennial, Colorado, dedicated to producing a 42-megawatt natural gas turbine called Superpower for data centers. The Trump administration’s public support of Boom was once again evident as US Secretary of Energy Chris Wright participated in the event alongside Colorado Governor Jared Polis. Boom described its new Superpower factory as making turbine parts while still undergoing construction. It aims to deliver the first gas turbines to AI data center customers in mid-2027 while ramping up annual production to 25 turbines in 2028 and 50 turbines by 2029. Boom’s detour into powering data centers coincides with many tech companies prioritizing natural gas as the primary power source to get their projects up and running. Some data center developers are even using “mobile gas generators strapped to semitrucks” or “aeroderivative turbines originally designed for aircraft and warships,” according to the market intelligence platform Cleanview. However, Boom CEO Scholl also acknowledged the recent loss of a $1.25 billion deal that would have seen data center developer Crusoe purchase 29 of Boom’s gas turbines, in a post on the social media platform X on September 25. Crusoe has been involved in building Texas data centers for Oracle, Google and Microsoft that draw upon various power sources including natural gas and renewables. Scholl said Boom “will be delivering about 250MW of Superpowers next year to other sites” but did not specify the AI data center customers that may be using the gas turbines. Still, Boom has characterized the development of a gas turbine for data centers as being synergistic with its design of the Symphony engine for the Overture airliner. During the October announcement, it also described having completed a prototype turbine core, called Sprint Core, that is intended to eventually power both the Superpower gas turbine and Symphony engine for the airliner. That turbine core is set to undergo testing at the Colorado Air & Space Port, with Boom planning to fire the turbine for the first time before Thanksgiving. That would be a concrete development milestone, but the company still has much to do in proving it can get both its AI data center and supersonic airliner ventures off the ground.

An official extension for Anthropic’s Claude model was backdoored and threatened as many as 5,000 developers’ machines, multiple sources reported Friday. The compromised package was a VS Code extension available in the marketplace of Cursor, a maker of AI-assisted developer tools, the company said Friday. The malicious version, 2.1.313, subsequently affected “at most 5,000 users,” presumably when they either downloaded it or their systems automatically fetched and installed it. Anthropic pushed version 2.1.314 several hours after the previous version became available on Cursor, according to independent researcher Kevin Beaumont. The code was written to work on Windows, macOS, and Linux. University of Bristol’s Prof Michelle Spear looks at how your brain could be responsible for things that go bump in the night, not ghosts. A version of this article was originally published by The Conversation (CC BY-ND 4.0) It’s three in the morning. You’ve woken from a dead sleep because you’ve heard a thud in the darkness or a floorboard creaking downstairs. Or perhaps you’ve woken because you have the unmistakable sensation you’re not alone. During daylight, these phenomena might be dismissed as the heating, your neighbours or the sounds of an old house settling. But in the middle of the night, they can feel a tad more sinister. Your mind might immediately start reeling, jumping to the worst-case scenario – wondering if you’ve been broken into, or if you’re being haunted by some supernatural force. In reality, the explanation is far more simple. Things really do go bump in the night. But occasionally, the culprit is closer to home than we might imagine. Falling asleep is not the neurological equivalent of switching off a light. The brain moves gradually from wakefulness into sleep, accompanied by changes in sensory processing, muscle activity and awareness of the world around us. But these changes don’t always occur in perfect synchrony. So for brief periods, characteristics of sleep and wakefulness can overlap. The results can be surprisingly spooky. If you’ve ever been on the verge of sleep only to feel that you’ve fallen off a kerb, missed a step or dropped through the bed, you’ve probably experienced a hypnic jerk. Also known as a sleep start, this is a sudden involuntary contraction of one or more muscles as we move into the earliest stages of sleep. It’s an example of how the transition between wakefulness and sleep isn’t always a smooth one. Hypnagogic experiences can also involve vivid sounds, images or sensations. So as you’re on the edge of sleep, you may think you hear footsteps or whispers – or you may even think you see a figure in the room. Exploding head syndrome – perhaps the most alarming name for a harmless sleep condition – is another example of how the boundaries between sleep and wakefulness can become blurred. As someone is falling asleep, or occasionally waking, they suddenly perceive an enormous noise: an explosion, gunshot or slamming door – yet nothing in the outside world has made a sound. Its precise cause remains uncertain. But the phenomenon demonstrates something important: hearing a sound and a sound actually occurring are not quite the same thing. The conscious experience of hearing is constructed by the brain. So neural activity generated without an external sound can still produce an auditory experience that seems entirely real. The brain doesn’t even need to be asleep for this to happen. If you’ve ever thought you’ve heard someone call your name – only to turn around and find no one there – you’ve been tricked by your brain. Another possible explanation for the sounds we think we hear at night involves source monitoring. This is the brain’s ability to distinguish events generated internally from those coming from the outside world. Thoughts, memories and imagined speech are internally generated, yet normally retain the quality of being ours. But occasionally, that attribution goes awry – allowing something generated within the brain to feel as though it came from somewhere else. Around sleep, that boundary becomes particularly interesting. Another common – but frightening – experience that can happen at night is waking up with the sudden feeling that someone else is in the room. This experience is usually the result of sleep paralysis. During rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, when most of our vivid dreaming occurs, the brain temporarily suppresses activity in most muscles. This protective mechanism, known as REM atonia, prevents us from physically acting out our dreams. But during sleep paralysis, our waking awareness returns while this physical paralysis persists. You know where you are, but cannot move. Yet elements of dream perception can remain active. This is why you may have experienced the sensation that someone – or something – is in the room with you, despite clear sensory evidence of the contrary. One clue as to why this happens comes from experiments examining how the brain distinguishes itself from others. In one study, participants controlled a robotic device positioned in front of them, while a second robot, which was placed behind them, reproduced these movements by touching the participants’ backs. But when researchers introduced a slight delay between the movement and the touch, some participants began to feel as though somebody else was touching them. Some even sensed another person standing behind them. While this experiment doesn’t explain every felt presence that can happen during sleep paralysis, it does reveal something remarkable: the distinction between ourselves and others is something the brain has to construct. Usually, movement, touch and our sense of body position agree about what belongs to us. But if that relationship is disrupted, an internally generated sensation can begin to feel like it originated elsewhere. Now picture this happening in a dark bedroom, when you’re partly awake yet still on the edge of dreaming and unable to move. The sensation that somebody else is there becomes rather less mysterious. Perception is not a passive recording of the world. It combines incoming sensory information with expectation and previous experience to construct the most likely explanation. But at the boundary between sleep and waking, this process can become less reliable. We may hear sounds that were never made, experience fragments of dreams intruding our bedroom or interpret sensations arising from our own bodies as the presence of somebody else. Applebee’s has fixed a security flaw that allowed anyone to access the personal information of its registered customers, after a researcher found a password for an internal database inside Applebee’s mobile app, TechCrunch has learned. The popular restaurant chain confirmed on Thursday that it had taken action to address the security issue to prevent any unauthorized access to its customer database. TechCrunch withheld publication of this story until the bug was confirmed to be fixed. Robert Wiggins, a U.K.-based cybersecurity researcher at What Security, discovered the security lapse earlier this month. Wiggins is no stranger to discovering security issues, including exposed or sensitive information that should not be publicly accessible. For this latest discovery, Wiggins used AI to help find the credential buried inside thousands of lines of source code in Applebee’s Android app, which has more than a million downloads on Google Play. The credential allowed access to the company’s customer database. Storing a password or private key in software, known as hardcoding credentials, is one of the most severe types of security vulnerabilities because anyone with the app can extract the key and use it to log in to a remote system as if they were an authorized user. Wiggins said that the hardcoded credential could have also allowed a malicious attacker to modify customer data in the database. TechCrunch verified Wiggins’ findings by downloading and decompiling Applebee’s Android app to examine its source code and confirm that it contained the exposed credential. We also created an Applebee’s account with dummy account data, which allowed Wiggins to retrieve our information from the company’s database. The exposed database contained customer names, email addresses (including whether or not the customer verified their email), dates of birth, phone numbers, and ZIP codes. The data also included veteran status, the number of times customers had logged in, when their accounts were created, and the IP addresses of their most recent log-ins, which can be used to reveal the person’s approximate location. Wiggins was unable to alert Applebee’s or its parent company, Dine Brands, as neither have ways to report security issues. Wiggins asked TechCrunch for help in disclosing the security issue to the company. A spokesperson for Dine Brands, who did not provide their name, said in an emailed statement on Friday: “We appreciate TechCrunch and the security researcher for bringing this matter to our attention. Our security team promptly launched an investigation and has not currently found any evidence of unauthorized access.” When asked by TechCrunch, the spokesperson would not say if the company has server logs that it can review to determine whether anyone accessed the database, or if it has any logs at all. “Our investigation is ongoing, and we will take appropriate steps based on our findings,” the company added. It’s unclear how many customers were affected by the data exposure. Wiggins said that figuring out the number of customers in the database would have required the AI model to unnecessarily access potentially every customer record. Applebee’s had at least 8.5 million customers enrolled in its rewards program as of last year, though the company would not provide a number of how many customers had their personal information exposed by this security flaw. TechCrunch asked the company if it would alert customers or U.S. regulators about the incident, including whether it would file a disclosure with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, as publicly traded companies are required to do for material cybersecurity incidents. We also asked the company how long the exposed credential had been in the Applebee’s app, but we did not get a response. We’ll update this article if the company responds. It’s estimated that more than 7,500 people have stood on its peak, reaching an astonishing 29,031 feet above sea level in Nepal, typically climbing up the mountain’s south side. But Elizabeth Chai Vasarhelyi and Jimmy Chin’s documentary Everest: The Other Side shifts focus to the mountain’s north face. It is here that two people, ski mountaineers Jim Morrison and Hilaree Nelson, attempted to do something that no human being had done before. Morrison and Nelson met in 2015 on an expedition to Makalu, the world’s fifth-highest mountain, in the Mahalangur Himalayas. The pair developed a friendship before beginning a romantic relationship in 2017, bonding over their shared sense of adventure. “There’s a connection I don’t feel anywhere else…I love how it makes me feel alive,” Nelson says about ski mountaineering in the new film, which hit theaters on Oct. 30 following its premiere at the Venice Film Festival. Together, Morrison and Nelson conceived a plan: They would climb to the top of Everest’s north face, and once they’d reached the summit, become the first to ski down it. It was the ultimate proposal for a pair of adventurous thrill-seekers, but one wrong move could result in disaster. They never got the chance to take the risk together, though: Disaster struck before they embarked on the journey, a tragedy the directors capture in devastating detail in the new film. Vasarhelyi and Chin are drawn to stories of people who accomplish extraordinary things, and they set out to capture them in a manner that fully captures their feats, like in Free Solo, Meru, and The Rescue. Everest: The Other Side is playing in both regular format and IMAX cinemas, the latter of which takes particular advantage of the breathtaking shots the filmmakers were able to capture, offering a sense of how epic Everest is, and how dangerous. Though most people don’t participate in extreme sports, Vasarhelyi believes there’s something inherently relatable about these feats. “I think it’s fundamental to the human paradox, which is this ability to dream audaciously, and then the mentality, psychology, mania—whatever you want to call it—to actually have the grit, determination, and diligence to get it done. I think everyone can identify with that,” she tells TIME. Hilaree Nelson, a National Geographic Explorer and the 2018 National Geographic Adventurer of the Year Cavan Images / Alamy Stock Photo Vasarhelyi and Chin intended for Everest: The Other Side to capture Morrison and Nelson’s historic feat. But on Sept. 26, 2022, tragedy struck. Nelson and Morrison were climbing nearby Mount Manaslu in Nepal when an avalanche swept down the slope, killing Nelson. It was a devastating moment captured on camera, one that had both the filmmakers and Morrison questioning whether they should continue with their journey, and the film, at all.“We always knew we were making a film about Jim and Hilaree and their attempt to ski and climb the direct north face, but these films for us always had to have something deeper. The part that stayed true throughout was this extraordinary love story,” says Chin. Morrison had experienced tragic loss before. In 2011, he lost his wife Katie and their two children in a plane crash. “I knew about Jim’s history and the loss that he’d been through, and how he had moved through that loss and found the strength and courage to fall in love again with Hilaree. That was extremely moving to me,” says Chin. “When we lost Hilaree, we saw that again in real time. We were living it with him.” Together, they decided that Chin, a longtime friend of the couple and an experienced climber, would step in as Morrison’s climbing partner, replacing Nelson. “Jimmy and I fundamentally see this differently," says Vasarhelyi of whether they would continue making the film. “It was such a tragedy when Hilaree passed, and real life has to be lived in that moment. With the decision of Jimmy stepping in as Jim’s only climbing partner, my professional interest came into direct conflict with my personal responsibilities.” At the time of filming, Vasarhelyi and Chin were married with two children. The couple divorced in 2025. “The question was, ‘Do you want to make that film?’ It was going to be heavier. It had to be very candid, which all our films are, but this was requiring us to go one step further in this investigation. There was also the reality of, how do you wrangle the story that was conceived one way into something that could both honor her legacy as well as honor this incredible journey that Jim would go on?” says Vasarhelyi. Ultimately, it was Morrison who made the decision to continue filming. “In the beginning, I didn’t want to keep going to the mountains. I was devastated by the loss of Hilaree,” he says. Initially, it looked like the quest to ski mountaineer the north face of Everest was over, and there was a seven-month break in filming. “Over time,” Morrison explains, “the inner fire and the passion for this project Hilaree and I shared together became a driving force in my life. I began to call [Chai and Jimmy] to say, ‘I’m going to go back into the mountains. I’m going to go on another expedition and push myself to complete this goal for us and for her legacy and her story.” With Chin as his climbing partner and Vasarhelyi and Chin continuing to document the adventure, Everest: The Other Side would go on. Instead of a story of a couple facing this incredible physical feat together, it became a story of processing grief and overcoming tragedy. Chin explains why the north side of Everest, the more challenging route up the mountain and an extremely steep, 9,000-ft. face, holds such appeal. “In terms of pure technical difficulty, it’s really challenging. Because of those challenges and because it’s a direct line, it’s really appealing to climbers.” He continues: “When you’ve spent a lifetime in the mountains, you build experience to take on more and more difficult climbs, and as a ski mountaineer, more challenging ski descents. This is the ultimate line on the tallest mountain on Earth that literally splits the biggest face on Everest.” The 2024 expedition involved dozens of people, including climbers, camp managers, cooks, and cinematographers. The set-up involved three different camps. The climb would start at 20,000 ft. and go up 9,000 ft. to the top of the mountain. An advanced basecamp would be established at 18,100 ft., as well as three additional camps, with one right below the entrance of the Hornbein Couloir, the most challenging and steepest part of the climb. To accomplish such a challenging climb, the climbers would do rotations, going up the mountain and back down, in an effort to build up red blood cells and acclimatize themselves to the changes in altitude as well as to bring equipment up the mountain. The process involves pushing the body to its limits, over and over again, before returning to base camp and going again. “It’s hard to sleep at 18,000 ft., so it becomes this game of acclimatizing and getting all your equipment, the rope and all the oxygen cylinders and the sherpa up the mountain,” says Morrison. The expedition took seven weeks, and this time there were no injuries or unforeseen weather circumstances. On Oct. 15, 2025, they arrived at the peak. Jim Morrison, Jimmy Chin, and their team of climbers—Carla Perez, Esteban Mena Yanez, Pasang Sona Sherpa, Pemba Sherpa, Roberto Morales, Erich Reopke, Nigma Tenjing Sherpa, Pemba Gelje Sherpa, Pur Bahadur “Yukta” Gurung, and Tenging Gyaljen Sherpa—reached the summit. It wasn’t over yet: Morrison still intended to be the first person to ski down Everest’s north face. “We got to the summit about 2 p.m.,” recalls Morrison. “We spent about 45 minutes on top. Going down at least means more access to oxygen. “As I went further down the mountain, I was able to recover a lot quicker, and as my physical endurance wore away, I got into a more oxygen-rich environment, which helped keep me going and able to achieve this.” Morrison explains his feelings as he reached safety as “a tidal wave of emotions.” He recalls: “I just totally broke down. Israel’s election this week was so close that neither of the contending coalitions—Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s or the opposition, led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot—may emerge with the majority needed to govern. The question now is this: Does it matter which side ultimately wins? It depends on the subject. When it comes to whether Israel resumes its war against Iran, the election’s outcome will make some difference. As for curbing the violence of Jewish settlers in the West Bank, it might have a little bit of impact. On the war in Gaza, there is no daylight between the coalitions. And on the prospect of settling the broad conflict between Jews and Palestinians, the election uncloaked an aspect of Israeli politics that will likely worsen tensions, regardless of the final vote count. The key factor is this. Hamas’ murderous raid of Oct. 7, 2023; Israel’s wildly excessive retaliation; and the inability of outside parties to impose an arrangement that ends the war, empowers a peaceful Palestinian Authority, and repairs the devastation in Gaza—all of these acts of ultraviolence and neglect have frozen Israeli politics in a state of seemingly permanent trauma. Hamas will not disarm until Israel withdraws from Gaza; Israel will not withdraw until Hamas disarms. Both positions are, in their way, understandable. Meanwhile, the vast majority of Israelis—even those who were once lefties or pacifists—consider it suicidal to so much as think about surrendering territory to Palestinians, much less negotiating a separate Palestinian state. One surprising outcome of the election made this fact all too clear. Israel’s Arab parties won 19 parliamentary seats—almost twice as many as the 10 they won in the prior election. If either of the two main Jewish candidates—Netanyahu or Eisenkot—invited the Arabs to join his coalition, he would gain more than enough seats to win a majority and thus lead the government. (Netanyahu’s bloc won 53 votes, and Eisenkot’s 48; it takes 61—half of the Knesset’s 120 seats, plus 1—to form a majority.) However, neither politician is likely to do that. Netanyahu has no desire to court the favor of Arabs, and even if he did for the sake of retaining power, he would probably lose as many far-right partners from his coalition as he would gain. Eisenkot, while not quite as extreme on the matter, promised before the election not to form coalitions with parties that don’t formally declare Israel to be a Jewish state—a stance that was widely (and accurately) taken to mean an oath not to partner with Arab parties. The Arab parties do not represent the roughly 5 million Palestinians in Gaza or the West Bank; those residents of occupied territories lack the right to vote in Israeli elections at all. Rather, they were supported by the 2.1 million Arabs who live in Israel proper—and almost all of them are Sunni Muslims, Christian Arabs, or Druze. Over the years, Israelis have fended off charges that they live in an apartheid state by pointing to the equal rights held by these Arabs. Yet, in a season when the Arab parties have won enough seats to make them potential kingmakers, the Jewish parties—their leaders, and to a great extent their constituents—have decided, at least for now, that they’d rather forgo certain victory if it meant giving Arabs positions in the government. This blatant denial of political power to Israeli Arabs, even when they’ve earned it through peaceful democratic means, would further isolate Israel from its allies—and could radicalize Israeli Arabs who until now were content with working within their country’s political system. The double standard has always been there, but now it’s too obvious to pretend away or disguise. The question of whether the election will make a difference when it comes to Jewish settlers’ violence against Palestinians in the West Bank is more complicated. Eisenkot has spoken out against the government’s tolerance—in many instances blithe encouragement—of the violence. If Netanyahu is thrown out of power, so will his main henchmen on this issue, Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich, the minister of national security and the finance minister, respectively, who believe that Gaza and the West Bank should be formally annexed. Netanyahu—who has been prime minister, on and off, for 19 years, longer than any other in Israel’s 78 years as a nation-state—has clearly stated that he wants to resume full-scale war on Iran for the purpose of changing its regime. Eisenkot has dismissed regime change as a fantasy and has said he’d prefer to sway Tehran’s behavior—away from supporting terrorist groups and developing nuclear weapons—through economic pressure. Still, Netanyahu remains prime minister until the election is settled, and if he decides to start bombing Iran again, and if Iran bombs Israel in return, Eisenkot—the former top general of Israel’s military—would not likely oppose further retaliation against Iran. Some speculate that under those circumstances, he might accept calls to form a “unity government,” joining the two coalitions. It would be a tenuous, probably short-lived government, since the two leaders—and their followers—hate each other, but it would presumably be a popular move for the short term. The American president has said he might start bombing again after next week’s midterm elections—a telling qualifier. He wouldn’t do so before the midterms because he knows that the war is unpopular and that its resumption could wreck the Republicans’ already-slim chance of hanging on to the House—and possibly to the Senate as well. If the Republicans lose, especially if they lose big, will he take that as a sign to steer away from Middle East wars, as he long ago promised voters he would do? Will a Democratic Congress vote to forbid him from resuming attacks on Iran (as the narrowly Republican-controlled Congress almost did)? Will Trump abide by that vote or ignore it as a violation of his Article 2 powers? In the interim, Netanyahu would remain prime minister, but his supporters would form only a minority of parliament. This gridlock could persist for months. The stalemate would hobble the state, which might compel Netanyahu and his opponents to make some kind of deal, either with each other or with the Arab parties. Or maybe they won’t be able to pull off such a feat. The divisions in Israeli politics and society may be too severe to mask—and any deal could aggravate tensions among the coalitions’ constituents. The region’s wars have hit a lull in recent weeks, but they are far from over. The dynamics unleashed by the elections—Israel’s and ours—could extend the calm or recharge the violence. Donald Trump Sure Is Corrupt. If Only There Were a Constitutional Remedy for That.

Donald Trump Sure Is Corrupt. If Only There Were a Constitutional Remedy for That.

Visit Original Website
This is the fifth in a series of articles, “Try, Try Again?,” drawn from my new book—Great and Dangerous Offences: The Case For (and Against) Impeaching Trump a Third Time—due out in early 2027. The most common ground for impeachment throughout Anglo-American history has been corruption, the misuse of the status and powers of office for private gain, either for oneself or for one’s family, friends, or political allies. Both Parliament and the American Framers abhorred official corruption and made it impeachable because it undercuts the basic contract between rulers and the ruled—that the rulers will not place their private interests, whether in wealth or increased power, over the interests and well-being of the nation. Donald Trump has broken this covenant by repeatedly, corruptly accepting payments and gifts from foreign powers, thereby violating the foreign emoluments clause and betraying the national interests of the United States. This conduct is a clear impeachable offense. Corruption can take many forms, from simple bribery to complex networks of exchanges of money, commercial concessions, and political favors. An especially insidious form of corruption, one especially to be feared from a monarch or president, is the sale of the nation’s interests to a foreign power. The American Framers repeatedly mentioned corruption as being, next to treason, the most necessary ground for presidential impeachment. George Mason spoke of the “corruptibility” of presidents as a primary justification for impeachment. Gouverneur Morris specified “corruption” as one of the “few” offenses for which he would favor impeachment. Among the definitions of impeachable conduct provisionally approved early in the Constitutional Convention were “treason, bribery, or corruption” and then “treason or bribery.” “Bribery,” a specific form of corruption requiring a quid pro quo exchange of favors for something of value, is part of the final constitutional definition of impeachable conduct. Late in the convention, the delegates adopted the additional phrase “or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors” in important part to expand the reach of impeachable corruption beyond technical bribery. The Framers’ fear of corruption is also evident in the Constitution’s emoluments clauses. The domestic emoluments clause bars presidents from taking anything beyond their established salaries from either Congress or the states. The foreign emoluments clause prohibits all federal officials from accepting gifts of valuables, office, or title from any foreign country without express congressional consent. Critically, unlike bribery as traditionally defined in the criminal law, the prohibition on emoluments is not conditional on any corrupt bargain by the public official. Likewise, emoluments are barred regardless of whether the official takes or refrains from any act whatever. The Framers understood that, even for the most virtuous, receipt of favors creates a predisposition to do favors in return. Once that disposition exists, it is only a tiny step to corrupt action. Therefore, mere acceptance of an emolument, without any accompanying promise or reciprocal official act, violates the Constitution. Violation of the foreign emoluments clause is impeachable corruption. At the Philadelphia convention, Morris several times insisted on the need for impeachment in the case of a president in the pay of a foreign power. At the Virginia ratifying convention, Edmund Randolph said explicitly that “there is another provision against the danger … of the President receiving emoluments from foreign powers. If discovered, he may be impeached.” During Trump’s first term, foreign governments or entities closely affiliated with foreign governments paid large sums to his hospitality businesses for lodging, food, entertainment, meeting services, and the like. Although the foreign emoluments clause permits receipt of emoluments from foreign kings, princes, or states with the “Consent of Congress,” Trump never sought the required consent. In his second term, Trump’s pursuit of prohibited emoluments has been far more egregious, in both its brazenness and the amounts involved. However, it is useful to think about his first-term behavior in order to frame the issues surrounding his second-term conduct. From the beginning, Trump’s business arrangements presented the troublesome question of whether domestic or foreign government payments to a president avoid the prohibition on emoluments so long as they take the form of a commercial exchange. Trump supporters argued during his first term that an arm’s-length business transaction is not a “present” or “emolument.” To this Trump added the more general plaint that a businessman should not have to surrender his business (or even abandon its active management) to be president. Both contentions are flawed. In the founding period, the word emolument was used to mean not only salary but far more commonly profit, gain, advantage, or benefit. Even an arm’s-length commercial transaction implies mutual benefit. The seller provides a good or service and receives in return value exceeding the cost to him of the good or service—a profit. When the federal or state government or a foreign power buys from a president’s business, it confers on him a profit, gain, advantage, or benefit. It does not matter for emoluments purposes that there is no corrupt intention on either side. The point of the emoluments prohibition is to eliminate the need to prove such things. It is the conferring of the benefit that is prohibited, and the Constitution is emphatic in banning foreign emoluments “of any kind whatever.” Likewise, the contention that a president should not have to alter his profit-making activities to enter public office is wrong. The Framers wrote the salary and emoluments provisions into the Constitution to ensure not only that the wealthy could afford to serve the country but also that the poor and those of middle station would not become wealthy (or the already-wealthy wealthier) by entering public office. Trump’s position is that wealthy people like himself should be able to occupy the presidency while surrendering no private advantages, and indeed should be entitled to leverage public office to create greater personal wealth. In short, he claims a right to engage in the precise form of corrupt self-enrichment that has since the 14th century been the quintessential impeachable offense. Moreover, the Constitution does not ban foreign emoluments absolutely; it merely requires that Congress consent to them. A businessman president who wants to both serve and continue his foreign commercial connections simply has to ask for permission, and in doing so, provide the people’s representatives sufficient information to assure them that giving permission does not impinge on larger national interests. Trump has never asked. During either of his terms. And Congress has never given permission. The most notorious gift to Trump from a foreign power was Qatar’s 2025 transfer of a luxuriously appointed Boeing 747-8 jet worth $400 million. The announced terms of the gift were that the United States would upgrade the plane for use as Trump’s presidential aircraft, known as Air Force One, but that at the end of his term, it would pass to the ownership of the Trump presidential library. In the summer of 2026, Trump seemingly began to waver on whether the Qatari plane would go to his library, or whether he might try to decommission a different presidential plane and transfer that one. The question remains unresolved. The terms of the gift as originally announced plainly violated the foreign emoluments clause. It was a gift from a foreign government to Donald Trump, to be enjoyed by him while president and to pass into the ownership of his presidential library thereafter. Congress never approved either the original gift or the proviso that it would later become the property of an entity controlled by Trump. Even if Trump ultimately changes his mind about taking the plane with him when he leaves office, the congressionally unapproved gift nonetheless violates the Constitution. The distinguishing features of the Qatari airplane are its size, comfort, and the luxury of its interior furnishings, points Trump has gushed over. Foreign gifts made for the personal pleasure or enjoyment of the president during office do not escape the emoluments clause if someone else gets to enjoy them thereafter. If, for example, a foreign country gave a president an entirely new wardrobe of tailored suits and casual wear, that would be a prohibited emolument, even if the president promised that, upon leaving the White House, he would donate the whole lot to the Smithsonian or Goodwill. Of greater ongoing concern than the Qatari airplane is the flood of commercial agreements the Trump Organization, controlled by Trump but nominally operated by his sons Eric and Donald Jr., has entered into with foreign entities. For example, only weeks before the plane gift in May 2025, the Trump Organization announced its participation in development of a $5.5 billion luxury golf resort in Doha, Qatar. Trump would doubtless argue that the resort deal is a purely private business transaction between developers in Qatar and his family business, thus not qualifying it as an emolument from a foreign state. But the Qatari development company with which the Trumps are partnering is reported to be Qatari Diar, which is owned by the Qatari government. Moreover, Qatar is an absolute monarchy ruled by a hereditary emir without whose approval no decision by a government-owned entity to do business with a U.S. president’s family could possibly occur. In 1993, the Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel concluded that “business corporations owned or controlled by foreign governments will fall within the [Foreign Emoluments] Clause,” a result it reaffirmed in 2009. Likewise, any contention that money received by the Trump Organization is not an emolument to Trump because his sons run the company is a threadbare fiction. So far as can be determined, Trump owns all of or controlling interests in all the entities that make up the Trump Organization. Money paid to it is money paid to him. The golf resort deal is, therefore, a prohibited foreign emolument. Worse, the Qataris’ multiple gifts to President Trump are not merely technical violations of the foreign emoluments clause, but are a classic instance of why the Framers put that clause in the Constitution to begin with. Qatar has huge stakes in its relationship with the U.S. government. U.S. trade in goods and services with Qatar exceeds $10 billion annually. More importantly, as graphically demonstrated during the ongoing Iran–U.S. war, the United States is the principal security guarantor of Qatar. Indeed, in September 2025, only four months after Qatar gave Trump an airplane and his family business a major real estate concession, President Trump issued an executive order declaring that “the United States shall regard any armed attack on the territory, sovereignty, or critical infrastructure of the State of Qatar as a threat to the peace and security of the United States,” and stating that in the event of an attack, the United States would defend Qatar. What we have here is not a plane gift made as a friendly intergovernmental gesture between traditional allies or a normal, arm’s-length real estate deal that just happens to involve Trump’s family business, but what normal people would see as bribes paid by a foreign government to the president of the United States to induce him to take official actions favoring Qatar. And bribery is an impeachable offense. Moreover, the Qatar golf resort deal is not the only one of its kind. According to one source, as of July 2026, 24 Trump-branded real estate projects were underway in foreign countries. Not all of the contractual counterparties to these agreements have the direct government connection of Qatar’s Qatari Diar. But some do, as was true in December 2024, a month after Trump’s second election, when the Trumps secured participation in a real estate development supported by Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman. In addition, LIV Golf, a professional golf league financed by the Saudi sovereign wealth fund, paid the Trump family an undisclosed amount to host an April 2025 tournament at the Trump National Doral resort in Florida. Like Qatar, Saudi Arabia depends heavily on the United States for security support. Moreover, in the same period that the Saudis were paying to rent a Trump golf course and Trump businesses were moving into Saudi real estate with Prince bin Salman’s blessing, Trump’s administration was negotiating an agreement (on which Trump is now waffling) that would permit the Saudis to expand their use of nuclear power. The appearance of influence-buying is obvious. The Trump family’s cryptocurrency speculations have also involved repeated egregious violations of the foreign emoluments clause. In sum, firms backed by the government of Abu Dhabi and headed by a member of its royal family paid half a billion dollars for a 49 percent stake in the Trump crypto company World Liberty Financial, half apparently paid before Trump’s 2025 inauguration and half after. Thereafter, an Abu Dhabi–backed group invested $2 billion in crypto company Binance by purchasing World Liberty “stablecoins,” giving World Liberty $2 billion in deposits to invest. Like Saudi Arabia and Qatar, Abu Dhabi and the other members of the United Arab Emirates are deeply economically enmeshed with the United States and heavily dependent on America for national security protection. It would seem entirely accurate to characterize these investments in Trump’s crypto wealth as literal protection payments. And not unfair to suspect that the millions flowing into Trump’s personal coffers have been repaid in the coin of American lives and treasure expended defending the Emirates in Trump’s foolish Iran war. The bottom line is that Trump has repeatedly violated the letter of the foreign emoluments clause, thereby vastly enriching himself and his family businesses, while openly directing American foreign policy to favor his foreign benefactors. There can be no question that the Framers of the American Constitution would be united in finding this conduct both shameful and undeniably impeachable. GLP-1 weight-loss drugs are one of the biggest biotech breakthroughs of the last decade. But to some clinical psychologists, they can represent something more sinister—a potential trigger for eating disorders. A new report suggests that the global incidence of anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa shot up between 1990 and 2023—by a whopping 71%. By 2023, there were an estimated 18.1 million people living with one of these two disorders. What’s worse is that the number is likely to be much higher than that once we factor in other eating disorders. And, disturbingly, those in the field are already starting to see GLP-1 weight-loss drugs exacerbating the issue. Cheri Levinson is a clinical psychologist at the University of Louisville in Kentucky who has specialized in eating disorders for almost 20 years. She says she’s now seeing more patients with more severe symptoms than at any other time in her career, and that many of them are misusing GLP-1s. “This is the worst I’ve seen things,” she says. Eating disorders are mental health conditions in which people’s eating behaviors impact their health. They include anorexia nervosa (defined by a fear of weight gain), bulimia nervosa (in which people binge and purge), binge eating disorder, and others. It’s thought that some people have a genetic predisposition to certain eating disorders, but that some external factor is what triggers their onset. That might be stress, or social pressure to be skinny. Often, simply restricting food intake can trigger an eating disorder, says Levinson. Eating disorders—along with the pressure to look thin—were already prevalent in the 1990s (the heroin chic era springs to mind). To find out how things might have changed since then, Ruhai Bai at the Children’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University in China and his colleagues pulled together data from studies and surveys conducted on populations around the world. The team used statistical models to fill in any data gaps. That research allowed the researchers to estimate the annual incidence of two eating disorders—anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa—for each of 204 countries and territories between 1990 and 2023. They also estimated the impact those disorders had on global health by calculating their disability-adjusted life years (one DALY represents a year of healthy life lost). The findings, published in JAMA Psychiatry on Wednesday, are unsettling. Between 1990 and 2023, there was a 71% increase in the incidence of those two eating disorders, the team found. Those conditions had a huge impact on global health, too. In 2023, they “cost the world the equivalent of about 3.83 million years of healthy life,” says Bai. Bai and his colleagues expect these figures to continue to rise. In 2023, an estimated 18.1 million people had either anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa; by 2050, the team predict, the two conditions could affect 21.8 million people. It gets worse. Those two conditions represent less than half of all eating disorders, says Levinson, who was not involved in Bai’s study. The true impact of these disorders is “probably three times the rate that they’re reporting,” she says. Bai and his colleagues didn’t explore the reasons for this rise (although Bai points out that it can’t be entirely explained by population growth). So how did we get here? Social media may have played a role. “Social media comparisons, influencers, challenges, diet trends can all be precipitating … events for an eating disorder,” says Cynthia Bulik, a clinical psychologist at the University of North Carolina, who was not involved in the new study. Bulik has been studying and treating eating disorders since the early 1980s. “Between 1990 and 2023 there has clearly been an upsurge of those types of environmental exposures,” she says. Many social media users are exposed to filtered and modified images of skinny influencers who may have undergone cosmetic surgery or extreme dieting and who are promoting “thinspiration” or “fitspiration” content. But users with eating disorders appear to be more likely to engage with this content—and to be fed more of it as a result. When researchers at the University of Melbourne investigated this phenomenon a couple of years ago, they found that the algorithms of TikTok users with eating disorders delivered 4343% more “toxic eating disorder videos” than those of users who didn’t have the disorders. They also got more content focused on dieting or exercising. Many were fed over 1000 problematic videos per month. Bulik describes social media as “quicksand” for people with, or at risk of, eating disorders. The covid-19 pandemic—and the social isolation that accompanied lockdowns—also appears to have contributed to the rise in eating disorders. Research conducted in 2021 found that the number of people hospitalized with eating disorders in the US doubled between 2018 and 2020. Researchers don’t yet have good data on how trends in eating disorders might have progressed since 2023. But anecdotally, things seem to be getting even worse. “There are more eating disorders, more severe eating disorders… and we’re seeing earlier ages of onset,” says Levinson. “We’re seeing eight- and nine-year-olds with eating disorders.” “From what I’m hearing and seeing, I am not going to be surprised if that number isn’t at least doubling again,” she adds. Levinson particularly worries about the impact of GLP-1 weight-loss drugs, which have exploded in popularity in recent years. A Gallup survey conducted earlier this year suggested that 15% of Americans had used one of these drugs for weight loss. And they’re becoming more accessible—drug companies are working on new formulations that are both easier to take and more effective, and many people can buy versions of these drugs from compound pharmacies or dubious online sources. In a small study published earlier this year, Levinson and her colleagues found that 30% of people with eating disorders had used GLP-1 drugs. The drugs might benefit some of those people. But 10% appeared to have misused the drugs. And Levinson thinks that figure is also an underestimate. Levinson says she and her colleagues have seen children develop eating disorders after being prescribed GLP-1s for medical reasons. They’ve seen others—even children as young as 11—buy versions of these drugs over TikTok. “We’re very concerned that GLPs will lead to even faster and more extreme weight loss in people with anorexia nervosa, and that patients will come to us in even more advanced stages of illness that become difficult to reverse,” says Bulik. Both Bulik and Levinson are continuing to study the impacts of these drugs in eating disorders. In the meantime, both urge medical professionals to consider the risks GLP-1s might pose to people with those disorders. Bulik contributed to a set of research and clinical guidelines, published earlier this month, that recommends doctors screen for eating disorders before prescribing the drugs, for example. The guidelines also recommend more patient education and more robust monitoring of people who take GLP-1s. If you think you or someone you know might have an eating disorder, you can find information and support at the National Eating Disorders Association. This summer, hundreds of OpenAI agents tried to cheat on a cybersecurity test. They broke out of their test environment and successfully hacked Hugging Face, a site that they believed held clues about how they’d be graded. Around the same time, another OpenAI agent accessed private files from an Australian government website. It’s a problem that’s by no means confined to OpenAI. In the months since, Anthropic, Google, and Meta have also disclosed incidents in which their agents have gone off track. And worryingly, in many cases the agents eluded the very researchers and safety teams tasked with monitoring them. AI agents are taking unauthorized actions, and the companies building and running them are mostly relying on luck and hindsight to mitigate any harmful impacts. They must do more to ensure safety. That’s why EU regulators and US agencies are rightfully calling for agent developers and deployers to monitor agents in real time. But these rules assume that companies can see what their agents are doing and why—when in reality, they lack sufficient visibility. As agents roll out across critical sectors, from finance to government to medicine, we must be able to monitor them in real time to prevent wide-ranging and potentially devastating consequences.  And while safety depends on monitoring, monitoring requires observability: the capacity to capture what the system did, process it into signals, and present those to humans or automated oversight systems. In other words, we need to know what the agent did and why it did it. And as our recent research shows, current agent frameworks—the software that runs agents— do not reliably generate the crucial observability records we need to ensure that agents are deployed safely. Agents present new kinds of problems that generative AI alone does not. They inherit the unpredictability of the LLMs they are built on, but where a generative AI system produces a single output for a human to act on, an agent performs longer tasks—it plans, picks tools, spawns sub-agents, and takes actions. So an agent’s course can take shape in a multitude of different ways, and no tests before launch can fully anticipate the risks. This makes observing the agents while they work even more challenging, and even more important. With colleagues from academia, civil society, and industry, we studied the four most widely used agent frameworks to see what signals they actually emit. We found major gaps that made it hard to achieve key safety goals, like noticing policy violations, unauthorized actions, and even human oversight failures. Key observability requirements were missing—like Agent IDs that persist over time, and records about which humans approved agent tasks. This made it hard to tell which agents did what or assess whether they were taking unauthorized actions. The good news is that these gaps, once identified, can be closed. Doing so starts with capturing two types of records: those that reveal what happened in an agent’s run and how the agent made its decisions. The “what happened” records identify the agent (while preserving privacy) and log how humans intervened and whether agent permissions or “memory” shifted, while the “decision” records show an agent’s reasoning and levels of confidence in the actions it’s taken. Take, for example, agents working to process a bank’s mortgage loans. The system examines an application and recommends denying it, and then a human loan officer signs off on the decision. In a later audit, or perhaps even in real time, a bank or regulator needs to be able to reconstruct which agent made the call, which officer approved it, and how long they took. And the bank must also analyze records of how the agent arrived at its refusal decision in order to ensure it did not use off-limits information, like details about the neighborhood the applicant is from. Recording “what happened” and “how the agent decided” require different approaches, but both are feasible. The current lack of “what happened” records is mostly a software plumbing problem that can be addressed through OpenTelemetry, an open observability standard maintained by engineers across the industry and by the agent frameworks adopting it. OpenTelemetry has launched a working group drafting shared formats for tracing agents, but they have yet to be finalized. The group should urgently incorporate standards for maintaining persistent agent identity and for recording human interventions and changes to agents’ permission or memory. And it should be done before these systems can scale, all the while attending to the privacy implications of agent ID that links to real people. Preserving “how agents decided” records is more challenging. The issue raises open research questions for model providers and researchers: Does the step-by-step reasoning a model displays, known as chain of thought, reflect how the model actually made a decision? And do we have reliable ways to measure how confident the agent is in that decision? These questions need answers before anyone sets standards for how such observability records are used. No single organization or sector can close the observability gaps on their own, but together, they can support the infrastructure required to prevent dangerous incidents and support safety. AI developers building agent frameworks, like OpenAI, Anthropic, CrewAI, and LangChain, must integrate telemetry signals recording an agent’s persistent identity, any human interventions, and any changes to an agent’s permissions and memory. Meanwhile, enterprises adopting these agent frameworks have an influential role to play; enterprises in regulated industries, like banks and hospitals, can put pressure on the agent developers to supply these missing observability records where standards do not yet exist. Enterprises must also define policies, permissions, and roles that set out what agents can safely do, since telemetry alone cannot assess whether agents broke specific rules. Finally, regulators and AI standards institutes must specify the observability records required for high-stakes AI deployments while leaving the technical details to industry. We have already seen initial momentum here: NIST’s agent standards initiative in the US is working on agent identity, one of the most salient gaps we found in our research. No bank would let a thousand unbadged employees loose on customer accounts and hope to piece together what happened when something goes wrong. Yet that’s how we’re deploying AI agents. Making their behavior observable is the first step toward fixing this. And by building the regulatory and policy ecosystem that makes it possible to act on what we see, we can hold organizations accountable. Until then, every task we hand to an agent hands over our agency, too. Our climate models have a big, dangerous blind spot

MIT Technology Review | 2026-10-29

Visit Original Website
Climate change itself is increasingly fueling climate change. Rising concentrations of greenhouse gases are driving up global temperatures, setting off dangerous feedback loops that we have not fully accounted for in our climate models—or our emissions-reduction goals. Scientists have long warned that warming would propel further warming in various ways, but a growing number of observations and studies are at last providing a sharper sense of just how powerful these cascading effects could be. And, by extension, they’re showing how much faster the planet may warm in this century. A study in Environmental Research Letters in September, synthesizing the results of previous papers, found that the additional carbon dioxide and methane released from thawing permafrost, intensifying wildfires, hotter lakes and reservoirs, and wetter, warmer wetlands alone could drive global average temperatures up 0.2 °C to 0.4 °C this century. That would come on top of the 2.6°C of warming above preindustrial levels that modelers are already expecting through 2100. The additional heat could subject many of the world’s cities to monthslong heat waves and expose hundreds of millions of people to flooding. None of the models that inform the major UN climate reports, which assess how much the planet will likely warm under various emissions scenarios, account for all these feedback effects. Many of them don’t incorporate any. That leaves a huge blind spot in our understanding of how much the world could actually warm in the coming decades—and how rapidly nations will need to slash emissions to stay within critical boundaries. A separate analysis published in One Earth in March, which examined what the feedback problem means for our international climate goals, concluded that the world may be much further off track than is widely believed. It found that just three regional sources of warming-driven emissions—thawing permafrost, increasing fires in boreal forests, and warming tropical wetlands—may shorten by as much as 25% the time before the planet consistently breaches the 1.5 °C and 2 °C warming thresholds. Our climate models and the UN’s reports clearly need to be updated to better represent these effects. Nations must also step up their efforts to reduce emissions to reflect this improving understanding of the dangers we face, says Sam Abernethy, the lead author of the paper and a climate scientist at Spark Climate Solutions, a San Francisco–based nonprofit research group. At least some of this is starting to happen. Spark kick-started an initiative, called the Warming-Induced Greenhouse Gas Emissions Model Intercomparison Project, through which more than 20 international teams are running the same set of analyses on different models to develop greater understanding of feedback effects’ potential power. Researchers involved hope that the UN will incorporate those results into its next major climate assessment, due out in 2029. That report will update estimates of the remaining emissions that can be released before the world crosses key temperature thresholds. But what is already clear is that the planet is rapidly warming and that nations haven’t done nearly enough to cut fossil-fuel pollution. And even if we did manage to halt it, warming-driven emissions could continue. So a number of the research groups involved in the recent studies have started to explore what nations could do to get feedback emissions under control. Scientists at the Woodwell Climate Research Center in Massachusetts are, for example, investigating the possibility of preventing fires or rapidly detecting them and putting them out when they threaten to burn the vegetation and soil that help insulate particularly carbon-rich wedges of permafrost (a subject I explored at length in a recent feature story). In addition, the Environmental Defense Fund’s Climate Innovation Initiative has backed lab research on the potential of adding sulfate, iron, or other compounds to wetlands to counteract fast-rising methane emissions. Several startups are also exploring possibilities. They’re looking into suppressing the lightning strikes that are igniting more and more fires in high latitudes, placing synthetic mats above permafrost to prevent it from melting, and deploying autonomous drones to quickly suppress potentially high-emissions fires before they burn out of control. Investigating all these ideas is worthwhile. But researchers are only in the earliest stages of figuring out how big a dent in emissions any such efforts could achieve across sprawling global ecosystems undergoing massive transformations. The surest path to reducing the effect of warming-driven warming remains the one we’ve known all along: breaking society’s addiction to fossil fuels.
