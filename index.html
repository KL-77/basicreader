Attempts to locate the unconfirmed report were unsuccessful, but security firm watchTowr said it claimed to affect every PHP version from PHP 5, released in 2004, onward. Researchers from another security firm, Sansec, said they “expect mass attacks to start within 15 minutes of the release.” A third security firm, Webfence, also urged admins to prepare now. Hoping to quell criticism, Meta has dropped the word “train” from its “streamlined” commercial terms of service that kick in on October 30. Last week, Meta faced backlash after legal experts warned that the tech giant’s sweeping new commercial terms of service seemed to give Meta permission to endlessly scrape websites linked on business pages. Talking to Nieman Lab, experts suggested that Meta could possibly use the policy to avoid millions in licensing fees from news organizations by requiring all business users to share a wealth of AI training data simply for running a business page. A Meta spokesperson confirmed that Meta did not alert users to the changed language following backlash. This potentially only adds to confusion ahead of the policy rollout. In response to backlash, Meta’s spokesperson provided a statement to Ars, acknowledging Nieman Lab’s initial report but not its follow-up report reiterating experts’ concerns: “We’ve heard you and want to clarify: the crawling terms we consolidated only applied to our ads and business products, and we’ve clarified that in the terms themselves. It does not change how Meta uses web crawlers. As always, content owners can use robots.txt to signal their preferences. This is not about scraping news sites, and these updates don’t give us new rights that supersede those preferences.” The same experts who sounded the initial alarms were not impressed by Meta’s updates, which they told Nieman Lab do not go far enough to protect business page users from invasive scraping. Matt Topic, a media and intellectual property attorney at Loevy & Loevy, told Nieman Lab that “publishers should remain concerned.” “The revised version of the new terms still allows Meta to use that scraped content to develop any ‘business product,’” Topic said. “That doesn’t seem to be a defined term, and presumably Meta is developing AI business products. I think ‘develop’ is broad enough to include ‘train.’” Initially, Meta’s terms authorized Meta to use “any content” from websites posted on business pages “to provide, develop, improve, research, train, and maintain the integrity” of any Meta products, services, or features. The updated terms delete the word “train” and no longer refer to “any content.” Instead, they allow Meta to “periodically pull updates” from business users’ websites in order to “provide, develop, improve, research, and maintain advertising and business products,” as well as to help Meta “maintain the integrity and security” of its products. This scraping, Meta says, is for users’ benefit, feeding into Meta research into products to “help you create and deliver ads and other commercial content, provide users information about your products and services, and connect you with users who may be interested in your products and services,” the policy says. Following tweaks, it’s good that Meta added “more language about the permitted uses,” James Grimmelmann, a professor of digital and information law at Cornell Law School, told Nieman Lab. But “[the] ‘develop…advertising and business products’ [language] is still broad enough to drive a truck through,” he said. Ultimately, Grimmelmann agreed with Topic that “this does nothing to restrict the ‘associated with your products or services’ [language] that would let Meta scrape entire sites.” What would help—as University of Wisconsin law professor Benjamin Sobel told Nieman Lab—would be if Meta added specific language to the policy where the company agrees to respect scraping instructions in robots.txt files. Since complying with robots.txt is voluntary, Meta could possibly rely on the legally enforceable TOS omitting mentions of robots.txt to scrape sites that attempt to block scraping via robots.txt, experts suggested. When it comes to robots.txt, “you can still express the preference, and unless it’s a legally enforceable preference, it can still be ignored all the same,” Sobel said. Criticism goes beyond Nieman Lab’s reporting, as news groups globally urge regulators to intervene and block Meta’s controversial terms from kicking in. A chief executive for the United Kingdom News Media Association, Theo Bamber, accused Meta of a “shameless land grab” relying on the “worst kind of arrogant tech platform bullying.” Bamber urged UK regulators to “stand up for British creators and the business community” by “preventing Meta from setting this dreadful precedent.” Meanwhile, the European Publishers Council has already asked the European Commission to intervene. “Publishers must be able to decide whether and how their journalism is used, and negotiate fair payment,” the group’s executive director, Angela Mills Wad, said. “Burying such far-reaching claims in platform terms is no substitute for consent.” Similarly, Danielle Coffey, the president and CEO of the News/Media Alliance—which Nieman Lab noted is the largest news publishing trade association in the US—protested the terms as “outrageous and probably illegal.” In the US, Coffey urged regulators to recognize that Meta’s “decision to withhold access to its audience unless businesses allow Meta to scrape content from their websites is an appalling, bad-faith policy, and a clear abuse of the company’s market power.” “It cannot be allowed to be implemented on October 30,” Coffey said.

The price you see at the gas pump reflects more than just crude oil. Also built in are the costs of refining, distribution through wholesalers, various taxes, and the margin your neighborhood station charges. Crude oil is still the largest single driver of the final pump price, typically representing over half of each gallon’s cost. Spikes in oil prices tend to push gas prices higher in short order. It can also do a lot to ease the pain of sudden price jumps when supply gets disrupted. It’s not a permanent fix, as it’s more meant to provide immediate support for consumers and ensure critical parts of the economy like key industries, emergency services, public transportation, and so on can keep operating. Both oil and natural gas play key roles as major sources of energy. A big change in oil prices can affect natural gas by proxy. Trump’s remarkable mess on electric vehicles: How he made them more expensive and harder to find just as he went to war on Iran

Fortune | FORTUNE | 2026-10-21

Visit Original Website
Within three months of the U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran on Feb. 28, 2026, the average price of gasoline for American drivers climbed from US$2.94 to $4.35 a gallon. If there was ever a moment for Americans to switch to electric vehicles, this was it. From March to May 2026, in fact, sales of hybrid electric vehicles were 18% higher than over the same months in 2025. But sales of fully electric vehicles were 16% lower from March to May 2026 compared with the same months in 2025. Some observers took that as evidence that high gas prices alone wouldn’t push Americans toward fully electric cars, but that conclusion misses half the story. I study car buyers and their preferences, and there is an important distinction between what consumers want and what they can actually buy. Sales reveal what cars people actually bought, but not necessarily what they actually wanted. When the supply of some kinds of cars can’t keep up with demand, prices rise, and that’s exactly what happened for electric vehicles in 2026. In 2026, hybrids and all-electric vehicles faced the same jump in demand from the oil crisis. While there were plenty of hybrids available to meet that demand, the same was not true for EVs. Hybrid production had been growing for years, led by Toyota, which sells about half of the hybrids bought in the United States. As a result, even as sales surged, hybrid inventories on dealer lots were 19% higher in March 2026 than they had been a year earlier. However, EV production had been in retreat since the tax and spending law President Donald Trump signed in July 2025 ended federal tax credits for EVs. Buyers had been able to get up to $7,500 for new EVs and $4,000 for used ones, but that ended after Sept. 30, 2025. Automakers responded by cutting back their EV production. General Motors laid off more than 3,400 workers at its EV and battery plants and shifted its Tennessee plant to make batteries for energy storage, not cars. Ford wrote off $19.5 billion from its EV business, ended production of the all-electric F-150 Lightning and converted a Tennessee EV plant to build gas-powered trucks. Buyers, for their part, rushed to beat the tax credit deadline, pushing EVs to 11.3% of new vehicle sales in September 2025. But by November 2025, after the tax credits ended, their share dropped to 4.3%. As 2026 progressed and gas prices began to rise, so did EV demand. New EV inventories declined, shrinking from about 130 days of supply in the first quarter of 2026 to 78 days by August – just two days above the supply level of gas-burning cars. Discounts on new EV models shrank too. My analysis of online listings from marketcheck.com shows the median new EV was listed 9.8% below sticker price in February. By April, the median discount was 6.6%, and 4.7% in June. That’s a sign that dealers saw rising demand, not inventory to clear. Before the war, prices for used electric vehicles were still drifting down in the wake of the expired used EV federal tax credit. After the war began, they climbed 13% through late June 2026. For a typical used electric vehicle, that’s about $3,500 more. Other data sources point in the same direction. Recurrent, a company that reports on batteries and used EVs, found that the price of a used 2023 Chevrolet Bolt rose almost 20% from January to June 2026. And inventories of used electric vehicles dropped too, with only 31 days of supply in May, 23% below the year before. None of this means the electric vehicle market is healthy. High prices and uncertain access to charging stations are real stumbling blocks for people who are thinking about buying an EV. But the data doesn’t support the claim that electric vehicle sales stalled in 2026 because Americans don’t want them. Rather, the sales stalled because there weren’t enough EVs available to buy – and there were plenty of hybrids as an alternative. The distinction matters because automakers have treated a change in the policy landscape as a change in consumer demand, leading to shortages with real costs. The average new car now sells for about $50,000 and the average new electric vehicle for about $55,000, according to Kelley Blue Book, a company that tracks car prices. The current situation leaves drivers exposed to the volatility of oil prices with fewer alternatives. The U.S. is the world’s largest oil producer, yet a war in the Persian Gulf raised gas prices significantly in just three months. Oil trades on a global market, so American drivers pay global prices. Seven months into the war, the pressure on drivers is still going full throttle. Oil was still above $100 a barrel in mid-October, and diesel hit a record $6.52 a gallon on Sept. 22. Electric vehicle sales haven’t caught up. Argonne’s data show 86,000 of them sold in August, fewer than the 101,000 sold in May, while hybrid sales stayed above 216,000 a month all summer. Rather than move to increase electric vehicle supply and make them more affordable, federal policy continues to hinder the U.S. electric vehicle sector. The fuel economy rule released Sept. 28, 2026, loosens efficiency requirements for gas-powered vehicles and in 2028 ends a system that let automakers that beat the standards – mostly EV makers – earn credits for exceeding them and sell those to automakers that fell short. That means automakers will have fewer regulatory incentives to produce EVs going forward. Regulators say buyers will save $1,289 on the purchase price of a new gas-powered vehicle from reduced engineering and manufacturing costs to make less efficient engines. But the same analysis estimates drivers will spend about $1,600 more on gas over the life of the car, and that’s using fuel price forecasts well below today’s prices. Economists say the administration’s math does not properly account for those fuel costs. The U.S. has also effectively shut out Chinese automakers, even though nearly three-quarters of the world’s electric cars are built in China. In 2024, Chinese car buyers could choose from over 130 EV models priced under $40,000; American buyers had just four EV models under that threshold. Beyond tariffs of more than 100% on EVs made in China, a Commerce Department rule bars cars from Chinese-linked automakers starting with model year 2027, even if they are built in the U.S. A bill pending in Congress would widen the ban, blocking Chinese investment in U.S. auto factories altogether. As long as gas stays expensive, the gap between the electric vehicles Americans want and the electric vehicles dealers can sell them is likely to remain. Decisions such as ending the tax credit, rolling back fuel economy standards and banning Chinese technology all make it harder to build them, which means Americans will continue to have fewer affordable electric vehicles to choose from. Native to the tropical waters of the Indo-Pacific, lionfish have infiltrated the western Atlantic, Caribbean, and Gulf of Mexico, where they have become a destructive invasive species ever since the first was recorded near Miami in 1985, likely after being released from the home aquarium trade. With few predators in these new habitats, a single fish can produce more than 2 million eggs a year. Studies have found that just one lionfish can reduce the replenishment of native fish populations on some reefs by nearly 80%. In 2016, Bowman sold the first lionfish to Whole Foods in South Florida. “That was the ground-breaker,” she says, “because if Whole Foods is promoting something, then people know it’s good. Bowman’s work is part of a growing trend that’s not just about introducing new dishes to the local food scene; creating a market for invasive species may also bring ecological benefits for the local environment. Invasive species are considered one of the five most critical direct drivers of biodiversity loss, according to the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES), whose most recent report in 2023 notes that the global economic toll of dealing with these species cost more than $423 billion in 2019. Once established, invasive species are often impossible to eradicate because they reproduce rapidly, spread across vast, interconnected environments, and often go unchecked until they are established. And the challenge of managing invasive species is particularly acute in the ocean, says Helen Roy, a professor at the U.K. Centre for Ecology & Hydrology and the University of Exeter and a co-chair of the IPBES assessment. It’s a consequence of the openness and connectedness of marine systems, she says. A pot of green crab. Courtesy Lisa Nichols In 2004, conservation biologist Joe Roman coined the term “invasivorism” to describe the idea of eating invasive species to help control their populations. Since then, a growing movement across North America is attempting to transform ecological menaces into culinary commodities, from Florida to Hawai’i, British Columbia, Maine, and the Mississippi River basin. That movement is still expanding: in 2025, for example, 70 restaurants and fish markets across the U.S. participated in Green Crab Week, a national campaign encouraging diners to eat the invasive European green crab. The goal, says Isabelle Côté, a marine ecologist at Simon Fraser University, is often “functional eradication”: reducing an invasive population to a level where its ecological impacts are considered acceptable. That’s where our dinner tables can help, by turning the removal of the species into an economic opportunity. David Standridge’s philosophy is all about harvesting what’s plentiful and turning it into something delicious. Since 2022, the James Beard Award-winning executive chef of The Shipwright’s Daughter in Mystic, Conn., has processed more than 10,000 lbs of green crab, making it a menu mainstay. Commonly considered one of the world’s worst invasive species, the green crab has successfully invaded every continent except Antarctica. The species, native to Western Europe and Africa, has few predators, aggressively hunts its prey, and can consume a wide variety of shellfish, including oysters, clams, mussels, lobsters, and other crabs. One crab can eat more than 40 juvenile soft-shell clams in a day, while also destroying seagrass habitats. And a single green crab can produce up to 185,000 eggs per year, while its larval life stage allows young crabs to drift considerable distances in ocean currents, spreading from one invaded area to another. Rather than a nuisance, Standridge describes the green crab as tremendously flavorful, producing a beautiful stock that can be used across dishes. “We use them not to eliminate green crab, but to take some pressure off the ecosystem,” he says. Getting green crab onto the menu, however, wasn’t so straightforward. When Standridge first tried to source the species in 2020, he discovered that Connecticut, like many states, classified green crab as bait and technically couldn’t be sold for human consumption. He finally found a supplier to help in 2021, and then in 2025, a change to a single line of state code allowed green crab and other species on the bait list to be harvested and sold as food. Communities across the U.S. have been grappling with the ecological and economic impacts of introduced species for decades. In the 1950s, bluestripe snapper, also known in Hawai’i by its Tahitian name, ta’ape, was introduced to O’ahu, when roughly 3,000 fish were released to bolster fishing opportunities and establish a new food source. But ta’ape, native to the South Pacific, never became a sustained part of Hawai’i’s seafood market. By the 1980s, the overabundant fish was largely considered a “trash fish,” prompting the state’s Department of Aquatic Resources to launch a market-development project in 1985 that used consumer education and supermarket promotions to try to build demand. The effort failed to gain lasting traction: supply was inconsistent, and demand tapered off after the project ended. Where schools of snappers in their native ecosystems might number just a handful, she says Hawai’i’s ta’ape, with its electric-blue stripes and lemon-yellow skin, can blanket the seafloor. “You can see the power of numbers taking over these habitats where native fish belong,” she says. For Peter Stoett, a professor at Ontario Tech University and co-chair of the IPBES Assessment on Invasive Alien Species and Their Control, the key is to “measure the population, not the market.” A growing commercial fishery, more restaurants serving an invasive species, or thousands of pounds harvested may signal that a market is working—but not necessarily that the ecosystem is recovering. But measuring that requires sustained monitoring: “Money must be spent monitoring these populations or we won’t know if targets are being met.” Stoett argues that if a market is used to encourage removal, the commercial activity should exist because the species needs to be reduced, contained, or eradicated—not because the goal is to establish a sustainable fishery for that species. In other words, the conservation objective has to come first, with the market serving as a tool rather than becoming the goal itself. As populations decline, he says, there needs to be ongoing dialogue among scientists, industry, local communities, and government about whether incentives should continue, change, or eventually end. In Kentucky, there is some evidence that removal efforts are having an effect. The Kentucky Department of Fish and Wildlife Resources has increased its fishing incentive to $0.15 per lb. for invasive carp harvested from Kentucky Lake and Barkley Lake, citing evidence that management efforts are reducing populations in both lakes. Most of the carp processed by Yu comes from those same two lakes. Meanwhile, according to a USFWS spokesperson, around 30 million lbs of invasive carp are removed from Midwestern waters each year. The idea of harvesting and eating invasive species can sound like an elegant solution: turn an ecological problem into dinner and let appetite do the work. But Roy cautions against treating consumption as a panacea for control. The ecological payoff from harvesting is rarely proportional to the share of a population removed. Taking half of a population, for example, does not necessarily mean halving its impact on the ecosystem. That does not make the markets emerging around lionfish, green crab, ta’ape, and carp meaningless. They can provide food and income, engage communities and, in some cases, redirect fishing pressure toward invasive or overabundant species whose removal can benefit ecosystems. But Roy stresses that these benefits must be considered separately from the ecological damage caused by an invasion: “There’s no simple sum here.” Creating demand could theoretically give people an incentive to keep an invasive species abundant, although Côté says she is not aware of evidence that this has happened. And while “nothing beats prevention,” says Stoett, once an aquatic or marine invasion is established and containment is the only realistic option, a market for their safe consumption could provide another tool—one whose economic benefits can be weighed against the ecological impacts of the invasion. The goal, ultimately, is not to eat our way out of an ecological disaster. It is to make sure that, when we eat an ecological problem, we are actually helping to reduce it. Unlike lionfish or Asian carp, red and green sea urchins are native to the region. But when their populations become too abundant, they can have invasive-like effects. Urchins are remarkably effective grazers. Marine ecologist Anne Salomon, a professor of applied marine ecology and social-ecological system science at Simon Fraser University, says urchins and kelp can coexist when food is plentiful. But when food becomes scarce, urchins form what she calls “active feeding fronts,” stripping away vegetation that provides food, shelter, and habitat for a wide range of marine life. This means almost every time you open the phone you instinctively rotate it, and while it will work no matter how it’s rotated, it’s better to rotate it counterclockwise so the cameras are on top and the volume buttons are on the top left. Over our week of testing, this started feeling more natural, but it’s exactly the opposite of what you’d initially expect. It’s especially odd in Apple’s bumper case, which has a large notch to expose the Touch ID sensor and camera control, and it looks for all the world like it belongs at the bottom. You might think this is a little thing, but it’s strange to find yourself slightly unsure of how to hold a smartphone. A nontrivial part of the Duo experience is just reorienting yourself to where the volume buttons are as you flip the thing around. When closed, they’re on top of the phone, but if you rotate the Duo clockwise, they end up on the bottom right, and it’s the top button that turns the volume down because they don’t change orientation with the display. There’s a buried setting in iOS that makes the volume buttons reorient themselves as you turn the Duo around; Apple needs to set it so it works right by default. One other thing to get used to is simply unlocking the phone. Touch ID is integrated into the wake button on the side of the phone. There’s no Face ID at all, and it might take a minute to remember that when you stare at your phone for a beat and wonder why your notifications aren’t unfurling. Ask us how we know. But a week is long enough to get used to Touch ID life again, and it’s mostly painless once you remember you’re supposed to be using it. Now, here’s the thing: This is all pretty fun! The iPhone has been the same for so long that being forced to come up with little moves and tricks and ways to think about holding the Duo is a gadget nerd thrill. Other foldables do this too — Samsung and Google and Huawei have been pushing the envelope of gadget-y smartphones for ages — but Apple’s played it so safe for so long that the Duo is legitimately surprising. But Apple hasn’t found some magical software solution to making a product with this much inherent complexity any less chaotic. It just happens to be the kind of first-generation chaos that will delight early adopters and lead most everyone else back to their regular old iPhones. That first-generation wonkiness is most obvious in the software, especially in how the Duo supports third-party apps. Almost everyone who sees the Duo assumes that it simply opens up into being an iPad, but that’s not how it works at all. In classic Apple fashion, the company decided to throw everything out and start over. That means getting rid of a lot of weird iPad baggage, but it also means that solved problems on the iPad are back on the Duo. It’s big stuff and little stuff: For whatever reason, the Duo doesn’t support pinching the keyboard to make it smaller and friendlier to use one-handed — a feature iPads have had for ages. It also means there are new ways to think about multitasking, and sometimes you’ll be unsure what will happen when you swipe around the screen because apps interact with iOS and each other in very different ways. If you want to run two apps at once on the Duo, you swipe up and over to pin an app to one side, and then pin another app to the other side. If you tap on the little divider, you connect the apps into a set, and you can come right back to it in the app switcher. It’s neat, but it only works in landscape. If you want to have two apps open when you’re holding the Duo in portrait mode, you’re basically out of luck — unless what you want is to watch a video on the top while scrolling an app on the bottom. In that case, you need to make sure you have an app that supports background video, open that app, start the video, close that app to initiate the floating video window, open your other app, and then (and only then!) find a little button in the floating video window and hit that to pop everything into place. This is legitimately cool once you figure it out. But you will note that this is a totally different approach to multitasking than any previous iPhone or iPad, and nothing about the Duo’s interface makes it obvious that it can work this way. It’s a lot of these little moments of figuring it out for yourself that really solidify the Duo as an early adopter special — especially since some features only work in some apps sometimes. That floating video feature? Yeah, it works with YouTube TV, but it doesn’t seem to work with regular YouTube. This is initially surprising, because the YouTube app appears to be at least a little updated for the Duo. But it turns out that right now there are basically three ways apps work on the Duo. First, there are regular iPhone apps that haven’t been updated at all. As with an iPad, you can zoom and rotate these apps to make things a little more useful, but they look pretty silly. This is the vast majority of apps right now, although we saw lots of apps get updated to the second category during the course of our review. The second category is what you might call the messy middle: apps that have been updated for iOS 27. They get some automatic layout fixes so they’ll automatically expand to fill the inner screen in landscape orientation and don’t look quite as silly as standard iPhone apps. But you can tell they haven’t actually been designed for these displays, and those rough edges are super obvious. The telltale sign of these apps is the black bar on the right when you use them on the cover screen — proper Duo apps add controls to that space, but these in-between apps don’t. Then there are fully updated Duo apps built for the Duo-specific iOS 27.1 — we got to test a few of these, and they can be pretty good. For whatever reason, the company just didn’t go that way, and that means it will take a while for the Duo’s app story to settle down. Apple also insists on preserving the more narrow vertical aspect ratio when you use an app on the cover screen by reserving that strip on the side for controls, whereas Samsung lets content occupy the entire width of the screen. On the larger inner display, Samsung offers a couple of options to make the keyboard friendlier to use. You can switch to a one-handed option that’s easier to reach or a floating keyboard you can move around and resize. Neither phone should be used carelessly at the beach, but that higher dust rating offers a little more peace of mind. Overall, these feel like pretty familiar moves for both of these companies. Performance-wise, the Duo has an A20 Pro chip, and Apple says it has the same improved vapor chamber thermal management system as the iPhone 18 Pro. For most things it’s plenty fast, but the reality is that it’s also a thin phone driving two big displays, and it definitely gets warm, and sometimes hot. That’s fine for grabbing everyday shots, but it’s fundamentally a notch below flagship tier. You get that 2x crop zoom and up to just 10x digital zoom. There’s a 48-megapixel ultrawide that’s fine, but you just don’t have the flexibility you’ll find on basically every slab phone that costs half as much as the Duo. The problem with the Duo is that the outer screen isn’t useful enough, so you want to open it all the time, and Apple’s ecosystem means that eventually the apps will be useful on the inner screen. Scrubbing Earth of per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances—a.k.a. PFAS or forever chemicals—could eliminate a list of woes for people and planet. Used for decades in everything from nonstick pans to firefighting foams, PFAS have been linked to increased risks of fertility challenges, developmental delays, cancer, and reduced immune response. They are known for a strong bond between carbon and fluorine, which the environment struggles to break down. PFAS are everywhere: They spew from factories and slough off products and into water with normal use. Scientists have found them in everything from human blood to Antarctic snow. One 2022 report found that 89% of the US population had PFAS in excess of two nanograms per milliliter of blood plasma, a common threshold for potential adverse health effects, and 9% of the population had passed the riskier level of 20 nanograms per milliliter. Cleansing PFAS-contaminated soil is a project in its infancy, but it is possible to prevent more buildup by pulling the chemicals out of water systems. There are two ways to handle this: trap or destroy. Some scientists have found reverse osmosis—a process that pushes water through supertight filters—useful in point-of-use cases, like water filters at kitchen sinks. Other techniques in the pipeline might be more suitable for large-scale removal—for example, at water treatment facilities. One, called foam fractionation, generates bubbles that capture PFAS.
