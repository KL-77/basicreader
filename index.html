IBM has rolled out the newest models in its family of open-weight large language models designed to be downloaded and self-hosted. The newly launched Granite 4.2 comes in 3B, 8B, and 30B parameter variants. Like previous versions, IBM is taking a decoder-only approach here. These new releases offer a 128,000-token context window natively. The 8B and 30B variants (not the 3B one) also go through an agentic reinforcement-learning block; they were trained for expanded capabilities like using the terminal, searching the web, or using external tools. The 3B model supports tools too, but without the same level of specialized training. Beyond those tweaks, this release is particularly notable because, as IBM itself writes, “Granite 4.2 is the reasoning-focused release of the Granite language-model family.” When researchers or developers in the field say a model is capable of reasoning, they do not mean it in the same sense as we often assume when talking about human reasoning; the models are not consciously understanding the problem. Instead, they’re talking about functional reasoning, in particular via “chain-of-thought” and carrying intermediate results forward through multiple steps. For the user, this means more rigorous and accurate responses in some cases, but often slower response times and higher compute demands. IBM’s Granite family of models rarely grabs headlines for being the fastest or most aggressively innovative. Relative to even other competitors in the local enterprise space, like Nvidia’s Nemotron, the pitch seems to be predictable deployments—which is the priority you might expect from IBM these days. There has been an enormous amount of discourse about the cost and compute crunch around frontier cloud models from companies like Anthropic or OpenAI lately. Across many domains, both individual developers and enterprise organizations have been exploring local models as cheaper alternatives. That has also led to increased interest in model routers—AI tools whose main job is to interpret user prompts, tasks, or projects and route them to appropriately scoped models to balance performance, speed, and cost. Models like this are also popular with hobbyists, AI researchers, and individual developers because they can be tinkered with on local hardware without per-token API fees.

Xtracycle's Swoop ASM e-bike brings an automatic transmission to cargo bikes

The Velotric Discover M e-bike we reviewed earlier this year had a feature I found somewhat annoying: It would sporadically block much of the display with an alert telling me I should be in a different gear. While the implementation bugged me, the concept made a certain degree of sense; most e-bikes now know your cadence, how much torque you’re generating, and how that power is being translated into speed. That’s everything you need to know to determine an optimal gear. Somewhere in the back of my mind, I thought, “If you’re so smart, why don’t you just shift for me?” I was completely unaware that Shimano had already developed a system that did exactly that. And for the past few weeks, I’ve been using it on a $4,500 cargo bike from Xtracycle called the Swoop ASM that let me see what it’s like to hand control of my gears to an electronic brain. The short answer is that the electronics on the Swoop really are smart enough to figure out when it should be shifting. But the system is less adept at the actual shifting process, making for rides that are less smooth than they could be when it’s active. But you can shut that feature off, and the Swoop will shine as a fantastic cargo bike. Xtracycle got its start providing cargo trailers for mountain bikes and then branched into dedicated cargo bikes. The Swoop ASM is a long-tail cargo bike with what’s called a mullet setup: a larger tire up front and a smaller one in back. The small wheel is designed to help keep the weight held by the large rear rack a bit closer to the road. It also has a front suspension to help cushion the ride. Notably, the bike is built with steel tubes. Most manufacturers’ lineups use a mix of either carbon fiber and aluminum because those two materials can be made into the exact same shape, simplifying manufacturing. But other materials still have their fans, as they can offer distinct combinations of weight, stiffness, and vibration dissipation. I haven’t owned a steel tube bike since the 1990s, but the Swoop was a good reminder of why it still has its fans. It’s relatively lightweight (just under 70 lbs/31 kg) for its size, its ride is very comfortable, and it was surprisingly responsive when I stood in the pedals to accelerate away from stops. Despite its highly specialized geometry, the Swoop felt like a fundamentally good bicycle. There are limits to how well a single frame size can adapt to different body sizes, but Xtracycle does its best to widen the comfort window. It includes a double-telescoping seat post to maximize the seat-pedal distance flexibility. There’s also a quick release on the front handlebar clamp, making it easy to tweak the angle where the grips meet your hands. I found settings that were relatively comfortable but still a bit short of ideal for my 6-plus-foot (188 cm) frame—even with the seat post at its recommended maximum, I could touch the toes of both of my feet down while seated. Beyond that, plenty of nice touches make this a solid cargo bike. These include protective covers for the rear wheel and chain to keep clothing and limbs from accidentally getting entangled. A robust U-shaped kickstand keeps the bike stable even if you park it when fully loaded, and there’s a front cargo rack that can hold some baggage. The rear rack can be outfitted with a large range of accessories; mine came with the default setup meant for carrying passengers. This means footrests extending out at the level of the rear hub and two handgrip bars running parallel to the bench seating. But remove a soft cover, and the metal beneath reveals a series of slots that allow a wide variety of attachments to be fitted into place. You can also use one of Xtracycle’s bag attachments in this setup; I was provided one that ran the full length of the rear attachment and held a fair amount of cargo. Adding a second would have handled all of my typical shopping needs. (While having only one bag might seem awkward, the bike performed very well with everything I was carrying loaded on one side.) The only problem is that this hardware makes the rear wheel extremely difficult to reach. I struggled to lock the back tire to anything, and I considered myself fortunate to not have to figure out how to deal with a flat. All those basics make the Swoop a solid entry into the cargo bike market. What sets it apart is its use of a Shimano Cues Di2, one of the component maker’s low-end electronic shifting systems. Its mere existence surprised me, since I thought Shimano was reserving electronic shifting for higher-end component lines. But the company not only took it lower in the range; it added a couple of surprise features to its electronic shifting. First, the basics: the drivetrain uses a single chainring on the cranks and a 10-speed rear cassette. The rear derailleur requires only a power source and control signals—no cable required. Once properly aligned with the cassette gears, it executes a perfect shift every time it’s told to. This shift includes a brief period when the derailleur over-travels to ensure the chain engages the next ring along, then drops back into the optimal location for continued cycling. Everything in the system—the derailleur, the controllers, and the motor—takes power from a single removable battery. If the battery’s charge is good, you’re set to ride. The mid-motor offers three levels of assist, adjusted through a controller on the left side that also includes the power button and a simple display. Shifting is controlled by the one on the right, which lacks a display. Separating the two allows each to be fairly minimalist, making it easy to find the control you need by feel. On its own, the system worked beautifully. Shifting was fuss-free, and each change was blissfully silent. The assist worked well, and its three levels seemed to cover any needs short of going up steep hills with lots of cargo. This is not a bike that lets you skimp on pedaling in hot conditions; there’s no throttle, and even the highest setting of assist still requires you to exert some force on the pedals. But the motor definitely cuts down on the effort you have to make. The battery, while looking fairly compact, easily handled dozens of miles without needing a recharge, which means several days of typical errands. While the battery lacks a carrying handle, it’s easy to take it out to charge it, as it pivots sideways out of the frame. When in place, it acts as a protector that keeps anything on the left side of the bike from sliding through the frame and getting tangled in the chain. This is the first Shimano e-bike system I’ve tested, and it was just as good as you’d expect from a company that has come to dominate cycling drivetrains. Shimano Cues may be its lower-end system, but there’s no apparent skimping on the quality. And that’s before the bonus surprises Shimano has included. Somewhere in one of the controllers, Shimano’s software gathers all the relevant information about your ride: how fast you’re going, how much force your feet are exerting on the cranks, what gear you’re in, and how fast your legs are churning (and possibly more?). And it uses that data to do a couple of interesting things. One is what it calls Free Shift. Normally, a bike can’t shift to a different gear unless you’re turning the pedals to move the chain, which can potentially create problems. For example, you could move the derailleur down a gear but not complete the shift before stopping, meaning that the shift would be made when you start pedaling again, and the chain is under a lot of tension as you crank away from a full stop. Or you could move the derailleur several gears over without the chain following. But the whole point of having a motor is to move the chain. So if you shift without pedaling and the bike isn’t stationary, the Cues system will use the motor to move the chain just enough to complete the shift. It does so without speeding up the bike at all. Practically, this means that as you coast to a stop, you can simply hit the downshift button a few times, and the system will go into a low gear to make it easy to start up again without requiring you to turn the cranks. It’s … weird. Decades of cycling experience have programmed me to instinctually move my legs when I shift. Those instincts are suddenly superfluous. It gets even weirder if you enable the automatic transmission. Shimano has included two settings, one for general riding and one for hauling cargo or passengers. When the system recognizes that you can get more out of your pedaling in a different gear, it will simply move you into that gear. That includes doing all the downshifting as you’re coasting to a stop and rolling back up through the gears again as you accelerate away from it. Let me make that clear: You can coast to a stop without turning the cranks or touching the shifter, and you’ll still be in a different gear when you start up again. The system is remarkably good at matching when I feel a shift is needed—Shimano’s programmers have done a fine job. Because of that, it’s pretty easy to induce a shift by altering your pedaling, as well. You can tell when it’s close, and just a bit more force will cause the system to send you up a gear. It’s kind of pointless to do so, since the buttons that shift up and down still work when it’s in auto mode, but I found myself doing it regardless. The downside is that these shifts are not situations where instinctual actions are superfluous. To execute a smooth shift, you have to ease tension off the drivetrain by pedaling with less force, allowing the chain to slide over onto a neighboring gear with a bit less stress. Failing to do so can make for a clunky, inefficient shift and wear down components over time. My problem with the automatic transmission mode was that I could tell when a shift was likely to come, but I couldn’t predict its precise moment. So it typically came while I was pedaling away happily, with full tension on the chain, making for a loud and awkward shift. This was especially unpleasant when accelerating away from a stop, when the bike would make three or four clunky shifts in rapid succession, each emitting a noise that told me I’d just done something I’d spent years learning to avoid. Living in a neighborhood with lots of four-way stops meant I sometimes experienced that every single block. Shimano is not stupid, and these systems use parts that are specifically hardened to handle the added strain and wear. But it still felt bad every time I heard and felt that clunk. The Swoop is not an inexpensive bike; at $4,500, it sits somewhere between the Trek Fetch+ 2 and the Urban Arrow Family I’ve reviewed previously. While it lacks the latter’s capacious cargo tub, the longtail form factor provides a much more bicycle-like riding experience. I’d rate its ride as superior to the Trek because of its frame and front suspension made for a more pleasant ride. I also prefer Shimano’s drive system to the Bosch used by the other two bikes. It just felt better integrated with my pedaling, plus a bit quieter and less intrusive. The electronic shifting is a bonus—it’s not needed, but it definitely makes for a nice experience, and one where Shimano’s integration means you don’t have to worry about the status of the two additional batteries needed by many electronic systems. I don’t think I’d use the automatic shifting, but I could see where a less experienced cyclist might feel they benefit from it. So while it’s on the pricier side, the cost seems to be in line with the features it offers. If those features are important to you, the Swoop is definitely worth consideration. In the end, though, I’m not a huge fan of electronic shifting. I hate worrying about battery levels just to ride my bike, and I like the feel of a well-aligned mechanical shifter clicking into place. But even I have to admit that Shimano’s software does a fantastic job of shifting at the right times, and I think the system could really work well for anybody who hasn’t learned to associate a clunky-sounding shift with bad things.

Viral videos of the World Humanoid Robot Games show sprinting robots beating the human 100-meter record held by Usain Bolt—but also feature running robots crashing into barriers and falling down because they cannot readily stop running. A few fallen robots even break at the waist in a shower of sparks or catch on fire. That demonstration of both robotic prowess and limitations came from the second edition of the World Humanoid Robot Games hosted in Beijing from August 22–26. Like the inaugural event held in 2025, this year’s event challenged humanoid robots to run fast, perform kung fu or dance moves, fight one another in kickboxing, and play soccer or table tennis. This year’s games also tested robots in more humdrum practical scenarios like washing and hanging laundry, where they appeared to lag behind typical human working speeds. That suggests the event organizers, including Chinese state media and the Beijing city government, are keen to showcase the commercial potential of humanoid robots at a time when China’s robotics industry is rapidly developing and testing such robots. The fact that humanoid robots went from lagging behind human record-holders last year to surpassing human world records in the 100-meter sprint and standing high jump events this year suggests significant progress in robotic whole-body control for accomplishing specific tasks, Dipam Patel, a PhD candidate in computer science at Purdue University and a research assistant at the US Army DevCom Army Research Lab, told Ars. “All of that does display whole body control, because you need to move all the joints, hands, legs, arm, body, everything to accomplish that task,” said Patel, who is also a graduate student member of IEEE. “So there has definitely been a huge push in engineering from where they were last time to this time.” However, the robots on display in the World Humanoid Robot Games are still very far from general-purpose autonomous robots capable of handling practically any task. Measures of robotic progress at the games could prove even more meaningful if, for example, they challenged robots to run the same race 10 times, Patel suggested. The less flashy events that debuted during the World Humanoid Robot Games this year may ultimately prove more useful benchmarks for robotic progress. The Beijing Continental Grand Hotel provided robots with a different set of timed challenges, including moving wheeled luggage to assigned hotel rooms, cleaning up beds and restocking supplies. But not all robotics researchers are convinced that humanoid robots are necessary, especially when the most useful robots currently doing productive work usually come in other shapes and sizes. For his own part, Patel is focused on developing software algorithms that allow many different types of robots to seamlessly switch between tasks.

Ars Technica - All content | 2026-08-25

Visit Original Website
Fashionistas of a certain age no doubt remember the late designer Stephen Sprouse, who gave Blondie’s Debbie Harry her distinctive Bowery punk style. Sprouse’s first collections in the mid-1980s made a major splash, melding graffiti prints, a playful ’60s aesthetic, and bright Day-Glo colors that won admirers such as Andy Warhol. Several examples of Sprouse’s work are preserved in the textiles collection of the Indianapolis Museum of Art at Newfields, where conservationists are trying to learn more about the phosphorescent and fluorescent pigments the designer used in his creations in order to keep the colors from dulling over time. They described recent advances in that research at a meeting of the American Chemical Society (ACS) held this week in Chicago. Scientists began describing minerals that glowed in the dark as phosphors in the Middle Ages. Typically, such materials take on a charge when exposed to light, storing the absorbed energy and releasing it gradually as re-emitted light. Henri Becquerel discovered radioactive decay in 1896 because of his work with phosphorescent uranium salts: He left the salts in a closed drawer with photographic plates, which fogged up even without a light source. DayGlo paints and similar materials exploit the related phenomenon of fluorescence. DayGlo pigments fluoresce in daylight by essentially converting UV light into visible light to produce brighter, more vibrant colors. Sprouse is not the only one to find inspiration in DayGlo and similar phosphorescent and fluorescent materials. All glow-in-the-dark toys, stickers, paint, coatings in fluorescent lamps, cathode-ray tube TVs, and some clock dials rely on phosphorescence. (By contrast, glow sticks rely on a chemiluminescent process.) They’re also commonly used for traffic signage and on safety gear. DayGlo pigments were used extensively during World War II to make fluorescent fabric panels for US ground troops, while DayGlo’s black light paints were used on Navy aircraft carriers to enable planes to land at night. Artists have used both phosphorescent and fluorescence materials extensively from the mid-20th century on, most notably for psychedelic art and Sprouse’s designs. “For artists, the effect isn’t simply cosmetic, because they intentionally use these colors for their visual impact,” said Sarah Schmidtke Sobeck, a photochemist at The College of Wooster who spoke at the ACS meeting. Sobeck and Gregory Smith, a conservation scientist at the Indianapolis Museum of Art, have been collaborating on researching such materials for the last decade after meeting at a cultural heritage science conference. Their partnership has already produced a two-part study on the composition, spectral properties, and light stability of daylight fluorescence artists’ pigments. That earlier work showed that the optical brightener compounds that give fluorescent pigments their brightness degrade more quickly than the pigment dyes. Thus, even when there is no degradation in the pigments, the colors can appear to darken. That poses a unique challenge for conservationists responsible for long-term restoration of these works. Not only must they match colors under both visible and UV light, but the pigments will also change subtly over time. Gaining a better understanding of the unique photochemistry of such materials is critical for this purpose. During her talk, Sobeck reported on the group’s most recent work, extending their research to glow-in-the-dark phosphorescence pigments, with particular focus on the museum’s Sprouse collection. Among the early findings: Such materials have a markedly different composition from fluorescent colorants, which typically use organic dyes. The phosphorescent materials contain mineral-based pigments and inorganic compounds. Also, humidity turns out to be a significant factor in the breakdown of phosphorescent pigments—as much if not more so than prolonged exposure to light, which will help the museum improve its storage and exhibition practices for such artworks. The next step in this ongoing project will focus on the white pigment powder lithopone, testing how long phosphorescent materials containing that pigment will glow after each charge.

Some people are cursed with motion sickness, which can make it impossible to read or use a phone in a moving vehicle without suffering waves of nausea. There’s a surprisingly simple trick that can make smartphones usable in such situations. Apple deployed it on the iPhone a couple of years ago, and now Google’s own version is finally coming (slowly) to Android. Motion sickness is caused by a disagreement between what your inner ear senses and what your eyes see. If you’re looking at a book or a screen while in a car, the sensory mismatch can lead to a pretty unpleasant time. As part of iOS 18 in 2024, Apple released a feature called Motion Cues that places a network of moving dots on the screen to alleviate motion sickness, and it seems to work surprisingly well. Google’s version is similar, but it’s called Motion Assist. Google began testing Motion Assist back in 2024, but it never expanded beyond an internal test until now. Android Authority reports Motion Assist is appearing on Pixel phones running Android 17, but there’s no app or update you can install to get the feature—it’s being delivered by Play Services as part of a staged rollout. When activated, Motion Assist adds small dots around the edges of your phone screen over top of the content. It plugs into your phone’s accelerometer and gyroscope, allowing the dots to move in concert with the motion of the vehicle; they travel down the screen while the vehicle is moving forward, to the left when turning right, and so on. They basically mimic the way objects in the real world would move through your vision if you weren’t glued to the screen. Here’s how Apple’s version works. People who have used Apple’s anti-nausea dots have reported that the feature works wonders in reducing motion sickness. So there’s every reason to expect Google’s version will be similarly effective. Motion Assist can be activated manually as needed from the menu or using a quick settings tile. You can also let your phone turn it on automatically when it detects you’re in a moving vehicle. Google’s version of this feature also includes a few customization options, letting you change the shape, size, color, and opacity of the anti-nausea dots. If Motion Assist is available on your phone, you’ll find it in the Google services settings, usually nested one level below the main settings: Settings > Your profile > All services. Motion Assist will be under the Personal & Device section near the bottom. So far, Motion Assist has only appeared on Pixel phones running Android 17. The feature was obviously tested on earlier versions of Android, but it’s unclear if the final version will be exclusive to either Google’s phones or the latest version of Android. For decades, right-wing politics have carried particular weight in Germany, where atonement for the Holocaust includes mandatory education about the rise of National Socialism. It leads both of the traditional broad-based parties, the center-right Christian Democrats and the center-left Social Democrats. Its base of support is in the formerly Communist east—generally poorer, older, and angrier than the rest of Germany. And that’s where it’s poised for a breakthrough later this summer. In past state elections, other parties have contorted themselves to keep the far-right faction out of governing coalitions, maintaining a so-called firewall even when the AfD has won the most votes. That may not be possible in Saxony-Anhalt, where ahead of a September 6 vote, the AfD is polling at more than 40 percent. If smaller parties fail to reach the required 5 percent threshold to enter Parliament, this kind of showing by the AfD could be enough for it to win an outright majority, meaning that it wouldn’t need cooperation from other parties to govern. In fact, the Christian Democrats have a policy, formalized in 2018 and known as an incompatibility resolution, against working with either party. Given everything that’s happened with our public health leadership since Donald Trump returned to power, most public health professionals Slate spoke with took a dim view of our possible responses to a future crisis. After a summer of foodborne illness crises, it’s clear our public health officials are struggling to handle basic public health. Indeed, they seem to be taking us backward: Just last week, it was reported that school vaccination rates have hit a new record low. Still, how worried should we be about another pandemic? Historically, pandemics have happened multiple times a century, and many scholars believe that they’ll become more frequent due to climate change and deforestation. In the six years since COVID arrived in the U.S., the public health community has drawn no significant new conclusions about how to beat back conspiracy theories during a pandemic. The situation appears bad enough that when Slate asked experts how to conspiracy-proof a pandemic, responses often offered such bleakly unhelpful tidbits as “We need to study the effectiveness of our communication” and “This is going to take careful thought about strategic communications.” As Tara Sell, a senior scholar at the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security, told Slate, “We’re in a bad spot right now.” But several experts, prodded for any note of optimism they could find, offered hope from artificial intelligence. Admittedly, looking to A.I. for rescue is usually an eye-roll-worthy impulse. And coming from the field of disinformation in particular, A.I. is a Pandora’s box of yet-to-be-imagined evils. Generative A.I. certainly has the potential to worsen pandemic conspiracy theories by equipping them with highly convincing fabricated evidence. But in this case, the desperation of the situation might just call for public health advocates leaning into the dangerous new technology. According to Tim Mackey, a professor of global health at the University of California, San Diego, A.I. responses appear to be getting better at summarizing research, weighing credibility, and discerning misinformation. This applies only to the A.I.-generated search results summary and not to chatbots, which are inclined to give users the answers they want. But for a single search, the A.I. performs “decently,” Mackey said. “And that’s better than social media.” It’s also better than straight Google searches, in which users “doing their own research” tend to click the links that will support their views and interpret the information they find how they want to, potentially reinforcing conspiracy theories. The hope is that A.I. will help by encouraging laziness—the less time we spend pursuing answers, the better. Of course, Mackey said, there’s no guarantee that the A.I. won’t go off the rails. If A.I. companies got into certain commercial models, they could start promoting sponsored results, such as pseudoscientific cures. Or they could bend to pressure from conservative activists or tech oligarchs who see the weeding out of conspiracy theories as the suppression of political speech. So the possibility that A.I. might help is admittedly a flimsy source of comfort. But it’s something. There’s a second theory that might point to A.I. as our savior, though. Multiple experts Slate spoke with agreed that deplatforming the conspiracy theorists didn’t help; purging bad actors from platforms only made things worse, they said, as the people feeling censored hardened in their views and audiences followed those accounts to even more insular and unregulated platforms. Some experts mentioned other technological solutions to help with public health: namely, the tweaking of algorithms to make it so the most inflammatory accounts aren’t always rewarded. But one scholar had a more offense-oriented idea. Neil Johnson, the head of the Dynamic Online Networks Lab at George Washington University, sees the problem of conspiracy theories in health emergencies as the natural result of the huge scale of online communities. Before COVID even had a name, he said, online parenting communities on Facebook were reaching out to other communities to get information about the new flu-like virus in China. They found ready information in anti-vax groups, which claimed to be able to answer questions that the public health experts could not; conspiracy theories will always move faster and more decisively than legitimate scientific inquiry. And because of the points of connection between different Facebook groups with shared interests, the anti-vax ideas spread like wildfire, reaching millions of users and, by extension, their loved ones. The scale of the problem is so vast, Johnson said, that it requires an equally ambitious strategy. And his idea is this: Have tech companies use A.I. to spur interactions among conspiracy theorists, pro-science activists, and the middle-ground groups, such as the parenting communities. Studies show that when you put all three together, he noted, the extremes tend to soften. “And over time, it will soften the entire universe,” he said. It’s a grim idea, that our hope is in a tool that discourages in-depth reading and a long-shot hypothetical strategy—both of which rely on technology known for sowing confusion. But consider what the public health communicators are up against. The public is primed to believe conspiracy theories because they don’t trust the medical system, in part because we operate in a fragmented and highly confusing for-profit system. Many people do not have primary care physicians, and often those who do cannot easily call up their doctors to ask questions. We don’t know who to go to for trusted information. These are systemic problems without easy solutions. Pandemics worsen the suspicion. They are fast-paced, overwhelming, and chaotic, and public health officials inevitably make mistakes. Responses involve multiple agencies and levels of government, and because of our polarized environment, any information coming from a presidential administration will be met with automatic suspicion by the president’s opposing party. And, at a more fundamental level, pandemics breed conspiracy theories because someone inevitably loses. Once the disease gets out of hand, someone has to take a lower priority: elderly people vulnerable to illness versus children missing out on school, for example, or business owners facing closures versus hospitals worried about limited beds. Those who study conspiracy theories hope that the next federal pandemic response will do a better job of communicating uncertainty. They also hope it will not be embodied by a single man, a central messenger who, like Fauci, can be easily discredited in certain communities. They hope the arguments within the community will be less pedantic. (Who cares if we say aerosolized or airborne?) That people will acknowledge the pain and sacrifices that come with public health measures. That community leaders and representatives of the business and faith communities will feel included in the decisions. (Unsaid in this is that public health officials have to be comfortable relinquishing control and allowing more people to get sick or even die in the short term in exchange for long-term compliance.) That we will not try to drive a one-size-fits-all response across the states. But none of those improvements will stop conspiracy theories. Nor will media literacy programs, stronger education about viruses, centralized hubs for medical information, “prebunking” campaigns, or community engagement programs—all good ideas from experts that still cannot meet the sheer force of our paranoia. The conspiracy theories, they all agree, will still happen. The distrust is too bad, the hucksters too loud. If it were possible to conspiracy-proof a pandemic, we would get there only with investment and strong leadership. Instead, the federal government has eliminated positions that dealt with “infodemics” and slashed funding for research into disinformation. Academics are afraid to probe questions the administration doesn’t like, worried about grant money. Our top health official is a COVID conspiracy theorist who has staffed the agencies with other conspiracy theorists. The entire federal government is scaling back on providing services to the public. On Monday, the Supreme Court allowed the Trump administration to begin implementing Donald Trump’s mail-ballot executive order, which seeks to nationalize an election procedure that the Constitution has assigned to the states by ordering the United States Postal Service to refuse to mail certain ballots. Even though nearly all midterm primaries have already happened and the general election is only months away, the court bought the government’s argument that it’s simply too early to consider a lawsuit about the order’s legality. As Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson pointed out in her dissent, this decision is another piece in this court’s deteriorating democratic docket, letting the president sow chaos over voting rights across an electorate increasingly dissatisfied with his governance. In March, Trump issued an executive order, “Ensuring Citizenship Verification and Integrity in Federal Elections,” that sought to give the White House and the executive branch control over mail ballots. The order creates a three-part behemoth of new voting measures. First, it directs the Department of Homeland Security to create citizenship lists of people in every state who the federal government believes are eligible to vote. Second, the order directs the attorney general to “prioritize” prosecutions against local election officials who violate election law by allowing people to vote who shouldn’t. Finally, the order launches a process for the USPS to issue a rule about new special barcodes on mail ballots; through that rule, the Postal Service is directed to refuse to mail ballots that don’t contain this brand-new barcode. Given that the Constitution gives the president no authority over election processing, no lower court could stomach the EO’s illegality. After a group of 23 states sued, multiple federal courts issued injunctions blocking its implementation. But then Trump’s government went to the Supreme Court, as it often does. At no point did the government attempt to argue that the EO is legal. Instead, it argued solely that the states lacked standing to bring the lawsuit to begin with. The Republican appointees sided with the government, reasoning that the order technically required other federal agencies to take action, rather than the states. Therefore, the states did not meet the injury required to bring their case at this time. But throughout the opinion, the court made no claims as to whether the court would rule in favor of the administration once the legal issues are squarely before the court. And to make matters more confusing, a separate court still has a separate injunction blocking parts of the EO in a case brought by the League of Women Voters. There’s a more twisted point that’s worth zeroing in on, and that’s the relationship between Trump’s mail ballot–suppression efforts and the Supreme Court’s horrid election law jurisprudence of the past few years. Jackson flags this exact relationship in her dissent, as the court’s new ruling Monday “lets another shoe drop in the Kafkaesque nightmare that our precedents have been steadily creating for certain plaintiffs.” The nightmare works primarily by manipulating timing. As state and federal governments began taking increasingly controversial election actions over the past decade, such as gerrymandering or throwing out lawful ballots, voters and civil rights groups have filed more suits. But the Supreme Court has developed two new limitations on how a lower court is able to rule on these election issues. Understanding both these limitations together is key to seeing just how much of a democratic crisis this court is enabling. First, in a case from a couple of terms ago called Trump v. CASA, the court held that lower district courts do not have the authority in most instances to issue what are called nationwide injunctions. A nationwide or universal injunction is just an order requiring the government to stop implementing an unlawful rule against the entire country. Instead, after CASA, the court can usually prevent only a losing government from enforcing its unlawful rule against the specific parties that filed the lawsuit. But after hamstringing what types of remedies a court can order, SCOTUS also restricted when any orders can come down with its second restriction. This is the controversial Purcell principle, which states that courts are not permitted to change voting rules close to Election Day. This restriction holds true even if the government’s own delay is the reason the lawsuit arrived so late. Purcell creates an absurd situation in which a plaintiff can challenge an election rule in court, the court can agree the rule is illegal, but then no one is allowed to remedy the illegality because it’s too close to an election. Now pile onto Purcell Monday’s mailing order. In its unsigned opinion, in which the three liberal justices dissented, the majority alleges that it is simply too early to challenge the forthcoming USPS rule because, well, who could possibly know what that rule might say? It characterizes the lower court as engaging in “a string of speculation” about what the federal agencies might do when, and how that speculation illustrates that the state’s claims aren’t ripe for review. The majority goes so far as to assert that the lower court “had to speculate” about whether the USPS would even issue a final rule whatsoever. This charge is especially absurd given that the USPS had already issued the final rule days before the Supreme Court’s order, publishing the 95-page final rule last Friday. To recap: Purcell punishes a plaintiff in voting cases for being too late to court. But Monday’s order says that a plaintiff, like the states that sued, can also come to court too early. See the issue? The court’s precedents are creating an invisible tightrope very few plaintiffs can cross successfully. Europe gave itself the right to bring class actions against big companies five years ago. Ireland hosts the European headquarters of most large technology firms, and campaigners there have managed exactly one. Ellen O’Regan set out the problem for Politico from Dublin. Irish law bars anyone from funding a case unless they take part in it directly or hold a legitimate interest. Two offences do the work: maintenance and champerty. Maintenance covers funding someone else’s lawsuit without a stake in it. Champerty describes the version where the funder takes a cut of the winnings. Ireland inherited both from English law and wrote them into a statute in 1634. England abolished the offences in 1967. Irish courts have kept them. Ireland is the only country in the European Union with the restriction. Its courts also decide plenty else that matters here, having recently told the data regulator to reconsider a TikTok ban on China data transfers. The EU’s Representative Actions Directive, agreed in 2020, lets consumers across the bloc bring collective cases. Dieselgate prompted it. Volkswagen settled with American consumers for more than $9.5bn, and Europeans had no comparable route. The directive restricts who may file. Only qualified non-profit entities can bring a representative action. Non-profits do not have the money to sue Meta or Google on their own. They rely on outside funding, which is the thing Ireland prohibits. Johnny Ryan, who runs the enforcement unit at the Irish Council for Civil Liberties, calls that the “fatal contradiction”. Five non-profits have registered in Ireland to bring these actions. Three have a record of taking on large technology companies. Google, Meta, Spotify and Sony are contesting a Belgian law on paying creators at the EU court. Collective redress is the mechanism that moves compensation to consumers. In the one jurisdiction where the defendants sit, nobody can fund it. Prop trading is built off a simple idea. Prove you can trade, and get access to more capital than you have. A trader completes an evaluation and follows certain performance and risk rules.
